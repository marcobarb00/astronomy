Around AD 150 Ptolemy published the Almagest, a complete set of models and tables for the Sun, the Moon and the planets. With it anyone could compute where Mars would be on any date, past or future. We can still do that today, with his own numbers, and set his Mars beside the one in tonight's sky.

Mars tonight, by Ptolemy's model

Ptolemy's Mars moves on two circles. The planet goes round a small circle, the epicycle, once in each synodic period, the time between two of its conjunctions with the Sun, which is why it seems to stop and turn back from time to time. The center of the epicycle goes round the Earth on a larger circle, the deferent, whose center is off the Earth, at a speed that is uniform as seen from a third point, the equant. Page 4.2 builds this model step by step.

Running it takes his numbers: where Mars and the Sun stood at one moment, how fast each moves, and the sizes and directions of the circles. Ptolemy gives them all in the Almagest (III 2, III 7, IX 3, IX 5, X 7–10). His moment, the epoch, is noon at Alexandria on 26 February 747 BC, the first day of the era of Nabonassar, from which, he says, "the ancient observations are, on the whole, preserved down to our own time" (III 7). From the epoch the model counts the days to any date, tonight included.

This page was written on the evening of 20 November 2026. At 21:00 UT that evening the real Mars was near Regulus in Leo, at ecliptic longitude 148°, and Ptolemy's model put it at 138°, about 10° to the west, as wide as twenty full Moons side by side. The error from AD 100 to 2100

A single date cannot tell a steady drift from a swing that comes and goes, so we take the difference between Ptolemy's ecliptic longitude of Mars and the real one, negative when his Mars is behind, on every date from AD 100 to 2100, and plot it in Figure 2. Every model of the course is judged by a curve of this kind, its error curve.

Ptolemy fitted the eccentricity and the apogee of his model to three oppositions of Mars he observed in AD 130–139 (X 7), when Mars stood opposite the Sun. In that decade his Mars is never more than 2° from the real one, about 1° behind on average. Since then it has fallen steadily further behind, and in the twenty years around 2026 it is at worst 12° behind.

Where the error comes from

Most of the error comes from the length of Ptolemy's year. He took the year, from one equinox to the same equinox, as 365 days 5 h 55 min 12 s (III 1), 6.4 minutes longer than the modern value. The mean Sun of his model, the Sun moving at its average speed, was already 1° behind the modern mean Sun in his own time, and it falls a little further behind every century, to 9° by 2026. This lag is the dashed line of Figure 2.

In Ptolemy's model the mean longitude of Mars and its anomaly, the angle on the epicycle, always add up to the mean longitude of the Sun, and he computed the planet's motion in longitude as the Sun's less the motion in anomaly (IX 3). His motion in anomaly is still right to half a degree after nineteen centuries, so almost all of the error in his year passes to Mars. On 20 November 2026, 9.2° of the 10.2° was this lag.

Around the dashed line the error swings, by less than a degree in Ptolemy's time and by several degrees today. The swing depends on the mean anomaly, the angle of Mars from the apogee of the deferent, the point farthest from the Earth, since that angle sets where Mars moves fastest and where it moves slowest.

Ptolemy's apogee moves 1° a century, at his rate of precession, and keeps its place among the stars (IX 5). The real direction of Mars' aphelion moves 1.8° a century, because the modern precession is faster and the apsidal line of Mars also turns among the stars. So his apogee has fallen 22° behind the real one since his time, while his mean Mars fell only 9° behind, and his mean anomaly, 5° off in his own time, is 13° off today.

Near an opposition, when Mars is on the near side of its epicycle and closest to the Earth, an error in the direction of the epicycle's center is seen about three times larger in the direction of Mars. The largest swings of Figure 2, in 2018 and 2033, fall at such oppositions.

Page 4.2 refits the same circles to modern observations, with the mean motion of Mars measured from oppositions 79 years apart, and over the two centuries 1926–2126 they stay within about a degree of the real Mars. Over two centuries the circles themselves are good to about a degree, and the ten degrees of tonight come from rates that were slightly wrong and were carried over nineteen centuries.

The models of the course

We rebuild the models of Mars in the order in which they were made, and fit each one to modern observations, so that its error measures its geometry. Figure 3 gives the largest error of each over 2010–2025, with Ptolemy's model as he left it in the first row.

Copernicus' arrangement has no row of its own. Moving Ptolemy's circles into it, with the Sun at rest, gives Mars the same directions, so its error is that of page 4.2. His epicyclet, which replaces the equant, is the one step in the figure that made the error larger.

Every position on this page was measured against the sky as it turns each day about the poles, carrying the stars, the Sun and Mars with it. Ptolemy's first argument in the Almagest is that the heavens move like a sphere (I 3), and the next page starts there too.
