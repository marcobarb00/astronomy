An interactive course in classical positional astronomy

Predicting the Sky

For almost two thousand years, astronomers tried to say where the Sun, the Moon and the planets would be on a given night. Hipparchus and Ptolemy did it with circles. Kepler replaced them with ellipses, which are still the first approximation used today for the orbits of the planets and for the Sun's path relative to the Earth.

This course follows that story, from Hipparchus and Ptolemy to Copernicus, Tycho and Kepler. We rebuild each model as its author conceived it and test it against a modern reference sky, with a running error curve that shows what each step removes. Some steps refine an astronomer's own model, as Ptolemy did for the Moon; others change its form, as Kepler did.

Circles come close: the Sun's circle fitted to 2026 stays within two arcminutes of the real Sun all year, and Ptolemy's Moon fitted to the same year within two degrees. Much of what we meet on the way, from the coordinates of the sky and sidereal time to the equation of time, precession and the limits of eclipses, is used as it is today.

The planets show the limits of circles most clearly. Kepler found his ellipse in the motion of Mars, and the same law then holds for the Earth's orbit, and so for the Sun. The Moon needs more than an ellipse, because the Sun's pull keeps changing its orbit; the epilogue follows the story from there.

By the end you will be able to compute where the Sun, the Moon and a planet are on any date, using models you have derived yourself, and you will know how wrong each one is and why.

Sources

  • Copernicus, De Revolutionibus (transl. E. Rosen).
  • L. Foucault, "Démonstration physique du mouvement de rotation de la Terre au moyen du pendule", Comptes rendus de l'Académie des sciences 32 (1851).
  • R. Fitzpatrick, A Modern Almagest.
  • J. Meeus, Astronomical Algorithms (2nd ed., 1998).
  • E. M. Standish, Keplerian Elements for Approximate Positions of the Major Planets (JPL): the reference Sun and planets.
  • J. Vondrák, N. Capitaine, P. Wallace, "New precession expressions, valid for long time intervals", A&A 534, A22 (2011): the precession of the reference sky.
  • F. Espenak and J. Meeus, Five Millennium Canon of Solar Eclipses (NASA, 2006): the polynomials for ΔT.
  • IERS (International Earth Rotation and Reference Systems Service): today's ΔT, from the leap seconds.
  • SIMBAD (CDS, Strasbourg): the positions, proper motions and magnitudes of the stars; two magnitudes on the blue plates come from the Hipparcos catalogue (ESA).
