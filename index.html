An interactive course in classical positional astronomy

Predicting the Sky

For almost two thousand years, people tried to say where the Sun, the Moon and the planets would be on a given night. They worked with a few circles, a lot of patience and whatever observations they could collect, and each generation found something the previous one had gotten slightly wrong.

This course follows that story, from Hipparchus and Ptolemy to Copernicus, Tycho and Kepler. We rebuild each of their models as they were first conceived, check it against a modern reference sky, and look at the error it leaves behind, because that error is exactly what the next astronomer set out to fix. A running error curve follows the model page after page, so you can see it improve.

By the end you will be able to compute where the Sun, the Moon and a planet are on any date, using models you have derived yourself, and you will know how wrong each one is and why.

Sources

  • Ptolemy, Almagest (transl. G. J. Toomer, 1984).
  • Copernicus, De Revolutionibus (transl. E. Rosen).
  • L. Foucault, "Démonstration physique du mouvement de rotation de la Terre au moyen du pendule", Comptes rendus de l'Académie des sciences 32 (1851).
  • R. Fitzpatrick, A Modern Almagest.
  • J. Meeus, Astronomical Algorithms (2nd ed., 1998).
  • E. M. Standish, Keplerian Elements for Approximate Positions of the Major Planets (JPL): the reference Sun and planets.
  • J. Vondrák, N. Capitaine, P. Wallace, "New precession expressions, valid for long time intervals", A&A 534, A22 (2011): the precession of the reference sky.
  • F. Espenak and J. Meeus, Five Millennium Canon of Solar Eclipses (NASA, 2006): the polynomials for ΔT.
  • IERS (International Earth Rotation and Reference Systems Service): today's ΔT, from the leap seconds.
  • SIMBAD (CDS, Strasbourg): the positions, proper motions and magnitudes of the stars; two magnitudes on the blue plates come from the Hipparcos catalogue (ESA).
