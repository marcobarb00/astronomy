The shared components at work

Do the sphere, the plots, the reference sky and the error curve fit together?

Everything on this page comes from shared/: the Sun's position from the reference ephemeris, clock and sidereal time from time.js, a draggable sphere, a 2D plot and the error-curve widget. One date drives the first two figures.

The sky on a date

The celestial sphere in the equatorial frame. The axis points to the north celestial pole P; the equator is the great circle 90° from it. The ecliptic crosses the equator at the vernal point γ, the origin of right ascension and of ecliptic longitude, and carries the Sun at its reference position for the chosen date. An observer on the Greenwich meridian at latitude $\varphi$ has the zenith Z straight overhead, and the horizon is the great circle 90° from Z: the Sun is up when it lies on Z's side of it. The dashed circle is the daily path of Vega ($\delta = +38.8^\circ$); a star never sets when $\delta > 90^\circ - \varphi$.

A 2D plot: the equation of time

The equation of time from the reference Sun (Meeus 28.1), $E = \bar\lambda - \alpha$ expressed in minutes, over the same year. Hover over the plot to read a value.

An error curve: the Sun on a uniform circle

The simplest solar model moves the Sun uniformly along the ecliptic, $\lambda_\text{model} = \bar\lambda_0 + \dfrac{360^\circ}{Y}\,(t - t_0)$, with $\bar\lambda_0 = 280.466^\circ$ at $t_0 = $ J2000. The curve shows $\Delta\lambda = \lambda_\text{model} - \lambda_\text{ref}$ against the reference Sun. The faint dashed ghost uses Hipparchus' year, $Y = 365\tfrac14 - \tfrac1{300}$ days.

Exercise

The Earth's orbit has eccentricity $e = 0.0167$. Before looking at the error curve over one year, predict the largest error of the uniform circle in arcminutes, and roughly when in the year it occurs. Then check your prediction on the curve (hover to read values).

To first order in $e$, Kepler's motion differs from uniform motion by the equation of centre $q \approx 2e\sin M$, where $M$ is the mean anomaly counted from perihelion (early January).

The largest $|q|$ is $2e$ radians: $2 \times 0.0167 \times 3437.7' = 114.8'$, reached when $M = \pm 90^\circ$, about three months after and before perihelion.

The curve agrees. Computed with ephemeris.js for 2026 (script output, with $Y = 365.2422$ d): the most negative error is −114.7′ on 2026 Apr 3 and the largest +115.2′ on 2026 Oct 5. The model is behind the Sun in spring ($q > 0$) and ahead in autumn. The two extremes differ by about 0.5′ mostly because the reference is the apparent Sun, which aberration displaces by −20″ all year.
