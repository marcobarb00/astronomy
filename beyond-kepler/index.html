Page 4.5 left Mars within 6′ of the real planet and the Sun within a quarter of an arcminute. Kepler had found the shape of the planets' paths, and the question his successors asked was why the paths have that shape.

Newton's law of gravitation

In the Principia (1687) Newton showed that a body attracted by the Sun with a force inversely proportional to the square of its distance, and bound to it, moves on an ellipse with the Sun at a focus, sweeps equal areas in equal times, and has a period whose square is as the cube of its mean distance. Kepler's three laws became consequences of Newton's laws of motion and his law of gravitation (Voelkel ch. 7).

The same law said why the ellipses could not be exact. Every body attracts every other, so each planet is pulled by the other planets as well as by the Sun, and the Moon by the Sun as well as by the Earth. Kepler's laws hold exactly only for two bodies alone. From then on, each error left in the tables was a test of the law: either the pulls it adds explain the error, or the law fails.

The Moon's perigee

The Moon showed the pulls first, because the Sun changes its orbit by degrees within weeks, while the planets' pulls on one another take decades or centuries to grow as large. In Newton's theory its orbit is an ellipse that the Sun's pull keeps changing. Ptolemy had found the largest of the changes, the evection of page 3.2, and Tycho a second one, the variation, largest between the new or full Moon and the quarters (HAMA p. 85); page 4.5 listed them with the others.

The ellipse itself turns: its perigee advances 3.04° each time the Moon goes round in ecliptic longitude (from page 3.1's rates). In the Principia the Sun's pull, worked out for a nearly circular orbit, moves the perigee by 1.52° a revolution (1°31′28″), half as much, and Newton remarked that "the apses of the moon progress around twice as fast" (Book I, Proposition 45).

In the 1740s Euler, d'Alembert and Clairaut, working separately, all found the same half. Clairaut proposed in 1747 to change the law of gravitation by adding a term to it, and found in 1749 that the missing half came from the same law, carried to a higher approximation.

Jupiter and Saturn

Halley compared the ancient observations of Jupiter and Saturn, those of the Almagest, with the modern ones, and found that no single mean motion fitted both: Jupiter had sped up and Saturn had slowed down. His tables, published after his death, added a correction to each that grew with time.

Laplace explained it in 1785 by the two planets' pull on each other. Five turns of Jupiter take 59.3 years and two of Saturn 58.9, so the planets meet at nearly the same places for centuries, and the pulls add up from one meeting to the next before they reverse. The result is a periodic change of about 900 years, the great inequality, not a change that grows for ever.

Over 1926–2025 both ellipses stay within 9′ of the planets, but a century sees only a short piece of a 900-year wave and takes it for part of the mean motion. Run back to 1000 BC, Saturn's ellipse is 13° behind the planet on average. Tables that last for centuries need the pulls as periodic terms, as VSOP87, the reference of page 4.5, carries them.

Besides such long swings, the pulls turn each ellipse steadily: page 4.5 found Mars' aphelion turning 0.43° a century among the stars.

The Moon's acceleration and the Earth's clock

Halley also noticed, in 1695, that the ancient eclipses happened where the Moon would have been had it moved a little more slowly then than now: the Moon seemed to accelerate. In 1787 Laplace found a cause in the planets' pulls, which slowly change the eccentricity of the Earth's orbit and with it the Sun's pull on the Moon.

In 1853 Adams showed that Laplace's cause gives only about half of the acceleration. Delaunay and Ferrel proposed in the 1860s that most of the rest is in the clock: the tides slow the Earth's rotation, so the day by which the eclipses were timed grows longer. This is the ΔT of page 1.3, about 2½ hours in Ptolemy's time, which every page from 2.1 on applies to an ancient timing.

Uranus and Neptune

Herschel discovered Uranus in 1781. Its tables, with the pulls of Jupiter and Saturn included, kept failing: Bouvard's of 1821 drifted away from the planet within a few years. Le Verrier, and Adams independently, supposed a planet beyond Uranus and computed where it would have to be to cause the errors.

Galle and d'Arrest found Neptune at Berlin on the night of 23 September 1846, within 1° of Le Verrier's place. It was the first planet found from its pull on another.

Mercury's perihelion

Le Verrier also worked on Mercury. In 1859 he found that its perihelion advances faster than the planets' pulls allow, by 38″ a century, and Newcomb in 1882 put the excess at 43″. The table gives the budget as Clemence set it out in 1947.

A planet inside Mercury's orbit, which would have explained the excess, was searched for and never found. In 1915 Einstein's general theory of relativity, which changes Newton's law slightly close to the Sun, gave 43″ a century for Mercury (42.98″ with today's constants), which accounts for the excess.
