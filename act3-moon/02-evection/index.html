Page 3.1 ended with an epicycle that places the Moon within 40′ at every new and full Moon and is wrong by up to 3° in between. The large errors come near the quarters, when the Moon is 90° from the Sun, the positions that eclipses never sample.

Ptolemy found the law of this second inequality and built a model for it, in which the epicycle comes nearer the Earth at the quarters. It brings the largest error in longitude down from 3.2° to 1.8°. It also makes the Moon's distance change by nearly a factor of two, and the Moon's apparent size shows that it does not.

1 · ObservationThe Moon's equation at the quarters

To see the discrepancy, we take the real Moon's whole equation, its true longitude minus its mean longitude, at two kinds of moments: the new and full Moons, which eclipses sample, and the quarters. Figure 1 plots it for each of them from 2021 to 2030, about 250 of each, against the anomaly $M$ of the reference Moon's mean motions, counted from the apogee as on page 3.1.

A curve $-a\sin M$ fitted to each set gives $a = 4.96^\circ$ at new and full Moon and $a = 7.68^\circ$ at the quarters. The epicycle of page 3.1 was fitted to eclipses, so it has the smaller amplitude at every phase. At the quarters of 2026 its error is about $2.8^\circ \sin M$, which has the shape of the equation itself. The model has the right sign there, and too small a size.

Ptolemy describes the same pattern (Almagest V 2). At new and full Moon he finds the discrepancy "either imperceptible or small". At the quarters it is "very small or nothing when the moon is at apogee or perigee of the epicycle", and greatest when the equation of the first anomaly is greatest.

He measured the size of the effect at the quarters (V 3). On 9 February 139 he found the Moon 7⅔° short of its mean position, where the first anomaly allows 5°. Hipparchus' sighting of 5 August 128 BC, the one page 3.1 ended with, puts it 7⅔° beyond. From these and other sightings Ptolemy takes 7⅔° (7.67°) as the greatest equation at the quarters.

2 · ModelPtolemy's crank

The epicycle on a turning eccentric

An epicycle seen from nearer looks larger, and its equation grows. So Ptolemy concluded that "we must suppose the moon's epicycle to be carried on an eccentric circle, being farthest from the earth at conjunction and opposition, and nearest to the earth at both quadratures" (V 2). Since the quarters come twice a month, the eccentric must turn, so that its perigee meets the epicycle twice in each synodic month.

Let $D = \bar\lambda - \bar\lambda_\odot$ be the mean elongation, the Moon's mean longitude minus the mean Sun's. The centre K of the epicycle still moves at the mean longitude $\bar\lambda$ as seen from the Earth O. The eccentric's centre C turns westward about O, and its apogee A lies at the longitude $\bar\lambda - 2D$, so that A and K always lie symmetrically about the line of the mean Sun, at $\bar\lambda_\odot \pm D$. The construction is usually called Ptolemy's crank mechanism, since C turns about O as a crank turns about its axle.

We measure lengths in units of OA, the greatest distance of K, which Ptolemy makes 60. With $\text{OC} = c$, the eccentric's radius CA is $1 - c$, and the angle at O between OC and OK is $2D$, which Ptolemy calls the double elongation (V 5). It is 0° at new and full Moon ($D = 0^\circ$ or $180^\circ$) and 180° at the quarters ($D = 90^\circ$ or $270^\circ$), so the crank goes through its whole cycle twice in a synodic month. The law of cosines in the triangle OCK gives the distance $\rho = \text{OK}$:

$$(1 - c)^2 = \rho^2 + c^2 - 2\rho c \cos 2D \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \rho = c\cos 2D + \sqrt{(1 - c)^2 - c^2 \sin^2 2D}.$$

At the mean new and full Moon, $2D = 0^\circ$, K is at the apogee A and $\rho = 1$. At the mean quarters, $2D = 180^\circ$, K is at the eccentric's perigee and $\rho = 1 - 2c$.

The equation of anomaly

The epicycle keeps its radius $r = e$ in these units, and the Moon its anomaly $M$, counted westward from the apogee of the epicycle. Seen from O, the construction is page 3.1's epicycle with $\rho$ in place of the deferent's radius, so the equation of anomaly is

$$q = -\operatorname{atan2}(e\sin M,\ \rho + e\cos M), \qquad \lambda = \bar\lambda + q.$$

Its greatest value is $\arcsin(e/\rho)$, which grows from $\arcsin e$ at new and full Moon to $\arcsin\big(e/(1 - 2c)\big)$ at the quarters. At new and full Moon the model is page 3.1's epicycle, so the ratio $e$ found from eclipses keeps its meaning. With Ptolemy's numbers the two greatest equations are 5.02° and 7.66°.

The direction of the epicycle

Between the new and full Moons and the quarters, at the octants, Hipparchus' sightings with the Moon near the apogee or the perigee of its epicycle still disagreed with the crank. Ptolemy concluded that the anomaly is counted from a different diameter of the epicycle (V 5). Its mean apogee, the point from which $M$ is counted, lies on the line drawn through K from a point N, on the far side of O from C, with $\text{ON} = \text{OC}$. Toomer translates Ptolemy's word for this as the "direction" of the epicycle.

To find the mean apogee, take a frame with its origin at the Earth O, its first axis towards K, at the longitude $\bar\lambda$, and its second 90° further east. There K is at $(\rho, 0)$ and N, opposite C, at $(-c\cos 2D,\ c\sin 2D)$. The line from N through K leaves K at the angle

$$\sigma = -\operatorname{atan2}(c\sin 2D,\ \rho + c\cos 2D)$$

from the line OK, counted eastward. It vanishes when N, O and K are in line, at new and full Moon ($2D = 0^\circ$) and at the quarters ($2D = 180^\circ$), and reaches 13.1° in between with Ptolemy's numbers.

The anomaly itself does not change: $M$ still grows uniformly, $M = M_0 + n_M\,t$, as on page 3.1. What changes is the point from which it is counted. In the crank alone it is counted from the true apogee, the point of the epicycle farthest from O, on the line OK, and the equation of section 2 uses it as it is. In Ptolemy's complete model it is counted from the mean apogee, which lies $\sigma$ east of the true apogee, so the Moon, $M$ west of the mean apogee, is $M - \sigma$ west of the true apogee.

The equation of anomaly is found in the triangle OKL, which needs the Moon's angle from the line OK. The complete model therefore uses the equation of section 2 with $M - \sigma$ in place of $M$:

$$q = -\operatorname{atan2}\big(e\sin(M - \sigma),\ \rho + e\cos(M - \sigma)\big).$$

In Ptolemy's own example the Moon is 333.2° from the mean apogee. It is therefore 345.2° from the true apogee, and its equation is $+1^\circ 26'$ (1.43°), as Ptolemy computes, where the crank alone gives $+2^\circ 33'$ (2.55°).

The model to first order

To compare Ptolemy's model with the modern theory of the Moon, we expand $q$ to first order: we keep the terms of first order in $e$ and in $c$ and their product, and drop the squares. For a small number $x$, four approximations do all the work:

$$\arctan x \approx x, \qquad \frac{1}{1 + x} \approx 1 - x, \qquad \sqrt{1 - x^2}\, \approx 1, \qquad \sin(M + x) \approx \sin M + x\cos M.$$

The last follows from the addition formula, with $\cos x \approx 1$ and $\sin x \approx x$. Write $\kappa = c/(1 - c)$. The square root in $\rho$ is $(1 - c)\sqrt{1 - \kappa^2\sin^2 2D} \approx 1 - c$, so

$$\rho \approx (1 - c) + c\cos 2D = (1 - c)(1 + \kappa\cos 2D).$$

In the equation of anomaly the denominator $\rho + e\cos M$ is positive, so $q = -\arctan\big(e\sin M/(\rho + e\cos M)\big)$. The first two approximations give $q \approx -(e/\rho)\sin M$, as on page 3.1, and then $e/\rho \approx e_0(1 - \kappa\cos 2D)$, with $e_0 = e/(1 - c)$, the epicycle's ratio when K is at the eccentric's mean distance. The crank alone therefore gives

$$q \approx -e_0\,(1 - \kappa\cos 2D)\sin M = -e_0 \sin M + \tfrac12 e_0 \kappa\,\big[\sin(M - 2D) + \sin(M + 2D)\big].$$

The crank adds two terms of equal size, one in $M - 2D$ and one in $M + 2D$. The direction of the epicycle changes $M$ into $M - \sigma$, with $\sigma \approx -\kappa\sin 2D$, and to first order it removes the term in $M + 2D$ and doubles the other (Exercise 2):

$$q \approx -e_0 \sin M + e_0 \kappa \sin(M - 2D).$$

The series of the reference Moon starts with the same two terms, $-6.29^\circ \sin M + 1.27^\circ \sin(M - 2D)$. The first belongs to the ellipse and the second is the evection of page 3.1. With Ptolemy's numbers $e_0 = 0.1057$ radians, or 6.05°, and $e_0 \kappa = 1.26^\circ$. Modern theory traces the evection to the Sun's pull on the Moon's orbit.

The table gives the exact coefficients of Ptolemy's model, computed numerically. In the crank alone, the term in $M + 2D$ is as large as the term in $M - 2D$. The direction of the epicycle reduces it to 0.06°, close to the small term of the reference Moon, and doubles the other, which comes within 0.06° of the evection.

3 · FitThe eccentric from the quarters

The crank needs one number besides those of page 3.1, the size of the eccentric, and Ptolemy takes it from the 7⅔° at the quarters (V 4). There K is at the eccentric's perigee G, and the greatest equation is seen where the line from O touches the epicycle, so $\sin 7^\circ 40' = r/\text{OG}$. With OA = 60 and $r = 5.25$ this fixes OG, then the eccentric's radius, half of OA + OG, and finally OC, which Ptolemy finds to be 10.32 (Exercise 1).

The point N comes from two of Hipparchus' sightings at Rhodes in 127 BC, made near the octants with the Moon near the perigee and the apogee of its epicycle (V 5). From them Ptolemy computes ON = 10.30 and 10.33, equal to OC within the accuracy of the observations, and takes ON = OC.

The crank fitted to 2026 keeps everything page 3.1 fitted, including the epicycle's ratio $e = 0.0844$ from three eclipses, takes the mean Sun of page 1.3 for $D$, and keeps the direction of the epicycle as Ptolemy found it. Its one new number, $c$, is fitted by least squares to the 25 quarters of 2026, and comes out at 0.1762, against Ptolemy's 0.1719. The greatest equation is then 7.49° at the quarters.

Fitted to the quarters of all ten years from 2021 to 2030, $c$ comes out at 0.1778. The value depends slightly on the moments chosen, because the model leaves out smaller inequalities that also act at the quarters.

4 · TestThe crank against the real Moon

Longitude

The quarters of 2026 were used for the fit, so they test little. The test is whether the complete model also holds between them, near the octants, where nothing was fitted, and in other years. Figure 4 gives its error over 2026, with the epicycle of page 3.1 and the crank alone as ghosts.

The crank alone corrects the quarters but leaves the octants almost as wrong as the epicycle was, near 2.6°. The direction of the epicycle, which was not fitted, brings them down to 1.5°, and the largest error of the year to 1.8°.

The model adds no motion of its own to those of page 3.1 and the mean Sun, so its error does not grow with time: over the century from 1976 to 2076 it stays within 2.0°.

Ptolemy's complete Moon

Ptolemy's own model, with his parameters of Books IV and V, can be judged in the same way. Figure 5 runs it over two months around his sighting of 9 February 139, with his simple Moon of page 3.1 as a ghost.

The complete Moon swings about a third as widely as the simple one over these two months. Both stay on average 0.9° behind the real Moon, an error the second anomaly cannot remove.

As page 3.1 found, that offset comes from Ptolemy's Sun, here 42′ behind, and from the epoch he fixed with it. Over the centuries the complete Moon drifts as the simple one does, and is 10° behind by 2026.

Distance

The crank was built to fit longitudes, but it also fixes the Moon's distance OL. That is greatest, $1 + e$, at new and full Moon with the Moon at the apogee of its epicycle, and least, $1 - 2c - e$, at a quarter with the Moon at the perigee. With Ptolemy's numbers the ratio of the two is 1.91. The real Moon's distance varies over 2026 by a ratio of only 1.14.

The apparent diameter of the Moon is inversely proportional to its distance, so it shows the difference directly. Figure 6 compares the real Moon's diameter over 2026 with what the two models predict. A model gives only ratios of distances, so each is scaled to have the real Moon's smallest diameter, 29.4′, at its greatest distance.

With Hipparchus' dioptra Ptolemy found the Moon's diameter equal to the Sun's when the Moon is at its greatest distance at full Moon; the size itself, 31⅓′, he computed from two lunar eclipses (V 14). On his model the Moon at a quarter, at its least distance, would measure nearly a degree. He reports no measurement of it, and the Almagest does not discuss the difference.

Copernicus turned it against the model. After the uneven motions the model requires and the parallaxes, "the error is evinced most of all by the body of the moon, whose diameter would similarly look twice as large and half as large" (De Revolutionibus IV 2). Anyone who measures it, he adds, "will find that it varies only as much as required by the epicycle without that eccentric". At its least distance, the model puts the Moon 1.7 times nearer than the real Moon ever comes.

The eccentric of page 2.1 errs in the same way, on a smaller scale. It makes the Sun's distance vary twice as much as the real one, which changes the Sun's diameter by about two minutes of arc instead of one. Ptolemy found no change in the Sun's diameter (V 14), and a minute of arc was beyond his instruments.

5 · ResidualThe variation, and the Moon's distance

The largest single part of what remains is a term of the reference Moon that no ancient model contains, the variation, $0.66^\circ \sin 2D$, greatest at the octants. In 2026 it accounts for over two fifths of the error's mean square. Nearly as large is an error in the first anomaly left by page 3.1's fit to three eclipses, whose anomaly at the epoch is 5° off. The annual equation, 12′ here, comes next.

Ptolemy proves that at new and full Moon the crank's effect is negligible (V 10). In 2026 the crank fitted to that year is within 42′ there, where page 3.1's epicycle was within 40′, so the longitudes that decide eclipses are no better and no worse.

The model's distances call for a different construction. Copernicus replaced the crank with a second, small epicycle carried on the first, on which the Moon goes round twice a month, nearest the first epicycle's centre at new and full Moon and farthest at the quarters (IV 3). His deferent stays centred on the Earth, and at the quarters his Moon ranges from 52.28 to 68.33 Earth radii (IV 17), a ratio of 1.31, much nearer the real 1.14.

The complete model gives the Moon's longitude within 2° at every phase, and within about 40′ at new and full Moon. An eclipse needs one more coordinate, since the Moon must also be near the ecliptic. Page 3.3 adds the Moon's latitude and decides which new and full Moons are eclipses.

Exercises

From the epicycle of 5.25 where OA = 60 and the greatest equation of 7⅔° at the quarters, find Ptolemy's eccentric: OG, the radius and OC. Then, with his values OG = 39.37 and OC = 10.32, find the greatest and the least distance of the Moon and their ratio. Ptolemy makes OA = 59 Earth radii (V 13): convert the two distances. If the Moon measures 31⅓′ at its greatest distance (V 14), what should it measure at its least? Compare with the real Moon in Figure 6.

The greatest distance is OA plus the epicycle's radius; the least is OG minus it.

At the quarters $\sin 7^\circ 40' = 5.25/\text{OG}$, so OG = 39.352. The eccentric's diameter is OA + OG, so its radius is 49.676, and OC = 60 − 49.676 = 10.324. Ptolemy, with his chords, writes 39.367, 49.683 and 10.317.

The greatest distance is $60 + 5.25 = 65.25$ and the least $39.367 - 5.25 = 34.117$, a ratio of 1.913. In Earth radii, multiplying by 59/60, they are 64.16 and 33.55: the 64⅙ and 33.55 that Copernicus quotes against the model (De Revolutionibus IV 2).

The apparent diameter goes as the inverse of the distance, so 31.33′ at the greatest distance becomes $31.33' \times 1.913 = 59.9'$ at the least, almost a degree. The real Moon's distance varied by a ratio of 1.140 in 2026, and its diameter only from 29.4′ to 33.5′.

Show that to first order the direction of the epicycle turns the anomaly by $\sigma \approx -\kappa\sin 2D$, with $\kappa = c/(1 - c)$. Then use it to complete the expansion of section 2: show that the complete model gives $q \approx -e_0 \sin M + e_0 \kappa \sin(M - 2D)$, with no term in $M + 2D$. Check $\sigma$ against Ptolemy's example of Figure 3.

Use the four approximations of section 2. For $\sigma$, first divide both arguments of the arctangent by $1 - c$.

Since $\rho + c\cos 2D$ is positive, $\sigma = -\arctan\big(c\sin 2D / (\rho + c\cos 2D)\big)$. Dividing numerator and denominator by $1 - c$, with $\rho/(1 - c) \approx 1 + \kappa\cos 2D$, the fraction becomes $\kappa\sin 2D/(1 + 2\kappa\cos 2D)$. With the first two approximations,

$$\sigma \approx -\kappa\sin 2D\,(1 - 2\kappa\cos 2D) \approx -\kappa\sin 2D,$$

dropping the term in $\kappa^2$; $\sigma$ is in radians.

The complete model uses $M - \sigma \approx M + \kappa\sin 2D$ in place of $M$. The term $\kappa\sin 2D$ is a small number, so the fourth approximation applies, with $x = \kappa\sin 2D$:

$$q \approx -e_0\,(1 - \kappa\cos 2D)\sin(M + \kappa\sin 2D) \approx -e_0\,(1 - \kappa\cos 2D)\,(\sin M + \kappa\sin 2D\cos M).$$

Multiplying out, the product of the two terms in $\kappa$ is of second order and is dropped:

$$q \approx -e_0\sin M + e_0\kappa\,(\cos 2D\sin M - \sin 2D\cos M) = -e_0 \sin M + e_0\kappa\sin(M - 2D).$$

In the sum-of-sines form of section 2, the direction's term $-e_0\kappa \sin 2D\cos M$ is $-\tfrac12 e_0 \kappa[\sin(M + 2D) - \sin(M - 2D)]$, which cancels the crank's term in $M + 2D$ and doubles its term in $M - 2D$.

With $\kappa = 0.2076$ and $2D = 90.5^\circ$, $-\kappa\sin 2D = -11.90^\circ$, against the exact $-12.03^\circ$ of Figure 3 and Ptolemy's 12°1′ (12.02°).

On 1 October 135, near the first quarter, Ptolemy measured the Moon's zenith distance at Alexandria as 50°55′ (50.92°), where his model, with the Moon's latitude, put its position as seen from the Earth's centre at 49°48′ (49.80°) (V 13). He took the difference, 67′, as the Moon's parallax, $\sin p = \sin z'/d$ with $z'$ the measured zenith distance and $d$ the distance in Earth radii. Find $d$. His model put the Moon at 40.42 where OA = 60: what does OA become in Earth radii? The reference Moon was then 385·409 km from the Earth's centre, 60.4 Earth radii. What parallax should he have measured, and what does his measurement say about his model?

Solve $\sin p = \sin z'/d$ for $d$, then use it the other way round with the real distance.

From 67′, $d = \sin 50^\circ 55' / \sin 67' = 39.83$ Earth radii; Ptolemy, through a chain of roundings, writes 39.75, and Toomer notes that 39.83 is more accurate. Scaling the model by Ptolemy's value, OA = $39.75 \times 60/40.42 = 59.01$ Earth radii, which he rounds to 59.

At the real distance the parallax would have been $\arcsin(\sin 50^\circ 55' / 60.4) = 44'$, not 67′. His measurement put the Moon at two thirds of its real distance, as his model required near the quarter.

This one observation fixes the scale of the model, OA in Earth radii, so it cannot test the model's ratio of distances: that needs parallaxes at both new or full Moon and the quarters. Toomer finds that the errors in the observation, the latitude and the model cancel, and writes "This is no accident; Ptolemy knew (approximately) what the parallax had to be at eclipses, and chose an observation which produced that amount". Copernicus made the comparison and found the parallaxes at the quarters to "differ very little or not at all" from those at eclipses (IV 2).
