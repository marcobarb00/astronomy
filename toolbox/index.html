Each page of the course built one model, fitted it to modern observations and tested it against the reference sky. Gathered here, the models give the positions of the Sun, the Moon and the planets on any day from 1900 to 2100, and their tests tell you how far to trust each one.

Positions on a date

For each body the calculator takes the best model of the course: the Sun's ellipse of page 4.5, the crank of page 3.2 for the Moon (with the inclined circle of page 3.3 for its ecliptic latitude), and an ellipse for each planet, seen from the Earth on the Sun's ellipse. Each position is then turned into right ascension and declination, and into the times at which the body rises, crosses the meridian and sets, with the frames and the clock of Act I.

Since the ellipses lie in the ecliptic, the calculator gives every planet an ecliptic latitude of 0. The real Venus reaches 7.6° from the ecliptic in 2026, so its declination, and with it the times of rising and setting, can be off by as much; the course left the planets' latitudes for a page of their own.

The course fitted an ellipse to Mars only. Those of Mercury, Venus, Jupiter and Saturn are fitted here in the same way as Mars' on page 4.5. At an opposition to the real Sun, an outer planet's geocentric ecliptic longitude is its heliocentric one, and at an inferior conjunction an inner planet's heliocentric ecliptic longitude is the Earth's. So a century of these events, 1926–2025, gives each planet's mean longitude, mean motion, aphelion and eccentricity, and the third law gives its distance.

Over 2026 the planets' ellipses stay within a few arcminutes of the reference, Jupiter's within 3.2′. Mercury's and Venus' errors are the largest, about 12′, mostly because the ellipses leave out the tilt of their orbits to the ecliptic, 7.0° and 3.4°, as page 4.5 left out Mars'.

How far each model can be trusted

Each model's largest error tells you what it can be used for: the Sun's eccentric of page 2.1 places the Sun within 2′, which is enough for a sundial, while page 3.2's crank leaves the Moon nearly 2° off, more than three times its apparent diameter. Formulas

Each formula below is a key result of one page, in the order of the course; its number links to the page that derives it.

The stage

1.1 The altitude of a star of declination $\delta$ at the hour angle $H$, for the geographic latitude $\varphi$

$$\sin a = \sin\varphi \sin\delta + \cos\varphi \cos\delta \cos H.$$

1.2 Ecliptic coordinates $(\lambda, \beta)$ to equatorial coordinates $(\alpha, \delta)$

$$\begin{aligned} \cos\delta \cos\alpha &= \cos\beta \cos\lambda, \\ \cos\delta \sin\alpha &= \cos\varepsilon \cos\beta \sin\lambda - \sin\varepsilon \sin\beta, \\ \sin\delta &= \sin\varepsilon \cos\beta \sin\lambda + \cos\varepsilon \sin\beta. \end{aligned}$$

1.2 Equatorial coordinates to the azimuth $A$ and the altitude $a$

$$\begin{aligned} \sin a &= \sin\varphi \sin\delta + \cos\varphi \cos\delta \cos H, \\ \cos a \sin A &= -\cos\delta \sin H, \\ \cos a \cos A &= \cos\varphi \sin\delta - \sin\varphi \cos\delta \cos H. \end{aligned}$$

1.3 Sidereal time at Greenwich and at the geographic longitude $\ell$, and the hour angle

$$\theta_G = L_0 + (360^\circ + n)\,d, \qquad \theta = \theta_G + \ell, \qquad H = \theta - \alpha.$$

1.3 Rising and setting: the hour angle $H_0$ at the altitude $h_0$, and the length of the Sun's day

$$\cos H_0 = \frac{\sin h_0 - \sin\varphi \sin\delta}{\cos\varphi \cos\delta}, \qquad \text{day} = \frac{2H_0}{15^\circ}\ \text{h}.$$

The Sun

2.1 The equation of center of the eccentric, $M$ counted from the apogee

$$q = -\operatorname{atan2}(e \sin M,\ 1 + e \cos M), \qquad \lambda = \bar\lambda + q, \qquad M = \bar\lambda - \lambda_A.$$

2.2 The equation of time, apparent minus mean solar time, and its first order (radians)

$$E = -q + (\lambda - \alpha), \qquad E \approx e\sin M + e_\varepsilon \sin 2\bar\lambda, \qquad e_\varepsilon = \tan^2\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.$$

2.3 Precession: the change of declination, and the sidereal year

$$\frac{d\delta}{dt} = \frac{p\,\sin\varepsilon\,\cos\beta\,\cos\lambda}{\cos\delta}, \qquad \frac{Y_\text{s}}{Y} = \frac{360^\circ}{360^\circ - pY}.$$

The Moon

3.1 The epicycle: the equation of anomaly, $e = r/R$, and the mean motions from the synodic month

$$q = -\operatorname{atan2}(e\sin M,\ 1 + e\cos M), \qquad n = n_\odot + \frac{360^\circ}{P_s}, \qquad n_M = \frac{269 \times 360^\circ}{251\,P_s}.$$

3.2 The crank: the distance of the epicycle's center, the direction of its apogee, and the equation

$$\rho = c\cos 2D + \sqrt{(1 - c)^2 - c^2 \sin^2 2D}, \qquad \sigma = -\operatorname{atan2}(c\sin 2D,\ \rho + c\cos 2D), \qquad q = -\operatorname{atan2}\big(e\sin(M - \sigma),\ \rho + e\cos(M - \sigma)\big).$$

3.3 The inclined circle, and the eclipse year

$$\sin\beta = \sin i\,\sin F, \qquad \tan(\lambda - \Omega) = \cos i\,\tan F, \qquad \frac{1}{Y_\text{e}} = \frac{1}{P_d} - \frac{1}{P_s}.$$

3.3 The limits of eclipses: the umbra at the Moon's distance, and a solar eclipse seen somewhere

$$u = 1.01\,\pi + \pi_\odot - s_\odot, \qquad |\beta| < \pi - \pi_\odot + s + s_\odot.$$

The planets

4.1 The simple epicycle, and the synodic period of an outer planet

$$\lambda = \bar\lambda + \eta, \qquad \eta = \operatorname{atan2}(r\sin\mu,\ 1 + r\cos\mu), \qquad \frac{1}{P_\lambda} + \frac{1}{S} = \frac{1}{Y}.$$

4.1 The stations, with $k = n_\mu / n$

$$\cos\mu_s = -\frac{1 + r^2\,(1 + k)}{r\,(2 + k)}.$$

4.2 The equant, OC = $e$ and OQ = $2e$

$$\text{QK} = -e\cos M + \sqrt{1 - e^2\sin^2 M}, \qquad q = -\operatorname{atan2}(2e\sin M,\ \text{QK} + 2e\cos M).$$

4.3 The periods about the Sun, and the distances (au)

$$\frac{1}{P} = \frac{1}{Y} \mp \frac{1}{S}, \qquad a = \frac{1}{r} \ \text{(outer)}, \qquad a = r \ \text{(inner)}, \qquad \sin\varepsilon_\text{max} = a.$$

4.4 The illuminated fraction of a planet's disk

$$k = \frac{1 + \cos\chi}{2}.$$

4.5 Kepler's equation, its solution by Newton's method, the distance and the third law

$$M = E + e\sin E, \qquad E \leftarrow E - \frac{E + e\sin E - M}{1 + e\cos E}, \qquad r = a(1 + e\cos E), \qquad P^2 = a^3.$$
