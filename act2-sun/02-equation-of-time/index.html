The clock of page 1.3 puts noon at 12:00 every day, but the real Sun crossed the meridian up to 14 minutes later than that in February and 16½ minutes earlier in November. Page 2.1 found one of the causes, the Sun's uneven motion along the ecliptic, and named the other, the tilt of the ecliptic to the equator.

Putting the two together gives the equation of time, the correction that makes a sundial agree with a clock. With the eccentric of page 2.1 and the obliquity of page 1.2, and nothing else, it comes out right to within a few seconds.

1 · ObservationThe sundial and the clock

A sundial shows apparent solar time, the hour angle of the real Sun, and reads 12:00 when the Sun crosses the meridian. A clock shows mean solar time, the hour angle of the mean Sun of page 1.3. We call the difference, sundial minus clock, the equation of time $E$. It is positive when the sundial is ahead.

At Greenwich in 2026, where the clock keeps Universal Time, the sundial is behind by up to 14 min 12 s on 11 February and ahead by up to 16 min 27 s on 3 November. In between it reaches two smaller extremes, +3 min 38 s on 13 May and −6 min 34 s on 26 July, and the two agree on four days: 15 April, 12 June, 1 September and 25 December.

Single days hardly show it. The true solar day, from one real noon to the next, lasts at most 29.8 s more than 24 hours (the day ending 23 December) and at least 21.4 s less (the day ending 18 September). But the short days come in long runs, 100 of them in a row in 2026, and over a run their shortfalls add up, as Figure 1 shows.

From 11 February to 3 November the days fall short of 24 hours by 30.66 minutes in all, which is exactly what the sundial gains in that time, from −14.20 to +16.45 minutes. The equation of time is the running sum of the days' differences from 24 hours. Over the whole year these differences cancel, to 0.06 s on average, because the clock is defined as the average of the Sun.

Ptolemy makes the same observation: the difference between a true and a mean solar day does not cause "a perceptible difference" for a single day, "but the accumulated difference over a number of solar days is quite noticeable" (Almagest III 9).

A camera can record the equation of time as well. Photographed at the same clock time on days spread over a year, the Sun traces a figure eight called the analemma. Its height is the Sun's declination, and its spread from side to side is the equation of time.

2 · ModelTwo causes of the uneven solar day

The equation of time

Both kinds of time are hour angles, $H = \theta - \alpha$, so at any moment their difference is the difference of the two right ascensions. The mean Sun's right ascension is the mean longitude, $\alpha_m = \bar\lambda$ (page 1.3), so in degrees

$$E = H - H_m = \alpha_m - \alpha = \bar\lambda - \alpha,$$

and in time 1° is 4 minutes, since the sky turns 360° in 24 hours. We want to split this difference into the effect of the Sun's uneven speed, which page 2.1 modelled, and the effect of the tilt. Adding and subtracting the true longitude $\lambda$ does it:

$$E = (\bar\lambda - \lambda) + (\lambda - \alpha) = -q + (\lambda - \alpha).$$

Ptolemy names the same two causes: the extra stretch of the equator that crosses the meridian each day "cannot be a constant, for two reasons: firstly, because of the sun's apparent anomaly; and secondly, because equal sections of the ecliptic do not cross either the horizon or the meridian in equal times" (Almagest III 9).

The eccentricity part

The first part is page 2.1's equation of centre with its sign changed. While the Sun runs ahead of its mean place, $q > 0$, its right ascension grows ahead of the mean Sun's, it reaches the meridian later, and the sundial falls behind. To first order $-q \approx e\sin M$, so this part swings by $e = 0.03347$ radians, or 1.918°, either way, which is 7.67 minutes of time.

The obliquity part

The second part would remain even for a Sun moving uniformly along the ecliptic. Its right ascension follows from $\tan\alpha = \cos\varepsilon\tan\lambda$ (page 1.2), and page 1.2's table showed $\alpha$ lagging behind $\lambda$ in the first quadrant. To see how the lag depends on $\lambda$, we write the tangent of the difference:

$$\tan(\lambda - \alpha) = \frac{\tan\lambda - \tan\alpha}{1 + \tan\lambda\tan\alpha} = \frac{(1 - \cos\varepsilon)\sin\lambda\cos\lambda}{\cos^2\lambda + \cos\varepsilon\sin^2\lambda}.$$

With $\sin\lambda\cos\lambda = \tfrac12\sin 2\lambda$, $\cos^2\lambda = \tfrac12(1 + \cos 2\lambda)$ and $\sin^2\lambda = \tfrac12(1 - \cos 2\lambda)$, and dividing above and below by $\tfrac12(1 + \cos\varepsilon)$,

$$\tan(\lambda - \alpha) = \frac{e_\varepsilon \sin 2\lambda}{1 + e_\varepsilon \cos 2\lambda}, \qquad e_\varepsilon = \frac{1 - \cos\varepsilon}{1 + \cos\varepsilon} = \tan^2\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.$$

This is the formula of page 2.1 for the equation of centre, $\tan q = -e\sin M / (1 + e\cos M)$, with $e_\varepsilon$ in place of $e$ and $2\lambda$ in place of $M$. The reduction to the equator behaves like an eccentric with eccentricity $e_\varepsilon = 0.04302$, today's, that goes round twice a year, so every result of page 2.1 carries over.

To first order $\lambda - \alpha \approx e_\varepsilon \sin 2\lambda$, which swings by 2.465° either way, 9.86 minutes of time; the second-order term, $-\tfrac12 e_\varepsilon^2 \sin 4\lambda$, is worth 12.7 s. The greatest value of $|\lambda - \alpha|$ is $\arcsin e_\varepsilon = 2^\circ 27' 57''$ ($2.466^\circ$), reached where $\cos 2\lambda = -e_\varepsilon$, at $\lambda = 46.23^\circ$ and at the same distance from every other equinox and solstice.

Differentiating $\tan\alpha = \cos\varepsilon\tan\lambda$ shows where the lag comes from: $d\alpha/d\lambda = \cos\varepsilon / \cos^2\delta$, so the two angles grow at different speeds. At the equinoxes the ecliptic crosses the equator at a slant, and $\alpha$ grows only 0.918 times as fast as $\lambda$. At the solstices the ecliptic runs parallel to the equator, but at a declination of 23.4°, where the hour circles are closer together, and $\alpha$ grows 1.090 times as fast.

So $\alpha$ falls behind $\lambda$ after each equinox and catches up before each solstice. The lag is greatest where the two speeds are equal, $d\alpha/d\lambda = 1$, which gives $\sin^2\lambda = 1/(1 + \cos\varepsilon)$, the same $\lambda = 46.23^\circ$ (Exercise 3). Figure 2 starts with $\varepsilon = 45^\circ$, nearly twice the real tilt, so that the arc can be seen on the sphere.

Copernicus describes the same thing in the language of his tables. The 88° of the ecliptic from the 16th degree of Taurus to the 14th degree of Leo "cross the meridian in about 93 time-degrees", 5 more than the mean, and the 92° from Leo to Scorpio in 87, 5 fewer (De Revolutionibus III 26 [f. 96v]). His limits, 46° and 134°, are the points where the two speeds are equal.

The sum

Adding the two parts, to first order in $e$ and $e_\varepsilon$ and in radians,

$$E \approx e\sin M + e_\varepsilon \sin 2\bar\lambda,$$

a wave with a period of a year and an amplitude of 7.67 minutes, plus a wave with a period of half a year and an amplitude of 9.86 minutes. In 2026 this approximation is within 45 s of the exact sum. Figure 3 shows the two parts and how they add.

The half-yearly wave is the larger, so $E$ has two maxima and two minima. The yearly wave makes them unequal: it deepens the February minimum and the November maximum, where it has the same sign as the half-yearly wave, and it reduces the other two. The four zeros of $E$ fall where the two waves cancel.

The analemma

At mean noon the mean Sun is on the meridian, $H_m = 0$, so the real Sun's hour angle is $H = E$, which in degrees is $E$ divided by 4 minutes. Plotted against the Sun's declination through a year, this hour angle traces the analemma of the photograph, as it would look at noon, upright.

The analemma crosses itself at a declination of 8.95°, where the Sun stands on 12 April and again on 29 August with the same equation of time. In 2026 the real Sun's hour angle at mean noon runs from 3.55° east of the meridian to 4.11° west of it.

3 · FitEccentricity, apogee and obliquity from the equation of time

The model has no parameter of its own: $e$ and $\lambda_A$ come from the seasons (page 2.1), $\varepsilon$ from the noon Sun at the solstices (page 1.2). We can turn the argument round. A clock and a sundial, compared through one year, measure $E$, and $E$ carries the same three parameters, so it should give them back.

To first order, with $M = \bar\lambda - \lambda_A$,

$$E \approx (e\cos\lambda_A)\sin\bar\lambda - (e\sin\lambda_A)\cos\bar\lambda + e_\varepsilon\sin 2\bar\lambda.$$

Over a whole year the functions $\sin\bar\lambda$, $\cos\bar\lambda$, $\sin 2\bar\lambda$ and $\cos 2\bar\lambda$ are orthogonal, so each coefficient is an average, for instance $e\cos\lambda_A = 2\,\langle E \sin\bar\lambda\rangle$. The two yearly coefficients give $e$ and $\lambda_A$, and the half-yearly one gives $e_\varepsilon$, hence $\varepsilon = 2\arctan\sqrt{e_\varepsilon}$.

Applied to the real $E$ of 2026, the averages give the first column of the table. They are close, but not exact, because the first-order formula leaves out a cross term: the half-yearly wave depends on the true longitude, $e_\varepsilon \sin 2(\bar\lambda + q)$, and the product of the two waves contains a yearly part of amplitude $e\,e_\varepsilon$, worth 19.8 s, which the fit takes for part of the eccentricity.

The exact fit

The second column fits the exact model, $E = -q + (\lambda - \alpha)$ plus a constant, to the 365 daily values by least squares. The model is not linear in $e$, $\lambda_A$ and $\varepsilon$, so the fit starts from the first-order values and improves them by Gauss–Newton iteration: at each step the model is linearised about the current values, the linear least-squares problem is solved for the corrections, and the corrections are applied. It converges in a few steps and leaves residuals of 0.9 s RMS.

The exact fit recovers the eccentricity of the seasons to 0.1%, and its apogee is the one of Kepler's ellipse, 103.4°, which page 2.1's fit missed by 0.35°. Its obliquity is 2.8′ too small, because the circle is not quite the right shape. At second order the eccentric gets a term in $\sin 2M$ wrong by 0.72′ (page 2.1), and since $2M$ is close to $2\bar\lambda$ plus a half turn, the fit takes up most of it by lowering $e_\varepsilon$, which costs about 3′ of obliquity.

4 · TestThe equation of time against the real Sun

Figure 5 compares the model, the eccentric fitted to the seasons of 2026 with the mean obliquity, with the real equation of time of the reference sky. Page 1.3's clock had no equation of time at all, so its noon was wrong by the whole of $E$, from −16.45 to +14.20 minutes.

Through 2026 the model is within 6 s of the real equation of time, from −4.8 s on 6 August to +6.0 s on 20 November. Almost all of it, up to 7.0 s, comes from the error of the eccentric's longitude, the 1.7′ of page 2.1, since 1′ of longitude is about 4 s of time. The rest, under 1 s, comes from nutation and the definition of the mean Sun. The small monthly ripples in (b) are the Moon's, as on page 2.1: the Earth swings about the common centre of mass of the Earth and the Moon, and the Sun seen from it moves to and fro by a few arcseconds.

Ptolemy's equation of time

Ptolemy computed the two parts with his own Sun and his own tables. He measured them over the intervals where each accumulates most, in time-degrees, the time the equator takes to turn 1°, 4 minutes:

Accumulated difference Ptolemy (III 9) his model
the anomaly, between the points of mean speed 4¾ 4.78
the meridian crossings, two signs about a solstice 4½ 4.53
mid-Aquarius to the end of Libra: anomaly 3⅔ 3.62
… and meridian crossings 4⅔ 4.72
greatest to least $E$ 8⅓ 8.38

His second line comes from his table of right ascensions: the two signs on either side of a solstice cross the meridian in $64;32$ time-degrees (64.53), 4½ more than their 60° (Almagest I 16). Every figure agrees with his own model to a few hundredths of a time-degree.

His total is larger than the real one. The real equation of time of AD 140 ran over 31.3 minutes, 7.82 time-degrees, while his 8⅓ time-degrees are 33⅓ minutes, because his eccentricity, 1/24, is a fifth too large (page 2.1).

For the Sun an error of half an hour hardly matters, but in 1⅑ hours, twice his greatest difference, the Moon moves 0.61°, and the Moon's tables are counted in mean solar days. This is why he gives the rule for converting true into mean solar days at the end of his theory of the Sun, just before that of the Moon.

5 · ResidualThe equation of time over the centuries

Our model holds its apogee fixed at its place of 2026. Over the century around 2026 its error grows to −11.4 and +12.7 s, and over a thousand years to −76 and +81 s. The reason is the one page 2.1 found for the longitude: the apogee moves relative to γ, by about 1.75° a century.

The real equation of time changes with it. Its half-yearly wave is tied to the equinoxes and solstices, but its yearly wave is tied to the apogee, so as the apogee moves the two waves meet at other places, and the curve changes shape, as Figure 6 shows.

Around 1246 the apogee passed the summer solstice, the perigee the winter solstice, and the curve was symmetric, from −15.65 to +15.65 minutes. Before, the deep minimum came first: −17.6 and +13.5 minutes in 141 BC. Since then the maximum has taken over: −14.2 and +16.5 minutes in 2026, −12.2 and +17.0 in 3000.

Copernicus measured the change. Where Ptolemy found 8⅓ time-degrees between the extremes, "at present", with the limits moved to about 20° of Aquarius and 10° of Scorpio, the difference "has contracted to 7° 48´ time-degrees. For, these [phenomena] too change in time on account of the mutability of the perigee and the eccentricity" (De Revolutionibus III 26 [f. 97r]). His 7;48 time-degrees are 31.2 minutes; the real range in 1515 was 31.1.

Most of the apogee's motion belongs to γ, the origin of every longitude, which slides westward along the ecliptic. The whole frame we have used since page 1.2 is turning slowly, and page 2.3 measures how fast.

Exercises

Compute the equation of time at 12:00 UT on 3 November 2026 from the eccentric of page 2.1 ($L_0 = 280.45897^\circ$, $n = 0.98564736^\circ$ a day, $e = 0.033473$, $\lambda_A = 103.732^\circ$) and the mean obliquity $\varepsilon = 23.4358^\circ$, and split it into its two parts. Check with Figures 3 and 4.

Find $d$ (in TT, 69 s later than UT), then $\bar\lambda$, $M$, $q$ and $\lambda$ as on page 2.1, then $\alpha$ from $\tan\alpha = \cos\varepsilon\tan\lambda$ in the quadrant of $\lambda$.

At 12:00 UT, $d = 9803.0009$ days, so $\bar\lambda = L_0 + n\,d = 222.7609^\circ$ and $M = \bar\lambda - \lambda_A = 119.0289^\circ$. The equation of centre is $q = -1.7041^\circ$, so $\lambda = 221.0568^\circ$, and $\alpha = 218.6309^\circ$, so $\lambda - \alpha = 2.4258^\circ$.

Then $E = -q + (\lambda - \alpha) = 1.7041^\circ + 2.4258^\circ = 4.1300^\circ$, which is $6.82 + 9.70 = 16.52$ minutes, or +16 min 31 s. Both parts are positive in early November, which is why $E$ is greatest then. The first-order formula gives $6.71 + 9.83 = 16.54$ minutes.

The real equation of time is +16 min 27 s, so the model is 4 s too large. The real Sun crosses the meridian of Greenwich 16 min 27 s before 12:00 UT.

Before looking at Figure 4, predict on which dates $E$ would vanish if the Sun moved uniformly ($e = 0$), and on which dates if the ecliptic were not tilted ($\varepsilon = 0$). Then find the real dates, and explain why there are four.

Each part vanishes where its sine does: $\sin 2\lambda = 0$ for one, $\sin M = 0$ for the other. In Figure 4, $E = 0$ where the curve crosses the meridian.

With $e = 0$ only the half-yearly wave is left, $E = \lambda - \alpha$, which vanishes four times, when the uniform Sun is at an equinox or a solstice: 22 March, 21 June, 21 September and 21 December 2026 ("obliquity only" in Figure 4).

With $\varepsilon = 0$ only the yearly wave is left, $E = -q$, which vanishes twice, at the perigee and the apogee: 4 January and 5 July ("eccentricity only").

With both, the model and the real Sun agree on the dates: 15 April, 12 June, 1 September and 25 December. There are four because the half-yearly wave is the larger, 9.86 against 7.67 minutes, so it decides how often the sum changes sign; the yearly wave only moves the zeros.

Show that $|\lambda - \alpha|$ is greatest where $d\alpha/d\lambda = 1$, that is, where $\sin^2\lambda = 1/(1 + \cos\varepsilon)$, and that its greatest value is $\arcsin\tan^2(\varepsilon/2)$. Then check Copernicus' limits with his obliquity, $23^\circ 28\tfrac12'$ (De Revolutionibus III 2): the 88° from 46° to 134° cross the meridian in about 93 time-degrees, and the 92° from 134° to 226° in about 87.

For the first part, differentiate $\tan\alpha = \cos\varepsilon\tan\lambda$. For the greatest value, use the result of page 2.1's Exercise 2 with $e_\varepsilon$ and $2\lambda$.

$\lambda - \alpha$ is extreme where its derivative, $1 - d\alpha/d\lambda$, vanishes. From $\sec^2\alpha\,d\alpha = \cos\varepsilon\sec^2\lambda\,d\lambda$ and $\sec^2\alpha = 1 + \cos^2\varepsilon\tan^2\lambda$,

$$\frac{d\alpha}{d\lambda} = \frac{\cos\varepsilon}{\cos^2\lambda + \cos^2\varepsilon\sin^2\lambda} = 1 \iff (1 - \cos\varepsilon)(\cos\varepsilon\sin^2\lambda - \cos^2\lambda) = 0,$$

so $\cos\varepsilon\sin^2\lambda = 1 - \sin^2\lambda$ and $\sin^2\lambda = 1/(1 + \cos\varepsilon)$. Then $\cos 2\lambda = 1 - 2\sin^2\lambda = -e_\varepsilon$, the condition of page 2.1's Exercise 2 ($\cos M = -e$) with $2\lambda$ for $M$, so the greatest value is $\arcsin e_\varepsilon$.

With $\varepsilon = 23^\circ 28\tfrac12'$ the speeds are equal at $\lambda = 46^\circ 14'$ (46.24°), Copernicus' 16th degree of Taurus, and by symmetry at 133.76°, his 14th degree of Leo. Between 46° and 134° the right ascension grows by 92.95°, "about 93", and from 134° to 226° by 87.05°. The greatest reduction is 2.474°, 9.90 minutes; today's is 9.86.
