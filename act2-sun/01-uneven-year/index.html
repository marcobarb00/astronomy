On page 1.3 we let the Sun move along the ecliptic at a constant rate. That Sun keeps time well on average, but its noon came out wrong by up to a quarter of an hour, and its day too short by up to 13 minutes at latitude 65°, because the real Sun runs up to 2° ahead of it in spring and as far behind it in autumn.

Hipparchus measured this unevenness and explained it with a single circle whose centre is not the Earth. With his circle we can predict the Sun's longitude to within two arcminutes, and from here on we judge every model by its error curve, the difference between the model and the real sky, plotted against time.

1 · ObservationFour unequal seasons

The equinoxes and the solstices divide the year at the moments when the Sun's longitude reaches 0°, 90°, 180° and 270°. A Sun moving uniformly would take a quarter of the year, 91.31 days, over each of these quarters. The real Sun of 2026 takes between 89 and almost 94 days, as the reference sky shows (times in UT; the seasons are named as in the northern hemisphere, as in the sources):

Season Begins Longitude $\lambda$ Length
spring 20 March, 14:31 $0^\circ$ to $90^\circ$ 92.74 days
summer 21 June, 08:15 $90^\circ$ to $180^\circ$ 93.66 days
autumn 23 September, 00:10 $180^\circ$ to $270^\circ$ 89.86 days
winter 21 December, 20:47 $270^\circ$ to $360^\circ$ 88.98 days

The half of the year from the March equinox to the September equinox is 7.6 days longer than the other half. Since the Sun covers 180° of longitude in each half, it must move more slowly in the northern summer than in the winter, and Figure 1 shows that it does.

The daily motion swings by about 3.4% on either side of the mean, from 57.2′ on 3 July to 61.2′ around New Year. Over a quarter of a year these small differences add up to the two or more days by which the longest and the shortest seasons differ from 91.31 days.

Hipparchus measured the same inequality in the second century BC. Ptolemy reports that he took 94½ days from the spring equinox to the summer solstice and 92½ days from there to the autumn equinox, "and then, with these observations as his sole data", found the size and the direction of the Sun's eccentric circle (Almagest III 4).

His seasons were not ours. The reference sky gives 94.01 days for the spring of 141 BC and 92.34 days for its summer, close to his values, so that spring was then the longest season, while in 2026 summer is. Section 5 comes back to this change, which the model of this page leaves out.

2 · ModelThe eccentric circle

Uniform motion on a circle

Greek astronomers required every celestial motion to be uniform and circular. Ptolemy states the rule precisely: if we imagine each body carried around by a straight line, "the straight line in question describes equal angles at the centre of its revolution in equal times" (Almagest III 3).

Seen from the Earth, the Sun's motion is uneven, so the rule allows two explanations. Either the centre of the Sun's circle is not the Earth, or the Sun moves uniformly on a second, smaller circle whose centre is carried around the first. These are the eccentric and the epicycle.

Ptolemy shows that both can produce the Sun's inequality, and chooses the eccentric "since that is simpler and is performed by means of one motion instead of two" (III 4). Page 3.1 proves that the two are equivalent, and uses the epicycle for the Moon.

The equation of centre

Let the Sun move uniformly, at the rate $n$ of page 1.3, on a circle of radius 1 whose centre C lies at a distance $e$ from the Earth O. The point of the circle farthest from the Earth, in the direction from O through C, is the apogee, at longitude $\lambda_A$, and the nearest, opposite it, is the perigee.

Seen from C, the Sun's longitude is the mean longitude $\bar\lambda = L_0 + n\,d$ of page 1.3, and we count its angle from the apogee, the mean anomaly $M = \bar\lambda - \lambda_A$. Seen from O, it is the true longitude $\lambda$, and the difference $q = \lambda - \bar\lambda$ is called the equation of centre.

To find $q$ we write the Sun's position as a vector from the Earth, $\vec{OS} = \vec{OC} + \vec{CS}$, in a frame turned to the direction of $\vec{CS}$: its first axis points to $\bar\lambda$ and its second 90° further east. There $\vec{CS} = (1, 0)$, and $\vec{OC}$, of length $e$ towards $\lambda_A = \bar\lambda - M$, is $e\,(\cos M, -\sin M)$, so

$$\vec{OS} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 + e\cos M \\ -e\sin M \end{pmatrix}.$$

The direction of this vector, measured from the first axis, is the angle from $\bar\lambda$ to $\lambda$, which is $q$:

$$q = -\operatorname{atan2}(e \sin M,\ 1 + e \cos M), \qquad \lambda = \bar\lambda + q.$$

From the apogee to the perigee, $0^\circ < M < 180^\circ$, $q$ is negative and the Sun lags behind its mean longitude, while on the other half of the circle it runs ahead. Ptolemy uses the same sign, subtracting the equation in the first half (Almagest III 8). Fitzpatrick counts $M$ from the perigee, so his $q$ has the opposite sign for the same $M$.

The Sun's eccentricity turns out to be about $1/30$, small enough to expand the formula. To first order in $e$,

$$q \approx -e \sin M \quad \text{(in radians)},$$

so the Sun is never more than about $e$ radians, nearly 2°, from its mean place. The terms we dropped are of order $e^2/2$, under 2′ for the Sun, and Exercise 2 shows that the largest value of $|q|$ is exactly $\arcsin e$.

The real orbit is an ellipse, and to first order Kepler's ellipse gives $q \approx -2e_\text{K} \sin M$, where $e_\text{K}$ is its eccentricity. The eccentric gives the same directions if its centre is offset by $e = 2e_\text{K}$, as our fit in section 3 confirms. Page 4.2 compares the two to second order.

Why the seasons are unequal

The lines from the Earth towards the equinoxes and the solstices cut the eccentric into four arcs. Because the Earth is not at the centre, the arcs are unequal, and the Sun, covering each of them at the same uniform rate, takes longest over the longest.

The longest arc is the one that contains the apogee, where the circle is farthest from the Earth. In 2026 the apogee is at about 104°, in summer, which is why summer is the longest season and winter, which contains the perigee, the shortest.

3 · FitEccentricity and apogee from two seasons

Besides the mean motion $n$, which page 1.3 took from the length of the year, the eccentric has three parameters: the eccentricity $e$, the longitude of the apogee $\lambda_A$, and the mean longitude at one moment, which fixes $L_0$. Hipparchus found the first two from the lengths of spring and summer alone.

Hipparchus' method

Put the Earth at the origin, with the $x$ axis towards γ and the $y$ axis towards the summer solstice, and the centre of the eccentric at $C = (x, y) = e\,(\cos\lambda_A, \sin\lambda_A)$, in units of its radius. The line from the Earth towards γ is the $x$ axis, which meets the circle where its height above C is $-y$, at the angle $-\arcsin y$ as seen from C.

In the same way the line towards the summer solstice meets the circle at $90^\circ + \arcsin x$, the autumn equinox at $180^\circ + \arcsin y$, and the winter solstice at $270^\circ - \arcsin x$. Seen from C the Sun moves $360^\circ/Y$ a day, so the lengths $T_1$ and $T_2$ of spring and summer give their arcs:

$$a_1 = \frac{360^\circ\, T_1}{Y} = 90^\circ + \arcsin x + \arcsin y, \qquad a_2 = \frac{360^\circ\, T_2}{Y} = 90^\circ - \arcsin x + \arcsin y.$$

Adding and subtracting the two equations separates the unknowns:

$$\arcsin y = \frac{a_1 + a_2 - 180^\circ}{2}, \qquad \arcsin x = \frac{a_1 - a_2}{2}, \qquad e = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}, \qquad \lambda_A = \operatorname{atan2}(y, x).$$

The other two seasons follow from the same centre, with the arcs $a_3 = 90^\circ - \arcsin x - \arcsin y$ for autumn and $a_4 = 90^\circ + \arcsin x - \arcsin y$ for winter. Two seasons therefore predict the other two, which makes the model testable even before we compare it with the sky.

With Hipparchus' 94½ and 92½ days, the method puts the centre at $e = 0.0414$ towards $\lambda_A = 65.4^\circ$. Ptolemy, who works with chords on a circle of radius 60, finds the centres 2;29½ apart, so that the radius is "approximately 24 times" that distance, and the apogee 24;30° in advance of the summer solstice. He adopts $e = 2;30/60 = 1/24$ and $\lambda_A = 65;30^\circ$ (III 4). Exercise 1 follows the computation.

The Sun of 2026

Applied to the seasons of 2026, 92.739 and 93.663 days with the modern year of 365.24219 days, the method gives $e = 0.03347$ and $\lambda_A = 103.73^\circ$. It then predicts autumn and winter to within 33 minutes of the real ones.

Kepler's ellipse for 2026, from the elements used by our reference sky, has $e_\text{K} = 0.01672$ and its apogee at $103.38^\circ$. Our $e$ equals $2e_\text{K}$ to within 0.1%, while our apogee is 0.35° too far east.

The apogee is the delicate part of the fit. Moving one hour from summer to spring moves it by 0.9° and hardly changes $e$, and Toomer notes that "even as small a change as 1 hour in an interval has an effect of about 1° in the location of the apogee" (note to III 4), while the ancient observations were timed to a quarter of a day at best.

The mean longitude at the epoch

The last parameter comes from the moment of the spring equinox, which we timed on page 1.2 to find γ. At that moment the Sun is at γ, at the angle $-\arcsin y$ as seen from C, which is therefore its mean longitude. In 2026 the equinox came on 20 March at 14:32 TT, 9575.106 days after J2000.0, with $-\arcsin y = 358.137^\circ$. Going back to the epoch at the rate $n$,

$$L_0 = 358.137^\circ - n \times 9575.106\ \text{d} = 280.459^\circ \pmod{360^\circ},$$

0.45′ less than the value 280.46646° of Meeus that page 1.3 used without deriving it.

Ptolemy fixed his epoch in the same way, from the autumn equinox he observed in AD 132. His eccentric put the Sun 116;40° of mean motion past the apogee at that moment, and he carried this back 879 years to the era Nabonassar, where the mean Sun was at Pisces 0;45°, that is, at 330.75° (III 7).

4 · TestThe eccentric against the real Sun

Figure 4 compares our fitted eccentric with the reference Sun through 2026, and keeps the uniform Sun of page 1.3 as a faint ghost. As on page 1.1, we plot model minus sky, $\Delta\lambda = \lambda_\text{model} - \lambda_\text{ref}$, in arcminutes.

The uniform circle is wrong by up to 115′, the eccentric by at most 1.7′, between −1.68′ on 19 November and +0.97′ on 6 August, about seventy times less. Figure 6 shows this remaining error at its own scale.

The eccentric also removes the error that page 1.3 left in the length of the day, which depends on the Sun's declination and so on its longitude. With the eccentric's longitude in the declination formula, the largest error of the day in 2026 falls from 5.3 to 0.2 minutes at Rome, from 7.5 to 0.2 minutes in London, and from 13.1 to 0.3 minutes at latitude 65°.

Ptolemy's Sun

Ptolemy's model has the same shape with his own parameters: the eccentricity 1/24, the apogee at 65;30°, and his mean Sun, which starts at 330.75° at noon at Alexandria on the first day of the era Nabonassar, 26 February 747 BC, and moves 0;59,8,17,13,12,31° a day. Ptolemy counted days of the turning Earth, so we convert the reference sky's uniform time to them with $\Delta T$, 5.85 hours at his epoch (page 1.3).

In AD 140 his Sun is behind the real one by 1.1° on average, between −1.5° and −0.7° through the year. Most of this is in his epoch. At the autumn equinox he observed on 25 September 132, two hours after noon, his model puts the Sun at 180.006°, at the equinox within a fraction of an arcminute, but the real Sun had passed 180° 34 hours earlier, at 04:21 on 24 September.

Ptolemy's other equinox and his solstice are also about a day late (Exercise 3), and whether he really observed them is one of the long disputes about the Almagest. Toomer calls the delay "the strongest argument of those modern critics who have maintained that Ptolemy 'faked' observations" (note to III 1).

The part of the error that changes through the year comes from his parameters: his eccentricity is a fifth too large and his apogee was already 5° behind the real one. Over the centuries the error grows because his year is 6.4 minutes too long. His mean Sun falls behind by 0.44° a century, and by 2026 his Sun is 9.2° behind.

5 · ResidualWhat the eccentric leaves out

Figure 6 shows our eccentric alone. Over 2026 it stays within 1.7′ of the real Sun. About half of this error is the shape of the circle, which differs from Kepler's ellipse at second order in $e$ by up to 0.72′ (page 4.2), and most of the rest comes from the small errors of our fit, whose apogee is 0.35° from Kepler's.

The small monthly ripples come from the Moon. The Earth and the Moon both turn about their common centre of mass, so the Earth swings a little to and fro every month, and the Sun, seen from the Earth, moves the opposite way by up to 7″.

Over the century around 2026 the error stays between −3.1′ and +2.7′, and over a thousand years it reaches −19′ and +17′, because the apogee is not fixed. Fitting the real seasons of other years by Hipparchus' method shows it moving east:

Year Spring Summer Apogee $\lambda_A$
141 BC (Hipparchus) 94.01 d 92.34 d $65.9^\circ$
AD 140 (Ptolemy) 93.90 d 92.56 d $70.7^\circ$
880 (al-Battani) 93.51 d 93.07 d $83.6^\circ$
1250 93.29 d 93.29 d $90.1^\circ$
1515 (Copernicus) 93.11 d 93.43 d $94.7^\circ$
2026 92.74 d 93.66 d $103.7^\circ$

The apogee moves by about 1.75° a century relative to γ. Around 1250 it passed the summer solstice, and spring and summer were then equally long. Ptolemy thought it fixed, because he found Hipparchus' 94½ and 92½ days again in his own time, and concluded that the eccentric "always maintains the same position relative to the solsticial and equinoctial points" (III 4). Exercise 3 shows what went wrong.

Later astronomers saw it move. Al-Battani, around 880, put the apogee 7°43′ before the solstice, at 82.3°, and Copernicus in 1515 found it 6⅔° after it, at 96.7°, and wrote of the eccentricity and the apogee that "both values are now found to have changed with a perceptible difference" (De Revolutionibus III 16 [f. 87v]).

Most of this motion belongs to γ rather than to the apogee. The origin of longitudes itself slides westward along the ecliptic, so that every longitude counted from it grows by about 1.4° a century, as page 2.3 explains. Only the remaining 0.3° a century is a real turning of the Earth's orbit, caused by the pull of the planets.

The eccentric also predicts the Sun's distance, and there it fails. With $e = 0.0335$ the distance ranges from 0.9665 to 1.0335 times its mean, so the Sun's disc should vary between 30.95′ and 33.10′ across. The real disc varies only between 31.46′ and 32.53′, half as much, because the ellipse's distance varies with $e_\text{K} = e/2$. The circle gets the directions right with twice the real variation in distance, a fault that returns, much larger, with the Moon on page 3.2.

Before following the apogee over the centuries, we take up an effect of the Sun's uneven motion that can be seen every day. A sundial reads the Sun's hour angle, which depends on its right ascension, and the right ascension depends both on the uneven longitude of this page and on the tilt of the ecliptic. Page 2.2 combines the two into the equation of time.

Exercises

Find Hipparchus' Sun from his two seasons: spring lasts 94½ days and summer 92½ days, with Ptolemy's year of 365;14,48 days (365.24667 days). Compute $e$, $\lambda_A$, the lengths of autumn and winter, and the greatest equation of centre, and compare them with Ptolemy's values. Check with the preset "Hipparchus" of Figure 3, then with "141 BC", the real seasons of his time.

Convert the two seasons into arcs of the eccentric, $a_i = 360^\circ\, T_i / Y$, and use the boxed formulas of section 3.

The arcs are $a_1 = 93.1425^\circ$ ($93^\circ 08' 33''$) and $a_2 = 91.1713^\circ$ ($91^\circ 10' 17''$), Ptolemy's 93;9° and 91;11°. So $\arcsin y = (a_1 + a_2 - 180^\circ)/2 = 2.1569^\circ$ and $\arcsin x = (a_1 - a_2)/2 = 0.9856^\circ$, which give $y = 0.03764$ and $x = 0.01720$.

Then $e = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} = 0.04138$, or 2;28,58 on a radius of 60, which Ptolemy rounds to 2;30, that is, $1/24$. The apogee is at $\lambda_A = \operatorname{atan2}(y, x) = 65.44^\circ$, 24.56° before the summer solstice, where Ptolemy has 24;30° and so 65;30°.

Autumn takes $a_3 = 90^\circ - \arcsin x - \arcsin y = 86.857^\circ$, or 88.12 days, and winter $a_4 = 90^\circ + \arcsin x - \arcsin y = 88.829^\circ$, or 90.12 days: Ptolemy's 88⅛ and 90⅛ days. The greatest equation is $\arcsin e = 2^\circ 22' 18''$, and with Ptolemy's rounded $1/24$ it is $2^\circ 23' 17''$, his 2;23°.

The real seasons of 141 BC, 94.01 and 92.34 days, give $e = 0.0352$ and $\lambda_A = 65.86^\circ$. Hipparchus' apogee is within half a degree, but his eccentricity is 18% too large, because his spring and summer together are 0.64 days too long.

Starting from $q = -\operatorname{atan2}(e\sin M, 1 + e\cos M)$, show that $q \approx -e\sin M + \tfrac12 e^2 \sin 2M$ to second order in $e$ (in radians). Then show that $|q|$ is greatest when $\cos M = -e$, and that its greatest value is exactly $\arcsin e$. Where is the Sun then, as seen from the Earth? Find Ptolemy's greatest equation for $e = 1/24$, and ours for $e = 0.03347$.

For small $e$, $\tan q$ is small and $q \approx \tan q$ to second order. For the maximum, differentiate $\tan|q| = e\sin M / (1 + e \cos M)$ with respect to $M$.

For $0 < M < 180^\circ$, $\tan|q| = e\sin M / (1 + e\cos M)$. Expanding the denominator, $e\sin M\,(1 - e\cos M + \dots) = e\sin M - \tfrac12 e^2 \sin 2M + O(e^3)$, and since $\arctan u = u - u^3/3 + \dots$ differs from $u$ only at third order,

$$q \approx -e \sin M + \tfrac12 e^2 \sin 2M.$$

The derivative of $e\sin M / (1 + e\cos M)$ is

$$\frac{e\cos M\,(1 + e\cos M) + e^2 \sin^2 M}{(1 + e\cos M)^2} = \frac{e\,(\cos M + e)}{(1 + e\cos M)^2},$$

which vanishes at $\cos M = -e$. There $\sin M = \sqrt{1 - e^2}$ and $1 + e\cos M = 1 - e^2$, so $\tan|q| = e / \sqrt{1 - e^2}$, which means $\sin|q| = e$. Since $M = 90^\circ + \arcsin e$ and $q = -\arcsin e$, the true longitude is $\lambda = \lambda_A + 90^\circ$: seen from the Earth, the Sun is a right angle from the apogee. This is Ptolemy's construction in III 4, a right angle at the Earth.

For $e = 1/24$ the greatest equation is $2^\circ 23' 17''$, at $M = 92^\circ 23' 17''$: Ptolemy's 2;23° at 92;23°. For $e = 0.03347$ it is $1^\circ 55' 05''$, at $M = 91.92^\circ$. The first-order formula $-e\sin M$ differs from the exact one by at most 1.96′ for our Sun.

Ptolemy reports that he found Hipparchus' 94½ and 92½ days again in AD 139–140, from an autumn equinox on 26 September 139 about an hour after sunrise, a spring equinox on 22 March 140 about an hour after noon, and a summer solstice on 25 June 140 about two hours after midnight, and he concludes that the apogee never moves (III 1, III 4). The reference sky puts these moments, in Alexandria mean time, at 24 September 139, 21:17, 21 March 140, 16:16, and 23 June 140, 13:49. What went wrong, and what were the real seasons and apogee in his time?

Compare the delays of the three observations, then use Figure 3 with the real lengths of spring and summer.

All three observations are late: the autumn equinox by about 34 hours, the spring equinox by 21 hours and the solstice by 36 hours. The real spring of AD 140 lasted 93.90 days and its summer 92.56 days, not 94½ and 92½.

Fitted by Hipparchus' method, these real seasons put the apogee at 70.7°, 5° further east than in 141 BC, with $e = 0.0349$. Ptolemy's delays happen to reproduce the intervals of Hipparchus' time, so his data showed no motion; since one hour moved between two seasons shifts the apogee by about 0.9°, errors of a day hide a shift of a few degrees completely.

The apogee kept moving at about 1.75° a century relative to γ: in 2026 it is at 103.7°. Page 2.3 explains that most of this is the motion of γ itself.
