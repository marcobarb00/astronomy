On page 1.3 we let the Sun move along the ecliptic at a constant rate. That Sun keeps time well on average, but its noon came out wrong by up to a quarter of an hour, and the length of its day by several minutes, because the real Sun runs up to 2° ahead of it in spring and as far behind it in autumn.

Hipparchus measured this unevenness and explained it with a single circle whose centre is not the Earth. With his circle we can predict the Sun's ecliptic longitude to within two arcminutes, and from here on we judge every model by its error curve, the difference between the model and the real sky, plotted against time.

1 · ObservationFour unequal seasons

The equinoxes and the solstices divide the year at the moments when the Sun's ecliptic longitude reaches 0°, 90°, 180° and 270°. A Sun moving uniformly would take a quarter of the year, 91.31 days, over each of these quarters. The real Sun of 2026 takes between 89 and almost 94 days. The table gives its seasons from the reference sky, with the times in UT and the names of the northern hemisphere, which the sources use.

Season Begins Ecliptic longitude $\lambda$ Length
spring 20 March, 14:31 $0^\circ$ to $90^\circ$ 92.74 days
summer 21 June, 08:15 $90^\circ$ to $180^\circ$ 93.66 days
autumn 23 September, 00:10 $180^\circ$ to $270^\circ$ 89.86 days
winter 21 December, 20:47 $270^\circ$ to $360^\circ$ 88.98 days

The half of the year from the March equinox to the September equinox is 7.6 days longer than the other half. Since the Sun covers 180° of ecliptic longitude in each half, it must move more slowly in the northern summer than in the winter, and Figure 1 shows that it does.

The daily motion swings by about 3.4% on either side of the mean. Over a quarter of a year these small differences add up to the two or more days by which the longest and the shortest seasons differ from a quarter of the year.

Hipparchus measured the same inequality in the second century BC. Ptolemy reports that he took 94½ days from the spring equinox to the summer solstice and 92½ days from there to the autumn equinox, "and then, with these observations as his sole data", found the size and the direction of the Sun's eccentric circle (Almagest III 4).

The reference sky shows that the seasons of his time were not those of today: it gives 94.01 days for the spring of 141 BC and 92.34 days for its summer, close to his values, so spring was then the longest season, where in 2026 summer is. Whatever sets the lengths of the seasons has moved since Hipparchus, and the eccentric fitted to one year cannot follow it.

2 · ModelThe eccentric circle

Uniform motion on a circle

Greek astronomers required every celestial motion to be uniform and circular. Ptolemy states the rule precisely: if we imagine each body carried around by a straight line, "the straight line in question describes equal angles at the centre of its revolution in equal times" (Almagest III 3).

Seen from the Earth, the Sun's motion is uneven, so the rule allows two explanations. Either the centre of the Sun's circle is not the Earth, or the Sun moves uniformly on a second, smaller circle whose centre is carried around the first. These are the eccentric and the epicycle.

Ptolemy shows that both can produce the Sun's inequality, and chooses the eccentric "since that is simpler and is performed by means of one motion instead of two" (III 4). Page 3.1 proves that the two are equivalent, and uses the epicycle for the Moon.

Both constructions take the Earth as at rest, as Hipparchus and Ptolemy did: they are geostatic. Observations made from the Earth cannot check that choice, because only relative motion is observed, as Copernicus puts it: "every observed change of place is caused by a motion of either the observed object or the observer or, of course, by an unequal displacement of each" (De Revolutionibus I 5).

The eccentric can therefore be turned into a heliostatic description. An equal circle, its centre offset the opposite way, carries the Earth about a resting Sun (Figure 2), and the line from the Earth to the Sun is at every moment the same, in length and direction, as in Hipparchus' eccentric. The Sun is seen exactly where the eccentric puts it, so the choice of the body at rest does not change a single prediction. Copernicus, who chose the Sun, explains the Sun's inequality in this way, with the Earth on an eccentric (III 15).

The equation of centre

Let the Sun move uniformly, at the rate $n$ of page 1.3, on a circle of radius 1 whose centre C lies at a distance $e$ from the Earth O. The point of the circle farthest from the Earth, in the direction from O through C, is the apogee, at ecliptic longitude $\lambda_A$, and the nearest, opposite it, is the perigee.

Seen from C, the Sun's ecliptic longitude is the mean ecliptic longitude $\bar\lambda = L_0 + n\,d$ of page 1.3, and we count its angle from the apogee, the mean anomaly $M = \bar\lambda - \lambda_A$. Seen from O, it is the true ecliptic longitude $\lambda$, and the difference $q = \lambda - \bar\lambda$ is called the equation of centre (Figure 3).

To find $q$ we write the Sun's position as a vector from the Earth, $\vec{OS} = \vec{OC} + \vec{CS}$, in a frame turned to the direction of $\vec{CS}$: its first axis points to $\bar\lambda$ and its second axis 90° further east. There $\vec{CS} = (1, 0)$, and $\vec{OC}$, of length $e$ towards $\lambda_A = \bar\lambda - M$, is $e\,(\cos M, -\sin M)$, so

$$\vec{OS} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 + e\cos M \\ -e\sin M \end{pmatrix}.$$

The direction of this vector, measured from the first axis, is the angle from $\bar\lambda$ to $\lambda$, which is $q$:

$$q = -\operatorname{atan2}(e \sin M,\ 1 + e \cos M), \qquad \lambda = \bar\lambda + q.$$

From the apogee to the perigee, $0^\circ < M < 180^\circ$, $q$ is negative and the Sun lags behind its mean ecliptic longitude, while on the other half of the circle it runs ahead. Ptolemy uses the same sign, subtracting the equation in the first half (Almagest III 8). Fitzpatrick counts $M$ from the perigee, so his $q$ has the opposite sign for the same $M$.

The Sun's eccentricity turns out to be about $1/30$, small enough to expand the formula. To first order in $e$,

$$q \approx -e \sin M \quad \text{(in radians)},$$

so the Sun is never more than about $e$ radians, nearly 2°, from its mean place. The terms we dropped are of order $e^2/2$, under 2′ for the Sun, and Exercise 2 shows that the largest value of $|q|$ is exactly $\arcsin e$.

Kepler's ellipse, which the modern theory keeps as the first approximation to the Sun's path relative to the Earth, gives to first order $q \approx -2e_\text{K} \sin M$, where $e_\text{K}$ is its eccentricity. The eccentric gives the same directions if its centre is offset by $e = 2e_\text{K}$, as the fit in section 3 confirms. Page 4.2 compares the two to second order.

Why the seasons are unequal

The lines from the Earth towards the equinoxes and the solstices cut the eccentric into four arcs. Because the Earth is not at the centre of the eccentric, the arcs are unequal, and the Sun, covering each of them at the same uniform rate, takes longest over the longest.

The longest arc is the one that contains the apogee, where the circle is farthest from the Earth. In 2026 the apogee is at about 104°, in summer, which is why summer is the longest season and winter, which contains the perigee, the shortest.

The Sun's eccentricity is so small that, drawn to scale, the circle looks centred on the Earth. In Figure 4 it is therefore drawn beside a circle with $e = 0.2$, six times the Sun's, on which the same geometry gives seasons that differ far more.

3 · FitEccentricity and apogee from two seasons

The offset centre of the eccentric is what makes the seasons unequal, so their lengths reveal it: two of them fix the centre, its distance $e$ from the Earth and the direction of the apogee $\lambda_A$. The mean motion $n$ is already known from the length of the year (page 1.3), and $L_0$ needs one moment at which the Sun's ecliptic longitude is known exactly, an equinox.

The centre from two seasons

Put the Earth at the origin, with the $x$ axis towards γ and the $y$ axis towards the summer solstice, so that the two axes are the four lines from the Earth towards the equinoxes and the solstices. We write the centre of the eccentric as $C = (h, k) = e\,(\cos\lambda_A, \sin\lambda_A)$, in units of its radius, and keep $x$ and $y$ for the axes.

On the eccentric the Sun moves uniformly as seen from C, so the time it takes over a season is proportional to the angle, at C, between the two points where the circle meets the axes at the start and at the end of that season. Figure 5 (a) draws these angles with C six times farther from the Earth than the fit puts it, so that they can be seen, and Figure 5 (b) shows C at the fitted distance.

The $x$ axis runs at the distance $k$ below C. The radius from C to the point where it meets the circle towards γ has length 1 and drops by $k$, so it makes the angle $\arcsin k$ with the parallel to the $x$ axis through C, and C sees that point at $-\arcsin k$. On the other side, towards 180°, the same axis meets the circle at $180^\circ + \arcsin k$ as seen from C.

In the same way the $y$ axis runs at the distance $h$ to the left of C, which puts its point towards the summer solstice at $90^\circ + \arcsin h$ and its point towards the winter solstice at $270^\circ - \arcsin h$. The formulas hold for either sign. When the apogee is beyond 90°, as it is today, $h$ is negative: C lies to the left of the $y$ axis, the radius to the summer solstice leans towards γ, and the angle $90^\circ + \arcsin h$ is less than 90°, as in Figure 4 and in the preset "2026" of Figure 5.

Seen from C the Sun moves $360^\circ/Y$ a day, so the lengths $T_1$ and $T_2$ of spring and summer give the arcs between these points, the thick arcs of Figure 5 (a):

$$a_1 = \frac{360^\circ\, T_1}{Y} = 90^\circ + \arcsin h + \arcsin k, \qquad a_2 = \frac{360^\circ\, T_2}{Y} = 90^\circ - \arcsin h + \arcsin k.$$

Adding and subtracting the two equations separates the unknowns, and the centre then gives the eccentricity and the direction of the apogee:

$$\arcsin k = \frac{a_1 + a_2 - 180^\circ}{2}, \qquad \arcsin h = \frac{a_1 - a_2}{2}, \qquad e = \sqrt{h^2 + k^2}, \qquad \lambda_A = \operatorname{atan2}(k, h).$$

The other two seasons follow from the same centre, with the arcs $a_3 = 90^\circ - \arcsin h - \arcsin k$ for autumn and $a_4 = 90^\circ + \arcsin h - \arcsin k$ for winter. Two seasons therefore predict the other two, which makes the model testable even before we compare it with the sky.

The Sun of 2026

Applied to the seasons of 2026 in the table of section 1, with the year of page 1.3, the method gives $e = 0.03347$ and $\lambda_A = 103.73^\circ$. It then predicts autumn and winter to within about half an hour of the real ones.

The apogee is the more delicate of the two. Its direction depends on $h$, the small component of C, which is set by the small difference between spring and summer, so moving one hour from summer to spring shifts the apogee by 1.2° and hardly changes $e$.

The mean ecliptic longitude at the epoch

At the spring equinox, which we timed on page 1.2 to find γ, the Sun's ecliptic longitude is exactly 0°. The Sun is then at γ, at the angle $-\arcsin k$ as seen from C, which is therefore its mean ecliptic longitude. In 2026 the equinox came on 20 March at 14:32 TT (14:31 UT in the table of section 1), and the centre fitted to 2026 gives $-\arcsin k = 358.137^\circ$. Going back to the epoch J2000.0 at the rate $n$,

$$L_0 = 358.137^\circ - n \times 9575.106\ \text{d} = 280.459^\circ \pmod{360^\circ},$$

within half an arcminute of the value of Meeus that page 1.3 used without deriving it.

HistoryHipparchus' and Ptolemy's parameters

Ptolemy reports that Hipparchus found the centre from these two seasons alone (section 1), and gives his own computation of it (III 4). Hipparchus quotes equinoxes observed on a bronze ring at Alexandria, fixed in the plane of the equator, which marks the equinox on the day when the lit side of its inner surface changes from one side to the other (III 1).

Ptolemy found such rings unreliable: one in his city sometimes changed sides twice on the same day. A solstice is harder still to time, since the Sun's declination hardly changes near it, and Hipparchus admits errors of up to a quarter of a day in his.

With his seasons the method puts the apogee within half a degree of the one the real seasons of his time give, but makes the eccentricity a fifth too large: his spring and summer together are over half a day too long. The presets "Hipparchus" and "141 BC" of Figure 5 and Exercise 1 follow the difference.

Ptolemy fixed his epoch as we did, from an equinox he observed himself, but carried it back to the start of the era of Nabonassar, because the ancient observations are preserved from that era on (III 7).

4 · TestThe eccentric against the real Sun

We test the eccentric by plotting its error through 2026 in Figure 6. As on page 1.1, the error is the model minus the sky, $\Delta\lambda = \lambda_\text{model} - \lambda_\text{ref}$, in arcminutes.

The uniform circle is wrong by up to 115′ and the eccentric by at most 1.7′, about seventy times less and a twentieth of the Sun's apparent diameter. Away from 2026 the eccentric's error grows, to about 3′ fifty years earlier and almost 20′ five centuries away, so an eccentric fitted to one year serves for a few decades.

The length of the day depends on the Sun's declination, and so on its ecliptic longitude. With the eccentric's ecliptic longitude, the largest error in the length of the day in 2026, at the three geographic latitudes of page 1.3, falls from up to 13 minutes to under half a minute.

HistoryPtolemy's Sun against his observations

Ptolemy checked his Sun against equinoxes and a solstice he observed himself, found Hipparchus' seasons again, and concluded that the eccentric "always maintains the same position relative to the solsticial and equinoctial points" (III 4). With timings good to a quarter of a day, which can move the apogee by several degrees, he could not have seen it move, as Toomer notes (note to III 4).

Set against the reference sky, with $\Delta T$ turning its uniform time into his days, which followed the Earth's slowing rotation (page 1.3), his observations come about a day after the real events (Exercise 3). At the equinox from which he fixed his epoch, his model puts the Sun at the equinox, as it must, but the real Sun had reached it more than a day earlier. Toomer calls the delay "the strongest argument of those modern critics who have maintained that Ptolemy 'faked' observations" (note to III 1).

In his own time his Sun is therefore about a degree behind the real one, most of it from the late equinox from which he fixed his epoch. His year is too long, so his Sun falls further behind every century.

5 · ResidualWhat the eccentric leaves out

About half of the eccentric's small error over 2026 comes from the shape of the circle, which differs from the ellipse of the modern theory in the terms of second order in $e$ (page 4.2), and most of the rest from the fitted apogee, about a third of a degree east of the reference Sun's.

Kepler's ellipse is the first approximation used today for the Sun's path relative to the Earth. To second order it gives $q \approx -2e_\text{K}\sin M + \tfrac54 e_\text{K}^2 \sin 2M$; the eccentric's second term, $\tfrac12 e^2 \sin 2M$, is larger by 0.72′ at most.

The small monthly ripples of Figure 6 come from the Moon. The Earth and the Moon both turn about their common centre of mass, so the Earth swings a little to and fro every month, and the Sun, seen from the Earth, moves the opposite way by a few arcseconds.

Away from 2026 the error grows because the apogee is not fixed. Fitting the real seasons of other years by the same method shows it moving east by about 1.75° a century relative to γ. Around 1250 it passed the summer solstice, when spring and summer were equally long. Al-Battani and Copernicus, who found seasons unlike Ptolemy's, saw it move (De Revolutionibus III 16).

Most of this motion is that of γ itself. The origin of ecliptic longitudes slides westward along the ecliptic, so that every ecliptic longitude counted from it grows by about 1.4° a century, as page 2.3 explains. Only the remaining 0.3° a century is a turning of the Sun's path: in the ellipse that the modern theory keeps as its first approximation, the line of apsides, through the apogee and the perigee, turns under the pull of the planets.

The eccentric gives the Sun's distance too, from $1 - e$ to $1 + e$ times the radius of its circle, so the distance swings by as much as the daily motion of section 1, about 3.4% either way. The real distance, and with it the Sun's apparent diameter, swings by only half as much, $e_\text{K} = e/2$. The eccentric gets the directions right but doubles the variation of the distance, a fault that returns, much larger, with the Moon on page 3.2.

On a sundial the Sun's uneven motion shows every day. A sundial reads the Sun's hour angle, which depends on its right ascension, and the right ascension depends both on the uneven ecliptic longitude of this page and on the tilt of the ecliptic. So a sundial and a clock disagree through the year, by an amount that page 2.2 works out.

Exercises

Hipparchus' spring lasts 94½ days and his summer 92½ days, with Ptolemy's year of 365.24667 days. Find the centre $C = (h, k)$ of his eccentric, then $e$, $\lambda_A$, the lengths of autumn and winter and the greatest equation of centre, and compare them with Ptolemy's values. Check with the preset "Hipparchus" of Figure 5, then with "141 BC", the real seasons of his time.

Convert the two seasons into arcs of the eccentric, $a_i = 360^\circ\, T_i / Y$, and use the boxed formulas of section 3.

The arcs are $a_1 = 93.1425^\circ$ ($93^\circ 08' 33''$) and $a_2 = 91.1713^\circ$ ($91^\circ 10' 17''$), Ptolemy's $93^\circ 9'$ and $91^\circ 11'$ ($93.15^\circ$ and $91.18^\circ$). So $\arcsin k = (a_1 + a_2 - 180^\circ)/2 = 2.1569^\circ$ and $\arcsin h = (a_1 - a_2)/2 = 0.9856^\circ$, which give $k = 0.03764$ and $h = 0.01720$: in Hipparchus' time the apogee was before the summer solstice, so $h$ was positive.

Then $e = \sqrt{h^2 + k^2} = 0.04138$, or 2.483 parts on a radius of 60, which Ptolemy rounds to 2.5 parts, that is, $1/24$. The apogee is at $\lambda_A = \operatorname{atan2}(k, h) = 65.44^\circ$, 24.56° before the summer solstice, where Ptolemy has $24^\circ 30'$ and so $65^\circ 30'$ ($65.5^\circ$).

Autumn takes $a_3 = 90^\circ - \arcsin h - \arcsin k = 86.857^\circ$, or 88.12 days, and winter $a_4 = 90^\circ + \arcsin h - \arcsin k = 88.829^\circ$, or 90.12 days: Ptolemy's 88⅛ and 90⅛ days. The greatest equation is $\arcsin e = 2^\circ 22' 18''$, and with Ptolemy's rounded $1/24$ it is $2^\circ 23' 17''$, his $2^\circ 23'$ ($2.383^\circ$).

The real seasons of 141 BC, 94.01 and 92.34 days, give $e = 0.0352$ and $\lambda_A = 65.86^\circ$. Hipparchus' apogee is within half a degree, but his eccentricity is 18% too large, because his spring and summer together are 0.64 days too long.

Starting from $q = -\operatorname{atan2}(e\sin M, 1 + e\cos M)$, show that $q \approx -e\sin M + \tfrac12 e^2 \sin 2M$ to second order in $e$ (in radians). Then show that $|q|$ is greatest when $\cos M = -e$, and that its greatest value is exactly $\arcsin e$. Where is the Sun then, as seen from the Earth? Find Ptolemy's greatest equation for $e = 1/24$, and that of the 2026 fit, $e = 0.03347$.

For small $e$, $\tan q$ is small and $q \approx \tan q$ to second order. For the maximum, differentiate $\tan|q| = e\sin M / (1 + e \cos M)$ with respect to $M$.

For $0 < M < 180^\circ$, $\tan|q| = e\sin M / (1 + e\cos M)$. Expanding the denominator, $e\sin M\,(1 - e\cos M + \dots) = e\sin M - \tfrac12 e^2 \sin 2M + O(e^3)$, and since $\arctan u = u - u^3/3 + \dots$ differs from $u$ only at third order,

$$q \approx -e \sin M + \tfrac12 e^2 \sin 2M.$$

The derivative of $e\sin M / (1 + e\cos M)$ is

$$\frac{e\cos M\,(1 + e\cos M) + e^2 \sin^2 M}{(1 + e\cos M)^2} = \frac{e\,(\cos M + e)}{(1 + e\cos M)^2},$$

which vanishes at $\cos M = -e$. There $\sin M = \sqrt{1 - e^2}$ and $1 + e\cos M = 1 - e^2$, so $\tan|q| = e / \sqrt{1 - e^2}$, which means $\sin|q| = e$. Since $M = 90^\circ + \arcsin e$ and $q = -\arcsin e$, the true ecliptic longitude is $\lambda = \lambda_A + 90^\circ$: seen from the Earth, the Sun is a right angle from the apogee. This is Ptolemy's construction in III 4, a right angle at the Earth.

For $e = 1/24$ the greatest equation is $2^\circ 23' 17''$, at $M = 92^\circ 23' 17''$: Ptolemy's $2^\circ 23'$ at $92^\circ 23'$ ($2.383^\circ$ at $92.383^\circ$). For $e = 0.03347$ it is $1^\circ 55' 05''$, at $M = 91.92^\circ$. The first-order formula $-e\sin M$ differs from the exact one by at most 1.96′ for the 2026 fit.

Ptolemy reports that he found Hipparchus' 94½ and 92½ days again in AD 139–140, from an autumn equinox on 26 September 139 about an hour after sunrise, a spring equinox on 22 March 140 about an hour after noon, and a summer solstice on 25 June 140 about two hours after midnight, and he concludes that the apogee never moves (III 1, III 4). The reference sky puts these moments, in Alexandria mean time, at 24 September 139, 21:17, 21 March 140, 16:16, and 23 June 140, 13:49. What went wrong, and what were the real seasons and apogee in his time?

Compare the delays of the three observations, then work out the real lengths of spring and summer and check them with the preset "AD 140" of Figure 5.

All three observations are late: the autumn equinox by about 34 hours, the spring equinox by 21 hours and the solstice by 36 hours. The real spring of AD 140 lasted 93.90 days and its summer 92.56 days, not 94½ and 92½.

Fitted by Hipparchus' method, these real seasons put the apogee at 70.7°, 5° further east than in 141 BC, with $e = 0.0349$. Ptolemy's delays happen to reproduce the intervals of Hipparchus' time, so his data showed no motion; since one hour moved between the seasons of his time shifts the apogee by about 1.1°, errors of a day hide a shift of a few degrees completely.

The apogee kept moving at about 1.75° a century relative to γ: in 2026 it is at 103.7°. Page 2.3 explains that most of this is the motion of γ itself.
