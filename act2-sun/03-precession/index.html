Page 2.2 ended on γ, the origin of every longitude, sliding slowly along the ecliptic. Hipparchus found this motion when he compared the places of a few stars with those recorded a century and a half before him, and it is the reason the eccentric of page 2.1 drifts away from the real Sun over the centuries.

Once we know how fast γ moves, we can let the Sun's apogee move with it. The eccentric then stays within a few arcminutes of the real Sun over two thousand years, where with its apogee fixed it drifts by more than half a degree.

1 · ObservationThe longitudes of the stars grow

Hipparchus measured the longitudes of a few bright stars near the ecliptic from the Moon, at lunar eclipses, and compared them with those of Timocharis, who had observed at Alexandria about 150 years earlier. Spica, the brightest star of Virgo, was no longer where Timocharis had put it. According to Ptolemy, Hipparchus "computes that the distance by which Spica is in advance of the autumnal [equinoctial] point is about 6° in his own time, but was about 8° in Timocharis' time" (Almagest VII 2).

"In advance" means to the west, so Spica's longitude had grown from 172° to 174° in about 150 years, and the reference sky agrees with both values to within a fraction of a degree.

Later observers found Spica further east still. The table gives every longitude of Spica that Ptolemy and Copernicus record, from Timocharis to Copernicus' own observations at Frombork, beside the real longitude on the same date. Ptolemy counts eastward from the solstice or equinox that begins the star's quarter of the ecliptic (VII 4), and the table converts his values to longitudes.

Observer Year Recorded $\lambda$ Real $\lambda$ Source
Timocharis 294 BC $172^\circ 20'$ ($172.33^\circ$) $172.00^\circ$ Almagest VII 3
Timocharis 283 BC $172^\circ 30'$ ($172.50^\circ$) $172.16^\circ$ Almagest VII 3
Menelaus AD 98 $176^\circ 15'$ ($176.25^\circ$) $177.41^\circ$ Almagest VII 3
Ptolemy AD 137 $176^\circ 40'$ ($176.67^\circ$) $177.95^\circ$ Almagest VIII 1
Copernicus 1515 $197^\circ 14'$ ($197.23^\circ$) $197.09^\circ$ De Rev. III 2

Over the eighteen centuries of the table the recorded longitude grows by 25°. From Hipparchus' time to ours the real Spica has moved almost 30°, a whole sign, which is why the signs of the zodiac no longer match the constellations they are named after.

Hipparchus had first supposed that only the stars near the ecliptic moved. Ptolemy checked the alignments Hipparchus had recorded, of stars inside and far outside the zodiac, and found every one unchanged in his own time (VII 1), so the stars far from the ecliptic must move with Spica and keep their places among the others.

Spica's latitude did not change, staying 2° south of the ecliptic from Timocharis to Ptolemy. But its declination did: in Hipparchus' time Spica was north of the equator, and Ptolemy found it half a degree south (VII 3). The real Spica crossed the equator in between, and is now 11.3° south of it.

To see whether this holds for every star, and over a longer time, Figure 1 follows six stars of the reference sky, from stars near the ecliptic to Polaris near the pole, over six thousand years.

2 · ModelThe equator turns over the ecliptic

A turn about the poles of the ecliptic

Since Spica's latitude had not changed between Timocharis and himself, Hipparchus concluded that the sphere of the stars turns about the poles of the ecliptic, not about the poles of the equator, and Ptolemy confirmed it for almost every star over the longer interval to his own time (VII 3).

Take the ecliptic frame of page 1.2, with the $x$ axis towards γ and the $z$ axis towards the ecliptic pole K, and let the stars turn eastward about K by the angle $p\,(t - t_0)$, where $p$ is the rate of precession, about 50″ a year (section 3). A turn about the $z$ axis keeps the angle from K and adds to the angle about it, so each star keeps its latitude while its longitude grows uniformly:

$$\lambda = \lambda_0 + p\,(t - t_0), \qquad \beta = \beta_0.$$

Only the motion of the stars relative to γ can be observed, so the same model can be read the other way round, as Copernicus did: the stars stay still and the equator turns. "The reason is not that the sphere of the fixed stars moves eastward, but rather that the equator moves westward" (De Revolutionibus III 1).

In the stars' frame the ecliptic then stays where it is, and the pole P of the equator, at the angle ε from K and at longitude 90° (page 1.2), turns westward about K, so that at the time $t$ it is at longitude $90^\circ - p\,(t - t_0)$.

The equinox γ lies on both circles, so it is perpendicular to both poles, in the direction of $\vec{P} \times \vec{K}$, at longitude $-p\,(t - t_0)$. The order of the product picks γ rather than the autumn equinox: at $t_0$, $\vec{P} = (0, \sin\varepsilon, \cos\varepsilon)$ and $\vec{P} \times \vec{K} = (\sin\varepsilon, 0, 0)$. Counted from this γ, every star's longitude has grown by $p\,(t - t_0)$, as in the first description.

In this second description the motion is easier to picture, because the stars and the ecliptic stand still and only the equator moves. Figure 2 draws it.

Latitude stays, declination changes

The declinations change because the equator moves under the stars. For a star at $(\lambda, \beta)$, the conversion formula of page 1.2 gives

$$\sin\delta = \sin\beta\cos\varepsilon + \cos\beta\sin\varepsilon\sin\lambda.$$

With $\beta$ and ε fixed and $\lambda$ growing at the rate $p$, the derivative of both sides with respect to time is

$$\cos\delta\,\frac{d\delta}{dt} = p\,\sin\varepsilon\,\cos\beta\,\cos\lambda, \qquad \frac{d\delta}{dt} = \frac{p\,\sin\varepsilon\,\cos\beta\,\cos\lambda}{\cos\delta}.$$

A star near the spring equinox, where $\cos\lambda = 1$, moves north fastest, by $p\sin\varepsilon$ or 20.0″ a year for a star on the ecliptic. A star near the autumn equinox moves south as fast, and a star near a solstice hardly moves at all.

This is the pattern Ptolemy found when he compared the declinations of Timocharis, of Hipparchus and of his own time. The stars between the winter and the summer solstice, through the spring equinox, had moved north, the others south, and "the differences are greater for stars near the equinoctial points, and less for stars near the solstices" (VII 3).

Spica, near the autumn equinox, moved 1.5° south between Hipparchus and Ptolemy, as the formula predicts. Ptolemy found 1⅒° (1.1°), about what his own, slower rate of precession implies.

The circle of the pole

One turn of P around K takes $360^\circ/p$, about 25,770 years, and as P moves the pole star changes. Polaris is now 0.63° from the pole and will come closest in 2102.

Five thousand years ago, around 2800 BC, the pole lay within a tenth of a degree of Thuban, a faint star of Draco. In about 11,600 years it will pass near Vega, though never closer than 5.7°. Figure 3 draws the path of the pole among the stars of today's sky.

Two kinds of year

Precession also splits the year in two. The year of page 1.3, the tropical year $Y$, is the time the Sun takes to come back to γ; the sidereal year $Y_\text{s}$ is the time it takes to come back to the same star. Hipparchus was the first to notice that the second is longer, as Copernicus recalls (De Revolutionibus III 1).

To compare the two, start the count at a moment when the Sun is at γ and in line with some star. After one tropical year the Sun is back at γ, but γ has moved west by $pY$, the precession of that year, so the Sun is still $pY$ short of its star. It covers that last arc at its speed among the stars, $360^\circ$ in a sidereal year, which takes $pY \cdot Y_\text{s}/360^\circ$, so exactly

$$Y_\text{s} - Y = \frac{pY}{360^\circ}\,Y_\text{s}, \qquad \frac{Y_\text{s}}{Y} = \frac{360^\circ}{360^\circ - pY}.$$

The precession of one year is tiny, $pY = 0.013970^\circ$, so $Y_\text{s}$ and $Y$ differ by only one part in 25,770. Putting $Y$ for $Y_\text{s}$ on the right of the first formula therefore changes the result by that same small fraction, and gives a simpler formula, correct to first order in $pY/360^\circ$:

$$Y_\text{s} - Y \approx \frac{pY}{360^\circ}\,Y = \frac{pY}{n},$$

where $n = 360^\circ/Y$ is the Sun's daily motion of page 1.3: the extra time is the time the Sun takes to cover the precession of one year. It comes to 20.4 minutes, and the approximation changes it by only a twentieth of a second.

Ptolemy chose the tropical year, "in which the sun, starting from one solstice or equinox or any point on the ecliptic, returns to the same point again", as the most natural definition (III 1). Copernicus, following Thabit ibn Qurra (De Revolutionibus III 13), preferred the sidereal year, whose "precise uniformity ... is clearly linked with the sphere of the fixed stars", and found 365 days 6 h 9 min 40 s (De Revolutionibus III 14), 30 seconds longer than the real one.

3 · FitThe rate of precession

From two records

Ptolemy found the rate from Regulus, the bright star in Leo, by comparing his own longitude of it with Hipparchus', 265 years older. The star had moved 2°40′ (2.67°), and Ptolemy concludes: "From this we find that 1° rearward motion takes place in approximately 100 years" (VII 2). Exercise 1 repeats his computation.

Hipparchus had given only a lower bound, "not less than 1/100th of a degree" a year, as Ptolemy quotes him (VII 2). Ptolemy took the bound as the rate, 36″ a year, and built it into his star catalogue: to find a star's longitude at another date, add 1° for every century after the catalogue's epoch, AD 137 (VII 4).

From eighteen centuries of records

A rate from two records 265 years apart is only as good as the records, since an error of 1° in either of them changes it by 14″ a year. Ptolemy knew the remedy, and states it for the length of the year: "the longer the time between the observations compared, the greater the accuracy of the determination" (III 1). Spread over the eighteen centuries from Timocharis to Copernicus, the same error of 1° changes the rate by only 2″ a year.

So we fit a straight line through all six records of Spica in the table, in Figure 4. Its slope is 50.6″ a year, or 1° in 71.2 years.

From the reference sky

The model needs one rate, and we take it, as page 2.1 took the eccentric's parameters, from the reference sky around 2026. There γ moves along the ecliptic by $p = 50.29''$ a year, which is 1.397° a century, or 1° in 71.6 years. The obliquity keeps its value of 2026 from page 1.2.

Copernicus, with the same records and a few more, found a mean rate of 23°57′ in 1,717 Egyptian years, or 50.25″ a Julian year (De Revolutionibus III 6), only a quarter of an arcsecond faster than the real mean rate over the span of his records. He also concluded that the true rate was uneven (section 5).

The moving apogee

Page 2.1 estimated the motion of the Sun's apogee from a few years. To give the model a rate that holds over millennia, we fit the apogee by Hipparchus' method every 250 years over four thousand years of the reference sky, and a straight line through the apogees: the apogee moves east relative to γ by 1.741° a century.

Most of this, 1.397° a century, is the motion of γ itself, which moves west under the apogee as under every star. The remaining third of a degree a century is the apogee's own motion among the stars, a slow turn of the relative orbit of the Earth and the Sun, caused by the pull of the planets.

The eccentric of page 2.1 therefore gets one more parameter. Its apogee moves uniformly at the rate $\dot\lambda_A$ from its value at $t_0$, the spring equinox of 2026, where page 2.1 fitted it:

$$\lambda_A(t) = \lambda_A(t_0) + \dot\lambda_A\,(t - t_0), \qquad \lambda_A(t_0) = 103.732^\circ, \qquad \dot\lambda_A = 1.741^\circ \text{ a century}.$$

4 · TestUniform precession against the real sky

Two questions decide whether uniform precession is good enough: how long a constant rate keeps the stars in place, and whether the moving apogee stops the eccentric's drift. Figure 5 answers both.

Over the two thousand years around 2026, uniform precession places Spica within 2.6′ of its real longitude, and within 18′ over six thousand years. Away from 2026 the model falls behind the real Spica on both sides, because the real rate grows slowly with time (section 5).

With its apogee moving, the eccentric now stays within 6′ of the real Sun over the same two thousand years, where with the apogee fixed it drifts by more than half a degree.

It also repairs the equation of time of page 2.2, whose half-yearly and yearly waves meet at places set by the apogee. Over a thousand years the model's largest error falls from 81 s to 11 s.

Ptolemy's rate

Ptolemy's rate, 36″ a year, is far below the real 50″, most likely because of the way he measured the stars: from the Moon, whose place he computed from his own tables of the Sun and the Moon. A star measured that way inherits the error of the tables (Toomer, note to VII 2), and Figure 6 shows it on thirteen records of Spica, Regulus and β Scorpii.

Because his year is too long (page 2.1), his Sun falls a little further behind the real one every year, by about 15″, and the stars measured from it fall behind with it. This accounts for most of the gap between his 36″ and the real 50″. Whether it is the whole story is debated: Toomer also finds gross errors in Ptolemy's reductions of the older observations (notes to VII 3).

5 · ResidualWhat uniform precession leaves out

The real rate of precession grows slowly: it was 49.8″ a year in Hipparchus' time and is 50.3″ now. A uniform model fitted in 2026 therefore falls behind the real sky on either side of it, which is the slow curve of Figure 5 (a).

The model also keeps the obliquity fixed, while the real one decreases, today by 47″ a century. Ptolemy's value was already 11′ too large for his time, while those of al-Battani and Copernicus are close to the real ones.

Observer Year Obliquity ε Real ε
Ptolemy (Almagest I 12) AD 139 $23^\circ 51' 20''$ ($23.856^\circ$) $23.678^\circ$
al-Battani (De Rev. III 6) 880 $23^\circ 35'$ ($23.583^\circ$) $23.584^\circ$
Copernicus (De Rev. III 2) 1525 $23^\circ 28\tfrac12'$ ($23.475^\circ$) $23.501^\circ$

Copernicus took Ptolemy's large obliquity and slow precession for real changes of the sky. He explained them by two slow oscillations of the Earth's axis, one in the obliquity, with a period of 3,434 years, and one in the equinoxes, twice as fast (De Revolutionibus III 3, III 6). Exercise 3 shows where his uneven rate came from.

Even the latitudes change a little. Spica's has changed by 9′ since Timocharis, 7′ of them because the ecliptic itself turns slowly, pulled by the planets, the same turn that lowers the obliquity; the rest is the star's own motion.

None of these models says why the equator turns. The Earth bulges at the equator, and the Sun and the Moon pull on the bulge as if to bring the equator into the ecliptic. The spinning Earth answers by turning its axis slowly about the pole of the ecliptic, as the axis of a spinning top turns about the vertical. Newton gave the first explanation of this kind.

The Moon's pull also makes the pole wobble about its mean place, by up to 17″ in longitude and 9″ in obliquity, with the 18.6-year period of the Moon's nodes. This nutation is, with the aberration of light, the small difference between the apparent and the mean positions of the reference sky.

What is left of the Sun's error, about 6′ at the ends of the two thousand years, comes from what the model holds fixed: the eccentricity decreases slowly, and the mean motion itself changes. About 2′ of it is already there around 2026, the error of the circle itself found on page 2.1.

A uniform circle, an eccentric and a moving apogee now predict the Sun's longitude within a few arcminutes over two thousand years. The Moon is next, and it is much harder, because its speed along the ecliptic changes far more than the Sun's, and in a more complicated way.

Exercises

Hipparchus placed Regulus 29°50′ (29.83°) east of the summer solstice in 128 BC, and al-Battani 44°5′ (44.08°) east of it around AD 880 (De Revolutionibus III 2). Find the rate of precession from these two records, in arcseconds a year and in years per degree. Then find Ptolemy's rate from Hipparchus' record and his own, 32½° in AD 139, with his interval of 265 years, and compare both with the real sky. Check with Figure 6.

Both are distances from the summer solstice, so their difference is the motion. From 128 BC to AD 880 there are 1,007 years, since there is no year 0.

Regulus moved $44^\circ 5' - 29^\circ 50' = 14^\circ 15'$ ($14.25^\circ$) in 1,007 years, which is 50.9″ a year, or 1° in 70.7 years. The real Regulus moved 13.90° in the same interval, 49.7″ a year: the two records together are 0.35° too far apart.

From Hipparchus to Ptolemy it moved $2^\circ 40'$ ($2.67^\circ$) in 265 years, 36.2″ a year, where the real Regulus moved 3.67° in 266.1 years, 49.6″ a year. Ptolemy's record is 1.56° below the real longitude and Hipparchus' 0.56°, so the motion between them comes out a degree short. In Figure 6, Ptolemy's Regulus lies near the band of his Sun, Hipparchus' near zero.

From the tropical year of page 1.3, 365.24219 days, and $p = 50.29''$ a year, find the sidereal year. Then find the sidereal year implied by Ptolemy's tropical year, 365;14,48 days (365.24667 days, Almagest III 1), and his rate of 1° a century. Compare both of Ptolemy's years with the real ones, and explain why one is much better than the other.

Use $Y_\text{s} = Y \cdot 360^\circ/(360^\circ - pY)$, with $pY$ the precession of one tropical year in degrees. For the explanation, think of what Ptolemy actually compared: the stars with his Sun.

In a tropical year γ moves $pY = 0.013970^\circ$, so $Y_\text{s} = 365.24219 \times 360/(360 - 0.013970) = 365.25636$ days, or 365 d 6 h 9 min 10 s. It equals the period of the Earth's mean motion in the reference sky's own fixed frame, 365.25636 days.

For Ptolemy, $pY = 0.01^\circ$ and $Y_\text{s} = 365.24667 \times 360 / 359.99 = 365.25681$ days, or 365 d 6 h 9 min 49 s. His tropical year is 6.45 minutes too long, but his sidereal year only 39 seconds.

The Sun moves among the stars by its tropical motion less the precession, $n - p$, per year. Ptolemy's mean motion is slower than today's by 15.9″ a year, and his rate of precession by 14.3″, because he measured the stars from his Sun. Their difference, the Sun's motion among the stars, is wrong by only 1.6″ a year, which is the 39 seconds of his sidereal year: his two errors nearly cancel in it.

Copernicus found 1° of precession in 100 years from Timocharis to Ptolemy, 1° in 65 years from Ptolemy to al-Battani, and 1° in 71 years from al-Battani to his own time, and concluded that the rate changes (De Revolutionibus III 2). In the reference sky the equinox moved 5.98° from 294 BC to AD 139, 10.28° from 139 to 880 and 8.98° from 880 to 1525. Find the real rate in each interval, then use Figure 6 to explain the rates Copernicus found.

Divide each interval by the motion of the equinox. In Figure 6, look at the records at the ends of each interval.

The real rates are 1° in 432 / 5.98 = 72.2 years, 741 / 10.28 = 72.1 years and 645 / 8.98 = 71.8 years. The real rate grows slowly, but only by about half a percent over these eighteen centuries.

The records give other rates. Spica moved $4^\circ 20'$ ($4.33^\circ$) from Timocharis to Ptolemy's catalogue, 430 years, 1° in 99.3 years; Regulus $11^\circ 35'$ ($11.58^\circ$) from Ptolemy to al-Battani, 741 years, 1° in 64.0 years; β Scorpii $11^\circ 55'$ ($11.92^\circ$) from Menelaus to al-Battani, 782 years, 1° in 65.6 years.

Every record Ptolemy reduced carries his Sun's error, 0.3° to 0.7° too large in Timocharis' time and 0.8° to 1.6° too small in his own and Menelaus'. This shortens the motion up to Ptolemy and lengthens it after him. Al-Battani's and Copernicus' records are within a quarter of a degree, so the last interval is right, and the unevenness Copernicus found comes from the records of the first two.
