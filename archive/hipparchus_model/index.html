Hipparchus' Model

The astronomy of Hipparchus of Nicaea (c. 190 – c. 120 BCE), step by step: what he inherited, what he added, and what he left to Ptolemy. Drag the spheres to rotate them.

Who was Hipparchus, and how do we know?

Hipparchus was born in Nicaea (today İznik, Turkey) and observed mostly from Rhodes; his dated observations run from 162 to 127 BCE. Almost all of his writings are lost. The only one that survives is a commentary on Aratus' poem Phaenomena and on Eudoxus' description of the constellations. Everything else we know mainly through Ptolemy's Almagest (c. 150 CE), which quotes him, uses his observations and often keeps his models.

What was on the table around 150 BCE

  1. from 747 BCE Inherited · Babylonian Dated records of eclipses and planetary phenomena, kept in Babylon for centuries; arithmetic schemes that predict the Moon with periods known to great precision; sexagesimal numbers and the zodiac of twelve 30° signs.
  2. c. 370 BCE Inherited · Greek Eudoxus of Cnidus: nested spheres turning uniformly about the Earth (the simulation below), and a description of the constellations on a celestial globe.
  3. c. 350 BCE Inherited · Greek Aristotle: a spherical Earth at rest at the center of a spherical cosmos; the heavens made of a substance whose natural motion is circular and eternal.
  4. c. 330 BCE Inherited · Greek Callippus adds spheres to Eudoxus' system, among other things to make the seasons unequal; Meton and Euctemon (432 BCE) and Callippus give season lengths and calendar cycles.
  5. c. 290 BCE Inherited · Greek Timocharis and Aristyllus measure star positions in Alexandria; Aristarchus estimates the relative distances of Sun and Moon.
  6. c. 200 BCE Inherited · Greek Apollonius of Perge: the geometry of eccentric circles and epicycles, and their equivalence.

The geocentric frame

Inherited · Greek (Aristotle)

Hipparchus never questions the frame he received: a spherical Earth at rest at the center, and a sphere of fixed stars turning once a day around it. The Sun, the Moon and the five planets drift slowly eastward against the stars, close to the ecliptic. Aristarchus had proposed a moving Earth a century earlier, but Hipparchus, like almost everyone, ignored it.

Uniform circular motion: the homocentric spheres

Inherited · Greek (Eudoxus)

Every motion in the heavens must be built from uniform rotations of circles or spheres. Eudoxus gave each planet four spheres, all centered on the Earth. Sphere 2 carries the planet along the zodiac in its sidereal period. Spheres 3 and 4 turn in opposite directions, in the synodic period, about axes tilted by i; together they move the planet on a figure-eight, the hippopede, around its mean position. When the backward speed on the figure-eight beats the forward speed of sphere 2, the planet goes retrograde.

The half-length of the figure-eight equals the tilt i, while its height in latitude is only a fraction of a degree for small tilts.

What was left unsolved

Inherited · Greek Hipparchus

  • Distances never change. Every body stays on a sphere centered on the Earth, so it cannot look brighter or larger at one time than at another. Mars and Venus obviously do, and the Moon's apparent size varies. Simplicius reports that Autolycus of Pitane had already raised the objection.
  • The seasons. The four seasons have different lengths. Callippus added spheres to reproduce this, but only qualitatively.
  • No numbers. The spheres were not fitted to dated observations, so they could not predict where a body would be on a given day. The Babylonians could predict, with arithmetic tables, but they had no geometric picture.
Hipparchus' program was to join the two traditions: geometric models built from uniform circles, with parameters derived from dated observations. Try the Mars presets: with the true synodic period of 780 days, even a tilt of 60° gives no retrograde motion, and it already throws the planet some 15° off the ecliptic.

Sources: Aristotle, Metaphysics Λ 8; Simplicius, commentary on De caelo; Almagest I 3–8 (Toomer); O. Neugebauer, A History of Ancient Mathematical Astronomy (1975).

The celestial sphere

Inherited · Greek (Eudoxus, Aratus)

The stars are treated as fixed on one sphere, with the Earth a point at its center. Eudoxus had described the constellations as drawn on a globe, and Aratus had put the description into verse. Hipparchus' surviving commentary checks them against the sky and corrects them, giving risings, settings and culminations with numbers.

A catalogue with coordinates

Hipparchus Debated

Hipparchus was the first to list stars with numerical positions, so that later astronomers could tell whether anything had moved. Pliny says he was prompted by a new star. Ptolemy refers to his work on the stars several times, and modern estimates give it about 850 stars. Which coordinates did he use? The commentary on Aratus mixes declinations and positions measured along the equator; a fragment of his catalogue found in 2022 under a later text (the Codex Climaci Rescriptus) gives stars of Corona Borealis in equatorial coordinates. Ptolemy's own catalogue of 1022 stars uses ecliptic coordinates, λ and β. Switch the grid to compare the two systems.

Magnitudes

Probably Hipparchus Later · Pogson (1856)

Stars are sorted into six classes: the brightest are "of the first magnitude", the faintest visible are "of the sixth". The scale is first attested in Ptolemy's catalogue, and is usually credited to Hipparchus.

The eye responds roughly to ratios of brightness, so equal steps in class are roughly equal ratios of light. N. Pogson fixed the modern definition: five classes are exactly a factor of 100.

The old records

Inherited · Greek (Timocharis, Aristyllus) Inherited · Babylonian

About 160 years before Hipparchus, Timocharis had observed the Moon covering Spica and other stars in Alexandria. Hipparchus also had older solstice observations (Meton, 432 BCE; Aristarchus, 280 BCE) and Babylonian eclipse records, which he used for the length of the year.

The discovery

Hipparchus

From lunar eclipses, which give the Moon's position and hence that of a nearby star relative to the Sun, Hipparchus found that Spica was about 6° ahead of the autumnal equinox in his own time, and about 8° in Timocharis' time. The stars keep their distances from one another, so it is the equinoxes that move, backwards along the ecliptic: he called it the "displacement of the solstitial and equinoctial points". Ptolemy quotes his conclusion: the motion is "not less than 1/100 of a degree" per year (Almagest VII 2).

Spica's latitude had stayed at about 2° south of the ecliptic, while its declination had changed. Hence the turning happens about the poles of the ecliptic, not of the equator (Almagest VII 3). Hipparchus remained cautious: Timocharis' observations were crude, and the interval short.

Two kinds of year

Hipparchus

Because the equinoxes move, the year of the seasons (from equinox to equinox) is shorter than the year measured against the stars. Hipparchus defined the year by the seasons, and found it shorter than 365¼ days by about 1/300 of a day, by comparing his solstices with those of Meton and Aristarchus (Almagest III 1).

Afterwards

Later · Ptolemy, Newton

Ptolemy compared his own positions with Hipparchus' and adopted exactly 1° per century, too small by almost a third (see his Regulus on the chart). A full turn takes about 25 800 years. Newton explained it: the Sun and the Moon pull on the Earth's equatorial bulge, and the Earth's axis turns like a spinning top.

Sources: Almagest III 1, VII 1–3 (Toomer).

The problem: unequal seasons

Inherited · Greek (Meton, Euctemon, Callippus)

The equinoxes and solstices are 90° apart on the ecliptic, yet the Sun takes different times to go from one to the next. The older Greek astronomers had already measured unequal seasons. Hipparchus timed equinoxes and solstices himself and took spring as 94½ days and summer as 92½ days.

The tool: an eccentric circle

Inherited · Greek (Apollonius)

Keep the motion uniform and circular, but put the Earth off the center of the circle. Seen from the center Z, the Sun moves at a constant rate; seen from the Earth, it is slower near the apogee (the far point) and faster near the perigee.

The numbers: eccentricity from the season lengths

Hipparchus

Ptolemy says that Hipparchus solved the problem "with great care" using the two season lengths as his only data (Almagest III 4), and gives the solution. Let n be the Sun's mean motion and T₁, T₂ the lengths of spring and summer. Spring plus summer go from equinox to equinox: a straight line through the Earth, which cuts the circle at a distance Zy from its center.

The apogee lies at Gemini 5½°, 24½° before the summer solstice. Then the model predicts the other two seasons, and the Sun's true longitude on any day:

Afterwards

Later · Ptolemy, Kepler

Ptolemy found the same season lengths in his own time and kept Hipparchus' model unchanged, concluding that the apogee does not move. It does: the "Today" preset puts it near 103°. In Kepler's ellipse the Sun sits at a focus; the eccentric circle imitates it with an offset of twice the orbital eccentricity (2 × 0.0167 ≈ 1/30 today).

Sources: Almagest III 1, III 3, III 4 (Toomer); A. Jones, J. Hist. Astron. 22 (1991).

The geometry

Inherited · Greek (Apollonius of Perge, c. 200 BCE)

An epicycle is a small circle whose center C moves uniformly on a larger circle, the deferent, centered on the Earth; the body moves uniformly on the epicycle. Apollonius studied both this construction and the eccentric circle, and proved where a body on an epicycle appears to stand still before going backward (quoted by Ptolemy, Almagest XII 1).

This is the tool needed for the planets. Hipparchus, according to Ptolemy, "did not even make a beginning" of a planetary theory: he collected the planetary observations and showed that the models of his time did not fit them (Almagest IX 2). Each planet has two anomalies, one tied to the Sun and one tied to the zodiac, and the loops change size around the sky: a single epicycle cannot do both.

The periods

Inherited · Babylonian Checked by Hipparchus

The Moon's speed varies along its orbit, and the fast point itself drifts around the zodiac: the Moon returns to the same speed (an anomalistic month, 27.55 days) a little later than it returns to the same star (27.32 days). Ptolemy writes as if Hipparchus derived the periods from eclipses; in fact they are Babylonian, and Hipparchus confirmed them by comparing eclipses of his own time with Babylonian ones some 345 years older (Toomer's note to Almagest IV 2).

Modern values: 29.530589, 27.554550 and 27.212221 days. The draconitic month brings the Moon back to the same node, and so governs eclipses.

Eclipses fix the size of the epicycle

Inherited · Babylonian (records) Hipparchus (method)

At the middle of a lunar eclipse the Moon is exactly opposite the Sun, and the solar model gives the Sun's longitude: so the Moon's true position is known, without any instrument and without parallax. Three eclipses give three true positions at known times; the mean motions give the corresponding mean positions; the three differences fix the radius of the epicycle and the Moon's place on it. Hipparchus used one triple observed in Babylon (383–382 BCE) and one in Alexandria (around 200 BCE).

He got two different answers: 6;15 with the eccentre and 4;46 with the epicycle (Almagest IV 11). Some concluded that the two hypotheses were not equivalent. Ptolemy showed that they are, and blamed errors in Hipparchus' time intervals; his own value is 5;15.

Afterwards

Later · Ptolemy

Eclipses only test the model at full Moon. Away from the syzygies the Moon can stray more than 2° from it: this "second anomaly" (evection) was established by Ptolemy, partly with observations of Hipparchus himself (Almagest V 3), and required moving the center of the deferent.

Sources: Almagest IV 2–11, V 3 (Toomer); Toomer, "Hipparchus' empirical basis for his lunar mean motions", Centaurus 24 (1980).

Trigonometry before trigonometry

Inherited · Babylonian (sexagesimal numbers, 360°) Inherited · Greek (Euclid, Archimedes) Hipparchus Debated (reconstruction)

Every model on this page turns an angle into a length or back (the eccentricity of the Sun, the epicycle of the Moon). The Greeks did this with the chord of an arc, the straight line joining its ends. Theon of Alexandria says Hipparchus wrote a work on chords in twelve books; it is the first table of the kind we hear of, and it is lost. G. J. Toomer reconstructed it from a few results computed with it: steps of 7½°, and a radius of 3438 units, so that the radius is measured in minutes of arc (21 600′ / 2π). It could be built with plane geometry alone: the chord of the supplement, and of half an arc, from a known chord.

Ptolemy's table (Almagest I 11) has steps of ½° on a radius of 60; Indian astronomers later tabulated half-chords, our sines, with the same radius 3438.

In a lunar eclipse the Moon crosses the Earth's shadow. The width of the shadow at the Moon, in Moon radii (k), links the two parallaxes: πM + πS = θM(1 + k), taking the Sun as large in the sky as the Moon.

The eclipse diagram

How far are the Moon and the Sun?

Inherited · Greek (Aristarchus) Hipparchus Debated (known through Pappus)

Aristarchus had estimated that the Sun is 18 to 20 times farther than the Moon. Hipparchus' book On sizes and distances is lost; Pappus' commentary on the Almagest summarises it. In book 1 he used a solar eclipse (now identified as 14 March 190 BCE) that was total near the Hellespont and covered four fifths of the Sun in Alexandria: the difference is a parallax of the Moon. Assuming the Sun too far to have any parallax, he found the Moon between 71 and 83 Earth radii away. In book 2 he gave the Sun the smallest parallax one could hope to notice (about 7′, i.e. 490 Earth radii) and found the Moon at least 62 and on average 67⅓ Earth radii away.

The eclipse diagram above shows why he could not do better. The two parallaxes add up to a fixed quantity, and the Sun's share is a tiny difference of two nearly equal numbers: one Earth radius more or less for the Moon changes the Sun's distance enormously. With Hipparchus' assumptions (the Moon 1/650 of its circle, the shadow 2½ Moons wide, Almagest IV 9) a Moon at 67⅓ gives a Sun near 490 Earth radii, as in his book 2. Ptolemy, with slightly different numbers, found 1210 (Almagest V 15).

Modern values: the Moon at about 60 Earth radii, the Sun at about 23 500. Sources: Almagest IV 9, V 11–16 (Toomer, with notes); G. J. Toomer, "Hipparchus on the distances of the sun and moon", Archive for History of Exact Sciences 14 (1974).

What Hipparchus left undone

Hipparchus Later · Ptolemy

Hipparchus had models with numbers for the Sun and for the Moon at syzygy, a star catalogue, precession, a trigonometry and the distances of the Moon. For the five planets he only collected observations and showed that the existing models failed. Ptolemy, three centuries later, built on all of this: he kept the Sun, corrected the Moon, extended the catalogue to 1022 stars, and built the planetary theory with the epicycle and the equant.
