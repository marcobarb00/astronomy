    Where do Mercury and Venus go?

    Everything beyond the Sun is easy to rank: the slower a planet crosses the zodiac, the farther away it is. Mercury and Venus break the rule, because they never leave the Sun's side and take one year to go round the sky, like the Sun itself. Ancient and medieval astronomers answered the question in several ways.

    Distances in Ptolemy's system were only chosen to keep the spheres nested. Copernicus could compute them, in units of the Earth–Sun distance, from the planets' observed loops.

    What Ptolemy inherited, and what he added

    The Almagest (c. 150 CE) is a synthesis. The Sun comes from Hipparchus, the epicycle from Apollonius and Hipparchus, and the equant, as far as we can tell, from Ptolemy himself. Each card below states what the idea explains, how Ptolemy justified it, which loose ends it leaves, and where to read more.

    Roman numerals are Almagest books. Ptolemy often gives results without showing how he found them, so "how he justified it" is partly the reconstruction of historians.

    Stations, oppositions and conjunctions

    One motion, two descriptions

    Put the Sun S at the origin, the Earth at E and the planet at P. What we see from the Earth is only the direction of the vector P − E, i.e. the planet's geocentric longitude λ. Split this vector in two:

    Both pieces are vectors of fixed length turning at a uniform rate, so their sum is exactly a deferent-epicycle model. Vector addition does not care about the order, so either piece can play the deferent: by convention the deferent is the longer one.

    • Superior planet (Mars, Jupiter, Saturn): the deferent is the planet's heliocentric orbit and the epicycle is the Earth's orbit. The epicycle radius is the Earth→Sun vector, so it always points towards the Sun.
    • Inferior planet (Mercury, Venus): the deferent is the Earth's orbit, so the guide point is the mean Sun, and the epicycle is the planet's own heliocentric orbit. The planet never strays more than sin⁻¹(r/R) from the Sun.

    Conjunction, opposition and the middle of the loop

    Let λ☉ be the Sun's geocentric longitude. For a superior planet, opposition (λ = λ☉ + 180°) means Earth, Sun and planet in a line with the Earth in the middle. On the epicycle this is the perigee: the planet is closest, brightest, and in the middle of its retrograde loop. Conjunction (λ = λ☉) is the apogee: the planet is behind the Sun and moves fastest forwards. For an inferior planet the perigee is the inferior conjunction and the apogee the superior conjunction; the greatest elongations are where the line of sight is tangent to the epicycle.

    Ptolemy's ratios are Copernicus's distances

    Ptolemy could only fix the ratio r/R from the size of the retrograde loops. His values (deferent radius 60 parts) match the heliocentric distances almost exactly: for a superior planet r/R = 1/a, for an inferior one r/R = a, with a the planet's distance from the Sun in astronomical units.

    This is the simplest model: circular, coplanar orbits traversed uniformly. The real orbits are slightly eccentric (Ptolemy needed the eccentric deferent and the equant for that), so real retrograde arcs vary from one opposition to the next around the values computed here.
