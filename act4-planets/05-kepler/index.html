Page 4.2's equant, moved into Copernicus' arrangement and Tycho's system on pages 4.3 and 4.4, gives Mars directions whose error reaches 0.88° near the oppositions. Turning the epicycle with the real Sun instead of the mean Sun brought the error down to 32′, and left the part that comes from the shape of Mars' path. Kepler, working on Tycho's observations of Mars, found that shape: an ellipse.

1 · ObservationThe Sun's speed and Mars' distances

Every model so far was judged by the directions it predicts. The two observations of this section are about distances: how far the Sun is from us through the year, and how far Mars is from the Sun around its path. Both can be measured without any model of the motion.

The Sun's speed and its apparent size

The Sun's apparent diameter measures its distance, since the two are inversely proportional. To see how its speed depends on its distance we set the two side by side in Figure 1, each in percent of its mean over 2025: the fastest daily motion exceeds the slowest by 7.0%, the largest diameter the smallest by only 3.4%.

The speed varies as the square of the diameter, so as the inverse square of the distance $r$: the product $r^2$ times the angular speed stays constant through the year within 0.10%, while $r$ times the angular speed varies by 3.5%. Page 2.1's eccentric, a uniform motion on a circle seen from a point off its center, gives a speed that varies as $1/r$ to first order. To match the Sun's speed it needs a distance that varies twice as much as the real one: this is why its distances, and those of page 3.2's Moon, varied twice too much.

Mars triangulated

The distance of Mars from the Sun cannot be read from its size by eye, but it can be found by triangulation. After one sidereal period, 686.98 days, Mars is back at the same point of its path among the stars, while the Earth is at another place on its own. The two lines of sight, one from each place, meet at that point. The Earth's two places come from the Sun's direction and its distance, which its apparent diameter gives.

From 2010 to 2025 a first sighting every ten days, with its partner one period later, gives 103 points. Pairs whose lines meet at less than 20° are left out, since a small error in either direction would move such a point far along the lines. The distances found agree with the reference within 0.0010 au, close enough to test the shape of the path. In Figure 2 we compare them with a circle: its diameter lies on the line of the apsides and reaches from the nearest distance to the farthest.

Near the apsides the points lie on the circle, and between them they fall inside it, by up to 0.0064 au, six times the scatter of the points. Mars' path is narrower than a circle across its long axis.

2 · ModelKepler's ellipse and the area law

The ellipse

An ellipse is the set of points whose distances from two fixed points, its foci, add up to the same length $2a$. Its center C lies halfway between them, $a$ is the semi-major axis, and the foci are at the distance $ea$ from C, with $e$ the eccentricity. Kepler put the Sun ☉ at one focus. We call the other focus Q, and the ends of the long axis the aphelion A, the point farthest from the Sun, and the perihelion Π.

The circle on the long axis, of center C and radius $a$, squeezed towards the axis in the ratio $b/a$, gives the ellipse, where $b = a \sqrt{1 - e^2}$ is the semi-minor axis. The point P′ of the circle at the angle $E$ from the aphelion, seen from C, goes to the point P of the ellipse straight below or above it. $E$ is the eccentric anomaly (not page 2.2's equation of time). Measured from the Sun, with $x$ along the axis towards A, P is at

$$\bigl(a(e + \cos E),\ b\sin E\bigr), \qquad r = {\odot}\text{P} = a(1 + e\cos E).$$

The distance follows from the sum of the squares, since $b^2 = a^2(1 - e^2)$. The squeeze is largest across the long axis, at $E = 90^\circ$, where the ellipse lies inside its circle by $a - b \approx ae^2/2$. The circle through the apsides of Figure 2 is this circle, so the points falling inside it there are this squeeze: with the values of Mars found in section 3, $a - b$ is 0.0067 au.

Equal areas in equal times

Kepler's second rule fixes how fast the body goes round: the line from the Sun to the body sweeps equal areas in equal times. The area swept between two moments is about $\tfrac12 r^2$ times the angle turned, so the angular speed seen from the Sun varies as $1/r^2$, which is what the Sun showed in Figure 1.

To place the body on a given date we need the area swept since the aphelion as a function of $E$. In the circle, the region swept from A to P′ about the focus ☉ is the sector A C P′ plus the triangle C ☉ P′, whose base C☉ = $ea$ has the height $a\sin E$:

$$\text{area}_\text{circle} = \tfrac12 a^2 E + \tfrac12\,ea \cdot a\sin E = \tfrac12 a^2 (E + e\sin E),$$

with $E$ in radians. Squeezing towards the axis multiplies every area by $b/a$, so the area swept in the ellipse is $\tfrac12 ab(E + e\sin E)$, and the whole ellipse has the area $\pi ab$. By the area law the fraction of the period $P$ elapsed since the aphelion, at the time $t_A$, is the fraction of the area swept:

$$\frac{t - t_A}{P} = \frac{\tfrac12 ab\,(E + e\sin E)}{\pi ab} = \frac{E + e\sin E}{2\pi}.$$

The left side, times $2\pi$, is the angle that a point turning at a constant rate, once a period, would have covered since the aphelion: $M = 2\pi(t - t_A)/P$, or $n(t - t_A)$ in degrees with $n$ the mean motion. It is the mean anomaly of every page since 2.1. There a mean Sun turned uniformly, at the mean longitude $\bar\lambda = \lambda_A + M$, and the real Sun was ahead of it or behind it by the equation of center $q$. Here too $M$ is the time since the aphelion written as an angle, the place of a uniformly moving body, and the real body is at $M + q$. The two meet at A and at Π. Equating the two sides gives Kepler's equation:

$$M = E + e\sin E.$$

Given the date we know $M$, and we need $E$. The equation cannot be solved for $E$ in closed form, but iteration solves it. Written as $E = M - e\sin E$, it says that $E$ is $M$ less a small correction. Starting from $E = M$ and putting each value back into the right side gives the steps $E \leftarrow M - e\sin E$. Newton's method looks instead for the root of $f(E) = E + e\sin E - M$, whose derivative is $f'(E) = 1 + e\cos E$, and each step $E \leftarrow E - f(E)/f'(E)$ is

$$E \leftarrow E - \frac{E + e\sin E - M}{1 + e\cos E}.$$

Exercise 1 compares the two. With $E$ known, P is placed by the coordinates above, and its direction from the Sun, $M + q$, is the true anomaly. In Figure 3 you can follow the body across areas swept in equal times.

The ellipse and the equant

The ellipse keeps more of Ptolemy's model than its shape suggests. Expanded in $e$ (page 4.2, Exercise 1), it gives $q \approx -2e\sin M + \tfrac54 e^2\sin 2M$ and $r \approx a(1 + e\cos M + e^2\sin^2 M)$, and page 4.2's equant with the same $e$ has the same first-order terms. Seen from the empty focus Q, the point of the ellipse turns uniformly to first order, as K turns about the equant. In Figure 3, for Mars, the direction of P seen from Q stays within 8.2′ of $M$, and seen from ☉ within 10.7°. Kepler, who had first given up Ptolemy's equal division of the eccentricity, restored it as the two foci of the ellipse (HAMA p. 172).

Page 2.1's eccentric needs $2e$ for the same directions, so its distance varies twice as much as the ellipse's. The Sun's speed in Figure 1 told the two apart: on the ellipse the distance changes by the factor $(1 + e)/(1 - e)$ between perihelion and aphelion, and the angular speed by its square.

The third law

Copernicus' arrangement gave each orbit a size relative to the Earth's, measured from its elongations or its loops (page 4.3), but no rule linked that size to anything else. Kepler found one that ties it to the period $P$ (page 4.3's symbol): the square of the period is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis. In years and astronomical units,

$$P^2 = a^3.$$

The periods in the law are sidereal, measured against the stars. In the table, page 4.3's periods about the Sun are made sidereal with the precession and set beside its distances. For Venus, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn the distance agrees within 0.3% with the one the law gives, so $P^2/a^3$ is 1 within 1%. Mercury's distance is 1.9% short, the error page 4.3 found for its elongated path.

Mars seen from the Earth

In Kepler's model every planet and the Earth move on their own ellipses about the Sun. Seen from the Earth, the Sun is at the ecliptic longitude $\lambda_\odot$ and the distance $R$ (not the deferent's radius), the Earth's ellipse described from the Earth, and Mars at its heliocentric ecliptic longitude $\lambda_\text{h}$ and the distance $r$ from the Sun. Leaving out the small tilt of Mars' plane to the ecliptic, the planet's direction from the Earth is that of

$$\bigl(r\cos\lambda_\text{h} + R\cos\lambda_\odot,\ \ r\sin\lambda_\text{h} + R\sin\lambda_\odot\bigr).$$

This is page 4.2's residual model, the epicycle turned by the real Sun, with the equant's circle replaced by Mars' ellipse and the epicycle by the Earth's.

3 · FitThe Earth's ellipse and Mars'

Each ellipse has five parameters: the mean motion $n$, the mean ecliptic longitude $L_0$ at J2000.0, the ecliptic longitude of the aphelion $\lambda_A$, the eccentricity $e$ and the semi-major axis $a$. The Earth's comes first, since every sighting of Mars is made from it. Seen from the Earth it is the Sun's ellipse, its apogee opposite the Earth's aphelion, and we fit it in that form, as page 2.1 fitted the eccentric.

The Sun's ellipse from the seasons

Page 2.1 fitted the eccentric to the lengths of spring and summer, Hipparchus' method, and the same two lengths fix the ellipse. Only the law of the motion changes.

The equinoxes and solstices are positions of the real Sun, at the ecliptic longitudes 0°, 90° and 180°, and what we observe is the moments it reaches them. In the model the Sun's position depends on the time only through $M$, which grows uniformly, $M = n(t - t_A)$. So to find when the model's Sun reaches a given ecliptic longitude we go back from that position to $M$, and the days between two positions are the difference of their values of $M$, divided by $n$. In 2026, for instance, the fitted ellipse gives 91.404° of $M$ over spring, the observed 92.735 days at $n$ = 0.98565° a day. Going back takes three steps: the ecliptic longitude gives the true anomaly $v = \lambda - \lambda_A$, then $v$ gives $E$, and Kepler's equation gives $M$.

The first step uses the place of the body found in section 2. Seen from the focus it is at $a(e + \cos E,\ \sqrt{1 - e^2}\sin E)$, at the distance $a(1 + e\cos E)$, so $\cos v = (e + \cos E)/(1 + e\cos E)$. Putting this into the half-angle formula $\tan^2(v/2) = (1 - \cos v)/(1 + \cos v)$, the denominators cancel and

$$\tan^2\frac{v}{2} = \frac{(1 - e)(1 - \cos E)}{(1 + e)(1 + \cos E)} = \frac{1 - e}{1 + e}\,\tan^2\frac{E}{2}, \qquad \tan\frac{E}{2} = \sqrt{\frac{1 + e}{1 - e}}\,\tan\frac{v}{2}.$$

Of the two roots we take the one with the sign of $\tan(v/2)$, since $E$ and $v$ always lie on the same side of the long axis. Kepler's equation then gives $M = E + e\sin E$. For a trial $e$ and apogee, the three ecliptic longitudes give three values of $M$, and so the lengths of the two seasons. Newton's method adjusts $e$ and the apogee until these match the observed ones, in a few steps.

With the seasons of 2026 and page 1.3's mean motion, the ellipse has $e$ = 0.016655, where page 2.1's eccentric needed twice as much, and its apogee at the ecliptic longitude 103.38°. The seasons of any other year of 2016–2025 change $e$ by less than 1%. The mean ecliptic longitude follows from the moment of the spring equinox, the apogee moves at page 2.3's rate, and $a$ = 1 au by definition.

Mars from its oppositions to the real Sun

At an opposition to the real Sun the Earth lies between the Sun and Mars, so the planet's geocentric ecliptic longitude is its heliocentric one, whatever the two distances. The eight oppositions of 2010–2025 therefore give eight heliocentric directions of Mars at known moments, as the oppositions to the mean Sun gave the directions of K on page 4.2.

We keep page 4.1's mean motion, fitted to oppositions 79 years apart on the earlier reference sky (as the Sun's mean motion and its apogee's rate come from pages 1.3 and 2.3), and fit the other three parameters by least squares, with the aphelion fixed among the stars as on page 4.2. The ellipse passes within 0.75′ of the eight oppositions, with $e$ = 0.0934. Page 4.2's equant, refitted with the real Sun, had found 0.0935, as its first order agreement with the ellipse requires.

For the size we use the third law. Page 4.1's mean motion, less the precession, is one turn among the stars in 686.981 days, 1.88082 sidereal years, so $a$ = 1.5237 au, where page 4.3 found 1.5197 au from the loops. No observation of distance enters this fit, so every distance the model gives is a prediction.

HistoryKepler's Mars

Kepler joined Tycho in 1600 and was set to work on the theory of Mars. He was the first to refer all the observations to the true Sun instead of the mean Sun (HAMA p. 171).

From the observations of Mars he also worked out the Earth's motion, and found that the Earth, too, moved faster near the Sun (Voelkel ch. 3). He gave its circle an equant, as Ptolemy had for the planets (HAMA p. 155), and so halved the eccentricity of the Sun's eccentric (Fitzpatrick § 1.4).

For Mars he first fitted a circle with an equant to the oppositions, the two points placed freely. It served the ecliptic longitudes but not the distances, and for a time he used one model for the longitudes and another for the distances (HAMA pp. 98, 172). With the eccentricity halved, as the distances required, the longitudes erred by up to about 8′ (Fitzpatrick § 1.4).

Taking the speed as inversely proportional to the distance, so that the sum of the distances measures the time, and taking that sum as an area, he came to the area law. On an eccentric circle it kept Mars too long on the sides of its orbit (Exercise 3 computes by how much), so the orbit had to be squeezed in there, "as though you held a fat-bellied sausage and squeezed it in the middle", in Voelkel's words. A first ellipse erred by almost exactly the opposite amount, and the one in between had the Sun at a focus (Voelkel ch. 3). The Astronomia Nova, which set out the ellipse and the area law, appeared in 1609.

The third law came last, on 15 May 1618, as Kepler finished the Harmonices Mundi, from periods known for centuries and distances derived from Tycho's observations (Voelkel ch. 4), the numbers of Exercise 2.

4 · TestThe ellipses against the real Mars and Sun

To see how much of the equant's error the ellipses remove, Figure 4 gives the error curve of the two ellipses fitted to 2010–2025, with page 4.2's equant as a ghost, both against VSOP87's Mars.

Over 2010–2025 the ellipses stay within 5.7′ of the real Mars, where page 4.2's equant is off by up to 0.81° on this reference. Over the two centuries 1926–2126 the error grows to 18′, the subject of the residual.

The distances are the stronger test, since none entered the fit. Mars' distance from the Earth stays within 0.0019 au of the real one over 2010–2025, ten times closer than page 4.2's equant on page 4.3's scale (0.0205 au), and the triangulated points of Figure 2 lie within 0.0010 au of the ellipse.

The same test for the Sun, in Figure 5, sets the ellipse against page 2.1's eccentric, its apogee moving at page 2.3's rate. In 2026 the ellipse stays within a quarter of an arcminute of the real Sun, where the eccentric is off by 1.74′. Over the five centuries after 2026 the eccentric's error grows faster than the ellipse's. In the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, though, the ellipse's error is slightly the larger, at most 2.7′ against 2.4′.

The Sun's distance tests what pages 2.1 and 3.2 left open. In 2026 the real Sun's greatest distance was 1.0339 times its least. The ellipse gives the same ratio, within 0.0001 au at either end, and page 2.1's eccentric 1.0693.

HistoryThe Rudolphine Tables

Kepler's models reached other astronomers through the Rudolphine Tables, printed in 1627, from which anyone could compute a planet's place for any date. They were some fifty times more accurate than Ptolemy's and Copernicus' (Voelkel ch. 6).

From them Kepler predicted that Mercury would cross the face of the Sun on 7 November 1631. He died before the day, and the transit came within six hours of his time. "I have found him, I have seen him where no one has ever seen him before!", wrote Gassendi in an open letter from France (Voelkel ch. 7).

5 · ResidualWhat the ellipses leave

What is left of Mars' error, a few arcminutes, comes from the planets' pulls on one another, which Newton's gravitation explains and Kepler's model leaves out. Some of it is periodic. The rest grows with time, because each ellipse slowly turns and changes. Fitted to the oppositions of 1931–1946 as well as 2010–2025, Mars' aphelion turns 0.43° a century among the stars. With that turn, and the mean motion fitted again to the same oppositions on this page's reference, the error over 1926–2126 falls from 18′ to 8.4′.

The model also leaves out the tilt of Mars' plane, 1.85° to the ecliptic, which changes the heliocentric ecliptic longitude by at most 0.9′.

Positions are computed today by integrating Newton's equations (JPL's ephemerides) or from series such as VSOP87, which start from Kepler's ellipses and add the planets' pulls as further terms.

About half of the Sun's remaining error comes from the Moon. Each month the Earth goes round the center of mass of the Earth and the Moon, which moves the Sun seen from the Earth by up to 6.9″ either way, the wobble of Figure 5. Averaged over a month, the error stays under 0.13′. Part of that is the nutation, which the reference includes and the model leaves out, and which grows in the tests over centuries.

The Moon after Kepler

An ellipse is also the first term of the Moon's motion, the 6.29° of page 3.1. The Sun, though, pulls on the Moon far more than the planets pull on one another, and the ellipse leaves large terms. The two largest are the evection of page 3.2, 1.27°, and the variation, 0.66°. Smaller ones follow, and the perigee and the nodes turn round, in 8.85 and 18.61 years. All of them come from the Sun's pull, which is Newton's subject, not Kepler's.

Exercises

Solve Kepler's equation for Mars, $e$ = 0.0934, at $M = 30^\circ$, first by repeating $E \leftarrow M - e\sin E$, then by Newton's method, both from $E = M$, and count the steps until $E$ changes by less than $10^{-8}$ degrees. Find the true anomaly $M + q$ and $r$. Then do the same for the view $e$ = 0.6 of Figure 3 at $M = 90^\circ$, checking $E$ in the read-out, and for Halley's comet, $e$ = 0.967, at $M = 30^\circ$. Why does the first method slow down?

Keep $e\sin E$ in radians, multiplied by $180^\circ/\pi$, when $E$ is in degrees. For the first method, compare the error before and after a step: $E_{k+1} - E = -e(\sin E_k - \sin E)$.

For Mars, $E$ = 27.526769°: the first method takes 9 steps (27.3243°, 27.5436°, 27.5254°, 27.5269°, …), Newton's 3 (27.52452063°, 27.52676909°, 27.52676909°). The point is at $(e + \cos E,\ \sqrt{1 - e^2}\sin E)$ = (0.9802, 0.4601) in units of $a$, so $M + q$ = 25.1472°, $q$ = −4.8528°, and $r = 1 + e\cos E$ = 1.08283 $a$.

For $e$ = 0.6 at $M = 90^\circ$, $E$ = 60.175677°: 19 steps against Newton's 5. For Halley's comet, $E$ = 15.341451°: the first method jumps between 2.30°, 27.78°, 4.18°, 25.96°, … and takes 312 steps, Newton's method 4.

Near the root, $E_{k+1} - E \approx -e\cos E\,(E_k - E)$: each step multiplies the error by $e|\cos E|$, 0.083 for Mars, 0.298 for $e$ = 0.6 and 0.933 for the comet. Newton's method squares the error at each step, once it is small: for Mars the errors are $2.2 \times 10^{-3}$ degrees, then $1.8 \times 10^{-9}$.

Then find Mars' distance from page 4.1's sidereal period of 686.981 days and the sidereal year of 365.2564 days, and compare it with the fit.

Divide the periods by 365 and the distances by 1000 first.

$P/a$ runs from 0.621 (Mercury) to 3.100 (Saturn): the period grows faster than the distance. $P^2/a^3$ is 0.995, 1.001, 1.000, 1.001, 1.002 and 1.010 from Mercury to Saturn, the same within 1%.

Mars: $P$ = 686.981/365.2564 = 1.88082 years, and $a = P^{2/3}$ = 1.52369 au, the fit's value, against Kepler's 1.524 and page 4.3's 1.5197.

Kepler's first try put Mars on an eccentric circle of radius $a$, with the Sun at the distance $ea$ from its center, moving by the area law. Show that it leads to Kepler's equation, with the angle $E'$ at the center in place of $E$. Expand its equation of center to second order, compare it with the ellipse's and the equant's, and find the largest difference from the ellipse for Mars, $e$ = 0.0934.

The area swept is the one of section 2 with $b = a$, and the direction from the Sun is $\operatorname{atan2}(\sin E',\ e + \cos E')$. Expand it with $E' \approx M - e\sin M + \tfrac12 e^2\sin 2M$.

With $b = a$ the area swept from the aphelion is $\tfrac12 a^2(E' + e\sin E')$, so the area law gives $M = E' + e\sin E'$. The direction from the Sun is $E' - e\sin E' + \tfrac12 e^2\sin 2E'$ to second order, and with $\sin E' \approx \sin M - \tfrac12 e\sin 2M$ this is $M - 2e\sin M + \tfrac32 e^2\sin 2M$. The ellipse, squeezed by $b/a$, has $\tfrac54 e^2$, the equant $e^2$: computed at $e$ = 0.001 the three coefficients are 1.5000, 1.2500 and 1.0000.

So the circle with the area law errs by $+\tfrac14 e^2\sin 2M$ and the equant by $-\tfrac14 e^2\sin 2M$: they fail in opposite directions, and the ellipse lies halfway between them. For Mars the circle is off by up to 7.53′ in heliocentric ecliptic longitude and the equant by up to 8.96′, where the second order gives $e^2/4$ = 7.50′.
