Copernicus' and Tycho's systems give Mars the directions of page 4.2's equant, which misses the real planet by up to 0.88° near its oppositions. Turning the epicycle with the real Sun instead of the mean Sun brought the error down to 32′, and left the part that comes from the shape of Mars' path. Kepler, working on Tycho's observations of Mars, found that shape: an ellipse.

To judge Kepler's model we need a reference that is not built from it. The planets of the reference sky, used on every page so far, are themselves Kepler ellipses whose elements drift slowly (Standish's tables), and a Kepler model fitted to them would agree with them almost by construction. On this page the Sun and Mars therefore come from VSOP87, a theory that adds the planets' pulls on one another to the ellipses. It stays within 0.61″ of JPL's modern ephemeris for Mars seen from the Earth, and differs from the reference sky of the earlier pages by up to 7.3′ for Mars and 0.69′ for the Sun.

1 · ObservationThe Sun's speed and Mars' distances

Every model so far was judged by the directions it predicts. The two observations of this section are about distances: how far the Sun is from us through the year, and how far Mars is from the Sun around its path. Both can be measured without any model of the motion.

The Sun's speed and its apparent size

The Sun's apparent diameter measures its distance, since the two are inversely proportional. Through 2025 its daily motion along the ecliptic ran from 0.9533° to 1.0196°, 7.0% apart, while its diameter ran from 31.46′ to 32.53′, only 3.4% apart. Figure 1 sets the two side by side, each in percent of its mean, with the square of the diameter.

The speed varies as the square of the diameter, so as the inverse square of the distance $r$: the product $r^2$ times the angular speed stays constant through the year within 0.10%, while $r$ times the angular speed varies by 3.5%. Page 2.1's eccentric, a uniform motion on a circle seen from a point off its center, gives a speed that varies as $1/r$ to first order. To match the Sun's speed it needs a distance that varies twice as much as the real one, which is the "twice too much" of pages 2.1 and 3.2.

Mars triangulated

The distance of Mars from the Sun cannot be read from its size by eye, but it can be found by triangulation. After one sidereal period, 686.98 days, Mars is back at the same point of its path among the stars, while the Earth is at another place on its own. The two lines of sight, one from each place, meet at that point. The Earth's two places come from the Sun's direction and its distance, the latter read from its apparent diameter as above.

From 2010 to 2025 a first sighting every ten days, with its partner one period later, gives 103 points where the two lines meet at 20° or more. Their distances from the Sun run from 1.381 to 1.665 au and agree with the reference within 0.0010 au. Figure 2 compares them with the circle through the farthest and the nearest points, whose diameter lies on the line of the apsides.

Near the apsides the points lie on the circle, and between them they fall inside it, by up to 0.0064 au, six times the scatter of the points. Mars' path is narrower than a circle across its long axis.

2 · ModelKepler's ellipse and the area law

The ellipse

An ellipse is the set of points whose distances from two fixed points, its foci, add up to the same length $2a$. Its center C lies halfway between them, $a$ is the semi-major axis, and the foci are at the distance $ea$ from C, with $e$ the eccentricity. Kepler put the Sun ☉ at one focus. We call the other focus Q, and the ends of the long axis the aphelion A, the point farthest from the Sun, and the perihelion Π.

The circle on the long axis, of center C and radius $a$, squeezed towards the axis in the ratio $b/a$, gives the ellipse, where $b = a \sqrt{1 - e^2}$ is the semi-minor axis. The point P′ of the circle at the angle $E$ from the aphelion, seen from C, goes to the point P of the ellipse straight below or above it. $E$ is the eccentric anomaly (not page 2.2's equation of time). Measured from the Sun, with $x$ along the axis towards A, P is at

$$\bigl(a(e + \cos E),\ b\sin E\bigr), \qquad r = {\odot}\text{P} = a(1 + e\cos E).$$

The distance follows from the sum of the squares, since $b^2 = a^2(1 - e^2)$. The squeeze is largest across the long axis, at $E = 90^\circ$, where the ellipse lies inside its circle by $a - b \approx ae^2/2$. For Mars, $e$ = 0.0934 and $a$ = 1.524 au give 0.0067 au, the narrowing of Figure 2. The circle through the apsides with its center at C is this circle.

Equal areas in equal times

Kepler's second rule fixes how fast the body goes round: the line from the Sun to the body sweeps equal areas in equal times. The area swept between two moments is about $\tfrac12 r^2$ times the angle turned, so the angular speed seen from the Sun varies as $1/r^2$, which is what the Sun showed in Figure 1.

To place the body on a given date we need the area swept since the aphelion as a function of $E$. In the circle, the region swept from A to P′ about the focus ☉ is the sector A C P′ plus the triangle C ☉ P′, whose base C☉ = $ea$ has the height $a\sin E$:

$$\text{area}_\text{circle} = \tfrac12 a^2 E + \tfrac12\,ea \cdot a\sin E = \tfrac12 a^2 (E + e\sin E),$$

with $E$ in radians. Squeezing towards the axis multiplies every area by $b/a$, and the whole ellipse has the area $\pi ab$. The fraction of the period since the aphelion is the fraction of the area swept, $\tfrac12 ab(E + e\sin E)/(\pi ab)$. Writing $M$ for $2\pi$ times that fraction, the mean anomaly counted from the aphelion as on every page since 2.1, we get Kepler's equation:

$$M = E + e\sin E.$$

Fitzpatrick counts $M$ and $E$ from the perihelion and writes it $M = E - e\sin E$. Given the date we know $M$, and we need $E$. The equation cannot be solved for $E$ in closed form, but it is easy to solve by iteration. Starting from $E = M$, either repeat $E \leftarrow M - e\sin E$, or take Newton's steps

$$E \leftarrow E - \frac{E + e\sin E - M}{1 + e\cos E}.$$

Exercise 1 compares the two. With $E$ known, P is placed by the coordinates above, and its direction from the Sun, the true anomaly, is $M + q$, where $q$ is the equation of center of page 2.1. Figure 3 draws the ellipse with the areas swept in equal times.

The ellipse and the equant

Expanded in $e$ (page 4.2, Exercise 1), the ellipse gives $q \approx -2e\sin M + \tfrac54 e^2\sin 2M$ and $r \approx a(1 + e\cos M + e^2\sin^2 M)$. Page 4.2's equant, with the same $e$, has the same first-order terms, and seen from the empty focus Q the point of the ellipse turns uniformly to first order: the empty focus is an equant. Kepler, who had first given up Ptolemy's equal division of the eccentricity, restored it as the two foci of the ellipse (HAMA p. 172).

Page 2.1's eccentric needs $2e$ for the same directions, so its distance varies twice as much as the ellipse's. The Sun's speed in Figure 1 told the two apart: on the ellipse the distance changes by the factor $(1 + e)/(1 - e)$ between perihelion and aphelion, and the angular speed by its square.

The third law

The ellipse leaves the size of each orbit free, as Ptolemy's and Copernicus' models did. Kepler found a rule that ties it to the period: the square of the period is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis. In years and astronomical units,

$$P^2 = a^3.$$

The periods are sidereal, measured against the stars. Page 4.3 found the distances of the five planets from their elongations and their loops, without any such rule, and its periods about the Sun turned to sidereal ones with page 2.3's precession give the table. The law holds within 0.3% for Venus, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn. Mercury's distance is 1.9% short, the error page 4.3 found for its elongated path.

Mars seen from the Earth

In Kepler's model every planet and the Earth move on their own ellipses about the Sun. Seen from the Earth, the Sun is at the ecliptic longitude $\lambda_\odot$ and the distance $R$, the Earth's ellipse described from the Earth, and Mars at its heliocentric ecliptic longitude $\lambda_\text{h}$ and the distance $r$ from the Sun. Leaving out the small tilt of Mars' plane to the ecliptic, the planet's direction from the Earth is that of

$$\bigl(r\cos\lambda_\text{h} + R\cos\lambda_\odot,\ \ r\sin\lambda_\text{h} + R\sin\lambda_\odot\bigr).$$

This is page 4.2's residual model, the epicycle turned by the real Sun, with the equant's circle replaced by Mars' ellipse and the epicycle by the Earth's.

3 · FitThe Earth's ellipse and Mars'

Each ellipse has five parameters: the mean motion $n$, the mean ecliptic longitude $L_0$ at J2000.0, the longitude of the aphelion $\lambda_A$, the eccentricity $e$ and the semi-major axis $a$. The Earth's comes first, since every sighting of Mars is made from it.

The Sun's ellipse from the seasons

Page 2.1 fitted the eccentric to the lengths of spring and summer, Hipparchus' method, and the same two lengths fix the ellipse. The only change is the law of the motion: the time from one ecliptic longitude of the Sun to another is now the difference of the two values of $M$ given by Kepler's equation, divided by $n$. Two seasons give two equations in $e$ and the apogee, which Newton's method solves in four steps.

In 2026 spring lasted 92.735 days and summer 93.653 days. With page 1.3's mean motion they give $e$ = 0.016655 and the apogee at the ecliptic longitude 103.38°, where page 2.1's eccentric needed $e$ = 0.033473, twice as much, with its apogee at 103.73°. Each year of 2016–2025 alone gives $e$ between 0.01665 and 0.01675. The mean longitude follows from the moment of the spring equinox, the apogee moves at page 2.3's rate, and $a$ = 1 au by definition.

Mars from its oppositions to the real Sun

At an opposition to the real Sun the Earth lies between the Sun and Mars, so the planet's geocentric ecliptic longitude is its heliocentric one, whatever the two distances. The eight oppositions of 2010–2025 therefore give eight heliocentric directions of Mars at known moments, as the oppositions to the mean Sun gave the directions of K on page 4.2.

We keep page 4.1's mean motion, fitted to oppositions 79 years apart, and fit the other three by least squares: $e$ = 0.0934, the aphelion at the ecliptic longitude 156.15° at J2000, fixed among the stars as on page 4.2, and $L_0$ = 355.44°. The ellipse passes within 0.75′ of the eight oppositions. Page 4.2's equant, refitted with the real Sun, had found $e$ = 0.0935.

The third law gives the size. Page 4.1's mean motion, less the precession, is one turn among the stars in 686.981 days, 1.88082 sidereal years, so $a$ = 1.5237 au. Page 4.3 found 1.5197 au from the loops; here no observation of distance enters the fit, so every distance the model gives is a prediction.

HistoryKepler's Mars

Kepler joined Tycho in 1600 and was set to work on the theory of Mars. He found that Mars' orbit had to be referred to the Sun's actual position (Voelkel ch. 3): he was the first to refer all the observations to the true Sun instead of the mean Sun (HAMA p. 171).

He turned the method of Figure 2 round. Seen from the Earth at several moments one period of Mars apart, the planet marks a fixed point, and the Earth's places can be triangulated from it. The Earth, too, moved faster near the Sun (Voelkel ch. 3), and Kepler gave its circle an equant with half the eccentricity, as Ptolemy had for the planets (HAMA p. 155).

For Mars he first found a circle with an equant, placed freely, that gave the longitudes within about 8′ (Fitzpatrick § 1.4), but not the distances: one model served the longitudes, another the distances (HAMA p. 98). Taking the speed as inversely proportional to the distance, and its sum as an area, he came to the area law. On an eccentric circle it kept Mars too long on the sides of its orbit, so the orbit had to be squeezed in there, "as though you held a fat-bellied sausage and squeezed it in the middle", in Voelkel's words. A first ellipse erred by almost exactly the opposite amount, and the one in between had the Sun at a focus; it came to him "as if I were roused from a dream and saw a new light" (Voelkel ch. 3). The Astronomia Nova appeared in 1609. Exercise 3 redoes the first step.

The third law came last, on 15 May 1618, as Kepler finished the Harmonices Mundi, from periods known for centuries and distances derived from Tycho's observations (Voelkel ch. 4). Exercise 2 uses his numbers.

4 · TestThe ellipses against the real Mars and Sun

Figure 4 gives the error curve of the model fitted to 2010–2025, the two ellipses, against VSOP87's Mars, with page 4.2's equant as a ghost on the same reference.

Over 2010–2025 the ellipses stay within 5.8′ of the real Mars, where page 4.2's equant is off by up to 0.81° on this reference. Over the two centuries 1926–2126 the error grows to 18′, the subject of the residual.

The distances are the stronger test, since none entered the fit. Mars' distance from the Earth stays within 0.0019 au of the real one over 2010–2025, ten times closer than page 4.3's equant (0.0205 au), and the triangulated points of Figure 2 lie within 0.0010 au of the ellipse.

The Sun's ellipse is tested the same way in Figure 5, with page 2.1's eccentric, its apogee moving at page 2.3's rate, as a ghost. In 2026 the ellipse stays within 0.25′ of the real Sun, where the eccentric is off by 1.74′. Within fifty years of 2026 it stays within 1.2′, and within five centuries within 2.7′, against 2.0′ and 4.0′ for the eccentric.

The Sun's distance closes the question left by pages 2.1 and 3.2. In 2026 the real Sun came within 0.98330 au and went out to 1.01664 au, a ratio of 1.0339; the ellipse gives 0.98335 and 1.01665 au, the same ratio, and page 2.1's eccentric 0.9665 and 1.0335, a ratio of 1.0693.

HistoryThe Rudolphine Tables

Kepler's test was his tables. The Rudolphine Tables, printed in 1627, let anyone compute a planet's place for any date, and were some fifty times more accurate than Ptolemy's and Copernicus' (Voelkel ch. 6).

From them Kepler predicted that Mercury would cross the face of the Sun on 7 November 1631. He died before the day, and the transit came within six hours of his time. "I have found him, I have seen him where no one has ever seen him before!", wrote Gassendi, who watched it in Paris, and astronomers had to grant that Kepler's theory was at least twenty times more accurate than any other (Voelkel ch. 7).

5 · ResidualWhat the ellipses leave

What is left of Mars' error, a few arcminutes, comes from the planets' pulls on one another, which Newton's gravitation explains and Kepler's model leaves out. Some of it is periodic. The rest grows with time, because each ellipse slowly turns and changes. Fitted to the oppositions of 1931–1946 as well as 2010–2025, Mars' aphelion turns 0.43° a century among the stars, and with that turn the error over 1926–2126 falls from 18′ to 8.4′.

The model also leaves out the tilt of Mars' plane, 1.85° to the ecliptic, which changes the heliocentric ecliptic longitude by at most 0.9′. The Sun's quarter of an arcminute is mostly the Moon's: each month the Earth goes round the center of mass of the Earth and the Moon, which moves the Sun seen from it by up to 6.9″ either way, the wobble of Figure 5. Averaged over a month the error stays under 0.13′, of the size of the nutation, 10.7″ in 2026.

Positions are computed today by integrating Newton's equations (JPL's ephemerides) or from series such as VSOP87, which start from Kepler's ellipses and add the planets' pulls as further terms.

The Moon after Kepler

An ellipse is also the first term of the Moon's motion, the 6.29° of page 3.1. But the Sun pulls on the Moon far more than the planets pull on one another, and the ellipse leaves large terms: the evection, 1.27°, the variation, 0.66°, the annual equation, 11′, and the turning of the perigee in 8.85 years and of the nodes in 18.61 years. They come from the Sun's pull, which is Newton's subject, and the Moon's story goes on in the epilogue.

Exercises

Solve Kepler's equation for Mars, $e$ = 0.0934, at $M = 30^\circ$, first by repeating $E \leftarrow M - e\sin E$, then by Newton's method, both from $E = M$, and count the steps until $E$ changes by less than $10^{-8}$ degrees. Find the true anomaly $M + q$ and $r$. Then do the same for the view $e$ = 0.6 of Figure 3 at $M = 90^\circ$, checking $E$ in the read-out, and for Halley's comet, $e$ = 0.967, at $M = 30^\circ$. Why does the first method slow down?

Keep $e\sin E$ in radians, multiplied by $180^\circ/\pi$, when $E$ is in degrees. For the first method, compare the error before and after a step: $E_{k+1} - E = -e(\sin E_k - \sin E)$.

For Mars, $E$ = 27.526769°: the first method takes 9 steps (27.3243°, 27.5436°, 27.5254°, 27.5269°, …), Newton's 3 (27.52452063°, 27.52676909°, 27.52676909°). The point is at $(e + \cos E,\ \sqrt{1 - e^2}\sin E)$ = (0.9802, 0.4601) in units of $a$, so $M + q$ = 25.1472°, $q$ = −4.8528°, and $r = 1 + e\cos E$ = 1.08283 $a$.

For $e$ = 0.6 at $M = 90^\circ$, $E$ = 60.175677°: 19 steps against Newton's 5. For Halley's comet, $E$ = 15.341451°: the first method jumps between 2.30°, 27.78°, 4.18°, 25.96°, … and takes 312 steps, Newton's method 4.

Near the root, $E_{k+1} - E \approx -e\cos E\,(E_k - E)$: each step multiplies the error by $e|\cos E|$, 0.083 for Mars, 0.298 for $e$ = 0.6 and 0.933 for the comet. Newton's method squares the error at each step, once it is small: for Mars the errors are $2.2 \times 10^{-3}$ degrees, then $1.8 \times 10^{-9}$.

Compute $P/a$ and $P^2/a^3$, in years and astronomical units, for each. Then find Mars' distance from page 4.1's sidereal period of 686.981 days and the sidereal year of 365.2564 days, and compare it with the fit.

Divide the periods by 365 and the distances by 1000 first.

$P/a$ runs from 0.621 (Mercury) to 3.100 (Saturn): the period grows faster than the distance. $P^2/a^3$ is 0.995, 1.001, 1.000, 1.001, 1.002 and 1.010 from Mercury to Saturn, the same within 1%.

Mars: $P$ = 686.981/365.2564 = 1.88082 years, and $a = P^{2/3}$ = 1.52369 au, the fit's value, against Kepler's 1.524 and page 4.3's 1.5197.

Kepler's first try put Mars on an eccentric circle of radius $a$, with the Sun at the distance $ea$ from its center, moving by the area law. Show that it leads to Kepler's equation, with the angle $\varphi$ at the center in place of $E$. Expand its equation of center to second order, compare it with the ellipse's and the equant's, and find the largest difference from the ellipse for Mars, $e$ = 0.0934.

The area swept is the one of section 2 with $b = a$, and the direction from the Sun is $\operatorname{atan2}(\sin\varphi,\ e + \cos\varphi)$. Expand it with $\varphi \approx M - e\sin M + \tfrac12 e^2\sin 2M$.

With $b = a$ the area swept from the aphelion is $\tfrac12 a^2(\varphi + e\sin\varphi)$, so the area law gives $M = \varphi + e\sin\varphi$. The direction from the Sun is $\varphi - e\sin\varphi + \tfrac12 e^2\sin 2\varphi$ to second order, and with $\sin\varphi \approx \sin M - \tfrac12 e\sin 2M$ this is $M - 2e\sin M + \tfrac32 e^2\sin 2M$. The ellipse, squeezed by $b/a$, has $\tfrac54 e^2$, the equant $e^2$: computed at $e$ = 0.001 the three coefficients are 1.5000, 1.2500 and 1.0000.

So the circle with the area law errs by $+\tfrac14 e^2\sin 2M$ and the equant by $-\tfrac14 e^2\sin 2M$: they fail in opposite directions, and the ellipse lies halfway between them. For Mars the circle is off by up to 7.53′ in heliocentric ecliptic longitude and the equant by up to 8.96′, where the second order gives $e^2/4$ = 7.50′.
