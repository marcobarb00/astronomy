In Ptolemy's models the epicycles of Mars, Jupiter and Saturn all keep step with the mean Sun, and the centers of the epicycles of Venus and Mercury move with it, which page 4.2 left without a reason. Copernicus linked the motions of the five planets to one point, the place of the mean Sun, on a single scale for all of them, and he fixed that point.

The geometry of each model stays Ptolemy's, so the directions it predicts do not change, and the error curve of page 4.2 comes out the same. The five separate models become one system instead: the planets fall into an order, and their distances become a prediction that the real sky can test.

1 · ObservationThe Sun in the motion of every planet

Each planet repeats its anomaly related to the Sun once in its synodic period, and each phase of it comes at the same place relative to the Sun. Figure 1 follows the five planets through 2022–2025 by their elongation, the ecliptic longitude of the planet less that of the Sun.

Venus and Mercury swing from one side of the Sun to the other, and in these years never got farther from it than 47.2° and 27.4°. Mars, Jupiter and Saturn fall behind the Sun through every elongation. The table gives the synodic period of each planet, the mean time between two of its loops over the century 1926–2025.

Ptolemy builds the Sun into each of his five models separately. For Mars, Jupiter and Saturn the radius KP of the epicycle stays parallel to the line from the Earth to the mean Sun (X 6). For Venus and Mercury the center K of the epicycle moves at the mean Sun's motion (IX 3), so that it lies in the direction of the mean Sun, as seen from the equant rather than from the Earth (X 1–3 for Venus).

Each model also has a ratio of its own, the size of its epicycle in the last column of the table, fitted to that planet alone, so the models do not say which planet is nearer.

In Ptolemy's time the order of Saturn, Jupiter and Mars was agreed, but Venus and Mercury were put below the Sun by the older astronomers and above it by some of their successors, and the question could not be settled, "since none of the stars has a noticeable parallax (which would let us derive the distances)". He kept the older order, with the Sun in the middle (IX 1).

2 · ModelCopernicus' arrangement

Exchanging the deferent and the epicycle

Take Mars in page 4.2's model and write its place as the sum of two vectors, from the Earth O to the center K of the epicycle, and from K to the planet P. The second always has the length $r$ and points to the mean Sun, at the ecliptic longitude $\bar\lambda_\odot$:

$$\vec{\text{OP}} = \vec{\text{OK}} + \vec{\text{KP}}, \qquad \vec{\text{KP}} = r\,\hat u(\bar\lambda_\odot).$$

Two vectors can be added in either order. Draw $\vec{\text{KP}}$ from O instead of from K, and call its end ☉. Then O, K, P and ☉ are the corners of a parallelogram, and

$$\vec{\text{OP}} = \vec{\text{O}\odot} + \vec{\odot\text{P}}, \qquad \vec{\text{O}\odot} = \vec{\text{KP}}, \qquad \vec{\odot\text{P}} = \vec{\text{OK}}.$$

P is the same point, so it is seen in the same direction at every moment. This is page 3.1's exchange of an epicycle and an eccentric, applied to a planet. The epicycle has become a circle of radius $r$ about the Earth, on which ☉ goes round once a year in the direction of the mean Sun, and the deferent, with its center C and its equant Q, has moved as a whole from O to ☉.

For Venus and Mercury we use the simple model of page 4.1, in which the deferent, of radius 1 about the Earth, carries K at the mean Sun's ecliptic longitude. K is then the point ☉ itself, and the planet goes round it on the epicycle, so the two circles exchange their roles. (In Ptolemy's full model K is in that direction as seen from the equant, and ☉ moves on an eccentric.) Figure 2 draws Mars, in page 4.2's model, and Venus, in the simple one, in both descriptions.

One scale for five planets

Each of Ptolemy's models measures its lengths in units of its own deferent, so nothing ties one model to another. Multiplying every length of a model by the same number changes no direction seen from O, so each model may be enlarged or shrunk as we like. For each planet we choose the number that makes the length O☉ equal to 1: we divide an outer planet's model by $r$ and leave an inner planet's as it is.

Then all five models have the same vector $\vec{\text{O}\odot}$, of length 1 towards the mean Sun, and so the same point ☉, and they can be drawn as one figure. The unit of length is now the distance from the Earth to ☉, the astronomical unit (au). The radius $a$ of each planet's circle about ☉, its mean distance from ☉, is its epicycle read in a new way:

$$a = \frac{1}{r} \ \text{(Mars, Jupiter, Saturn)}, \qquad a = r \ \text{(Venus, Mercury)}.$$

Ptolemy's epicycles give the distances of Figure 3 and of the table below.

Copernicus fixed the Sun

Copernicus took one more step: "At rest, however, in the middle of everything is the sun", and "whatever appears as a motion of the sun is really due rather to the motion of the earth" (I 10). Subtracting $\vec{\text{O}\odot}$ from every place moves the whole figure without turning it, as panel (b) of Figure 2 does. ☉ stands still, the Earth goes round it once a year at the heliocentric longitude $\bar\lambda_\odot + 180^\circ$, its direction seen from ☉, and every direction from the Earth to a planet stays as it was.

Copernicus' center is, strictly, the center of the Earth's circle, which lies near the Sun because the Earth's circle is an eccentric, as the Sun's was on page 2.1 (V 4). The point ☉ of this page, at the distance 1 in the direction of the mean Sun, is that center for an Earth moving uniformly on a circle about it.

Directions from the Earth depend only on where the bodies are relative to one another, so fixing the Sun changes no prediction. The predictions come from the arrangement: one point ☉ for the five planets, and their distances from it.

The periods about the Sun, and the order

Seen from ☉, an outer planet is in the direction of $\vec{\text{OK}}$: its heliocentric longitude is the ecliptic longitude of K, $\bar\lambda + q$, which grows on average at the planet's mean motion, so it goes round ☉ in its period in ecliptic longitude $P_\lambda$ of page 4.1. An inner planet is in the direction of $\vec{\text{KP}}$, which turns at $n_\odot + n_\mu$. With the synodic period $S$ and the year $Y$, the time $P$ a planet takes to go once round ☉ is therefore

$$\frac{1}{P} = \frac{1}{Y} - \frac{1}{S} \ \text{(outer)}, \qquad \frac{1}{P} = \frac{1}{Y} + \frac{1}{S} \ \text{(inner)}.$$

The first is page 4.1's relation $1/P_\lambda + 1/S = 1/Y$. With the sidereal year $Y_\text{s}$ of page 2.3 in place of the tropical year, $P$ is counted against the stars, as Copernicus counts it (V 1).

Multiplied by the length of a period relation, the equations become sums of whole numbers. Ptolemy already noted that for the outer planets the years equal the turns in longitude plus the returns in anomaly (IX 3), as in Mars' 79 = 42 + 37. For Venus and Mercury his relations have the planet go round the zodiac as often as the Sun; in Copernicus' arrangement the returns in anomaly are added to the years instead, and Venus goes round the Sun 8 + 5 = 13 times in 8 years (V 1).

The two columns put the planets in the same order: the farther from the Sun, the longer the turn. Copernicus kept the principle of the ancient philosophers, "that the size of the spheres is measured by the length of the time", and found in it "a marvelous symmetry of the universe, and an established harmonious linkage between the motion of the spheres and their size, such as can be found in no other way" (I 10).

Greatest elongations and loops

In this arrangement the observations of pages 4.1 and 4.2 take a simple form. Venus and Mercury are farthest from the Sun in the sky when the line of sight from O touches their circle about ☉. The radius ☉P is then perpendicular to OP, and the right triangle O P ☉ gives

$$\sin\varepsilon_\text{max} = a,$$

with $\varepsilon_\text{max}$ the greatest elongation (not the obliquity). Ptolemy's epicycle of Venus, 43.17 where the deferent is 60, gives $a$ = 0.719 and a greatest elongation of 46.0°, within the range of the real ones, 45.3° to 47.2° (page 4.1).

An outer planet goes back when the Earth, moving faster on its smaller circle, overtakes it, so a loop comes at opposition, the planet and ☉ on opposite sides of the Earth. Copernicus calls this effect of the Earth's motion a "parallax" (V 1), and explains: "all these phenomena, which the ancient astronomers sought [to explain] by means of an epicycle for each planet, happen on account of the single motion of the earth" (V 3).

A farther planet has a smaller $r = 1/a$ and goes back less, so its loops are shorter: the arcs "appear greater in Jupiter than in Saturn and smaller than in Mars" (I 10), as in the table of page 4.1.

The distance from the Earth changes too. Mars is $a - 1 = 0.52$ au away at opposition and $a + 1 = 2.52$ au at conjunction, almost five times as far, and Copernicus notes that "when it shines all night, [it] seems to equal Jupiter in size" (I 10).

Copernicus without the equant

Copernicus rejected the equant, a motion uniform about a point that is not the center of its circle (V 2, page 4.2). For Mars, Jupiter and Saturn he moved the center of the deferent to $\tfrac32 e$ from ☉, where Ptolemy's C was at $e$ and Q at $2e$, and put on it a small epicycle, the epicyclet, of radius a third of that, $\tfrac12 e$. The planet goes round the epicyclet once for each turn of the deferent, counted from the deferent's radius, which is twice relative to fixed directions (V 4).

The epicyclet gives the equant's directions to second order and its distances to first order (Exercise 2). At second order the distances differ: those of the equant exceed the distances of Kepler's ellipse by up to $\tfrac12 e^2$, those of the epicyclet by up to $e^2$. Fitzpatrick calls Copernicus' device "a marginally less accurate approximation" than Ptolemy's (§ 1.3).

Put in place of the equant in page 4.2's Mars, with the same parameters, the epicyclet moves the planet by up to 25′, and the largest error over 2010–2025 grows from 0.88° to 0.91°: giving up the equant made the model a little worse.

Kepler brought the equant's point back. In Neugebauer's words, he "not only reintroduced the equant for the planetary motion but applied it also for better representation of the earth's motion, eventually to become the second focus of the elliptic orbit" (HAMA p. 155). Page 4.2 found that, to first order, the empty focus of an ellipse is an equant, and page 4.5 tests the ellipse itself.

3 · FitThe periods and the distances of the five planets

No planet gains a parameter: its period and its distance are the motions and the epicycle of pages 4.1 and 4.2. Now that the distances of different planets can be compared, we fit all five, from observations already made, as every fit of the course.

The periods

Events far apart fix a period best, since the error of each is divided by the time between them. The synodic periods of section 1 are the slopes of straight lines through the times of every opposition, or inferior conjunction, of 1926–2025. The zodiacal anomaly moves single intervals by days, but over a century the variations average out, and the relations of section 2, with the sidereal year, give the periods about ☉.

The distances

For Venus and Mercury each greatest elongation gives a distance, $a = \sin\varepsilon_\text{max}$, exactly for a circle. Over 1986–2025 they fall in every part of the zodiac, so we take the mean of their sines. Venus' greatest elongations hardly vary, Mercury's vary widely (Exercise 3).

For an outer planet the simplest triangle would be a quadrature, the planet 90° from the Sun, where the angle at the Earth is right and $\cos\psi = 1/a$. The angle $\psi$ at ☉ would come from the time since the opposition, at $360^\circ/S$ a day. For Saturn, though, $\psi$ is 84°, close to a right angle itself, and to first order a day's error in the timing changes the distance by 16% (Figure 4).

The arc of a loop needs no timing, since it is the difference of two places. So for Jupiter and Saturn we measure the epicycle as page 4.1 did for Mars, from the mean arc of their loops of 1986–2025, and take $a = 1/r$. Mars keeps page 4.2's equant; its loops alone, measured the same way, give the same distance within a thousandth of an au.

HistoryCopernicus' periods and distances

Copernicus took his periods from the period relations that Ptolemy had received from Hipparchus, counted in years "measured by the fixed stars", since he had found that years counted from the equinoxes "are not entirely uniform", and corrected them with observations of his own (V 1).

For the distance of each outer planet he used a single observation away from opposition and solved the triangle of the planet, the Earth and the center of the Earth's circle (V 9, V 14, V 19). It is the general case of the quadrature above, with the angle at the center taken from his mean motions rather than timed. For Venus he used two greatest elongations taken with the Earth in opposite directions from the center of Venus' circle (V 21, Exercise 1), and for Mercury he found a radius that changed (V 27, Exercise 3).

His distances are Ptolemy's epicycles inverted, as he noted planet by planet: for Mars the result "differs only slightly from what Ptolemy found, and is almost identical therewith" (V 19). Neugebauer's verdict is that "the Ptolemaic theory excellently, however unknowingly, represents the order of magnitude of the actual dimensions of the planetary system" (HAMA p. 147).

4 · TestDirections and distances against the real sky

In Copernicus' arrangement, with page 4.2's model, Mars is seen in the directions that page 4.2 gave it: over the two centuries 1926–2125 the two descriptions agree almost exactly. Figure 5 is therefore page 4.2's error curve, with page 4.2's model, in Ptolemy's description, as a ghost.

The distances from the Earth are a prediction that Ptolemy's separate models could not make, since each had its own unit. Over 2010–2025 the model follows the real Mars within 0.020 au, at most 1.6% (Figure 6), and over the two centuries 1926–2125 it stays almost as close.

The real oppositions of 2010–2025 came at distances from 0.39 to 0.67 au. On a circle of radius $a$ centered on ☉, a deferent with $e = 0$, Mars would be 0.52 au away at every one of them. The model follows the real oppositions because its deferent is eccentric: page 4.2 found that the distance of K depends on the center C, $\rho \approx 1 + \text{OC}\cos M$.

Against the reference sky, the fitted periods agree within 0.1%, and the distances within 0.3% except Mercury's, which comes out 1.9% short. Mercury's path is elongated, so at a greatest elongation the line of sight is not quite perpendicular to its radius, and the sine gives less than the distance (Exercise 3).

The modern first approximation keeps this arrangement: every planet, the Earth included, moves on a Kepler ellipse about the Sun. The reference sky of this site uses such ellipses (Standish's elements), with Mars' semi-major axis 1.524 au.

HistoryCopernicus' check

Copernicus checked his mean motions as Ptolemy had, against observations far apart. For Jupiter he compared his own opposition of 1529 with Ptolemy's last, 1392 Egyptian years earlier, and found the motion "certain and confirmed because it agrees with the results obtained visually" (V 12).

Copernicus' distances were not accurate enough for the next step. Tycho Brahe, who had observed the planets for decades, wrote to Kepler that they would not serve his purpose, and suggested that Kepler use Tycho's own observations instead (Voelkel ch. 2).

5 · ResidualThe Earth's own unevenness

Copernicus' arrangement gives the directions of Ptolemy's models, so it keeps their errors. In it the epicycle of every outer planet is a copy of the Earth's circle, on which the Earth moves uniformly about ☉, while the Sun's path is not uniform (page 2.1). The Earth's unevenness is one and the same for every planet, and the model of this page leaves it out of Mars, Jupiter and Saturn alike.

Ptolemy could absorb it only into each planet's own deferent. In Neugebauer's words, the Earth's and the planet's eccentricities "produce a resultant eccentricity e which is the vector sum", and "Ptolemy, who did not realize this situation, ascribed the resultant eccentricity e to the deferent of the planetary orbit" (HAMA p. 147). Page 4.2's residual turned Mars' epicycle by the real Sun, in direction and distance, instead of the mean one, which gives the Earth's circle the real Sun's unevenness, and the error fell markedly.

Copernicus referred the planets to the center of the Earth's circle, the place of the mean Sun. Kepler found that Mars' orbit "had to take into account the sun's actual position", the true Sun rather than the mean one, and that the Earth "too speeded up when it approached the sun and slowed down as it receded" (Voelkel ch. 3): the unevenness that this page leaves out.

Before Kepler, Tycho Brahe kept Copernicus' arrangement of the planets about the Sun and held the Earth fixed instead. Seen from the Earth, his planets have the same directions. Whether any observation can decide between the two is the question of page 4.4.

Exercises

In 2025 Venus reached its greatest elongation east of the Sun on 10 January, 47.166°, and west of it on 31 May, 45.838°. Find the distance of Venus from each, and check the fitted value in Figure 2 by finding a greatest elongation of the model. Why do the two elongations of 2025 differ? Then redo Copernicus' computation of V 21: his two greatest elongations, 44°48′ (44.8°) and 47°16′ (47.27°), were taken with the Earth in opposite directions from the center of Venus' circle, the Earth's circle having radius 1. Find the radius of Venus' circle and how far its center is from the center of the Earth's.

In Copernicus' case the radius $a$ is the same in both triangles, and the distances from the Earth to the center of Venus' circle are $a/\sin\varepsilon_\text{max}$ in each; they add up to 2.

The sines are 0.7333 and 0.7174, a mean of 0.7254; the fit to the 50 greatest elongations of 1986–2025 gives 0.7226, so the model's greatest elongation, which Figure 2 shows in the Venus view, is 46.27° on either side.

The real distances are not constant. On 10 January 2025 Venus was 0.7213 au from the Sun and the Earth 0.9834 au, a ratio of 0.7335, the sine of the elongation; on 31 May they were 0.7280 and 1.0140 au, a ratio of 0.7179. Venus was nearer the Sun in January, which narrows the angle, but the Earth was nearer too, by a larger fraction, and the angle came out wider.

Copernicus: $\sin 44.8^\circ = 0.7046$ and $\sin 47.267^\circ = 0.7345$, so the distances to the center are $a/0.7046$ and $a/0.7345$, and their sum is 2. This gives $a = 0.7193$ and the two distances 1.0208 and 0.9792, so the center of Venus' circle is 0.0208 from the center of the Earth's: his 7193 and 208 in 10·000 parts.

Copernicus replaced the equant by an eccentric whose center is $d_1$ from the center of motion towards the apogee, carrying an epicyclet of radius $d_2$ (V 4). In the frame of the apsidal line the planet is at $(d_1 + \cos M - d_2\cos 2M,\ \sin M - d_2\sin 2M)$. Find $d_1$ and $d_2$ that give the equant's direction and distance to first order, then expand the direction and the distance to second order and compare them with the equant's. Copernicus' Mars has 1460 and 500 where the deferent is 10·000 (V 19): how close is that to Ptolemy's 6 and 12 where it is 60?

To first order $q$ is the component of the offset across the line of K, and the distance is 1 plus its component along it. For the second order, write the planet as $e^{iM}(1 + z)$ with $z = d_1 e^{-iM} - d_2 e^{iM}$, so that $q$ is the argument of $1 + z$, $z - z^2/2$ to second order.

Across the radius at $M$ the offsets give $-d_1\sin M - d_2(\sin 2M\cos M - \cos 2M\sin M) = -(d_1 + d_2)\sin M$, and along it $d_1\cos M - d_2(\cos 2M\cos M + \sin 2M\sin M) = (d_1 - d_2)\cos M$. So $q \approx -(d_1 + d_2)\sin M$ and $\rho \approx 1 + (d_1 - d_2)\cos M$, and the equant's $2e$ and $e$ need $d_1 = \tfrac32 e$ and $d_2 = \tfrac12 e$.

Direction: the imaginary part of $z$ is $-(d_1 + d_2)\sin M = -2e\sin M$, and that of $z^2$ is $(d_2^2 - d_1^2)\sin 2M = -2e^2\sin 2M$, so $q \approx -2e\sin M + e^2\sin 2M$, the equant's direction to second order (page 4.2's table). Distance: $\rho^2 = 1 + d_1^2 + d_2^2 + 2(d_1 - d_2)\cos M - 2d_1 d_2\cos 2M = 1 + 2e\cos M + e^2(1 + 3\sin^2 M)$, whose root is $1 + e\cos M + 2e^2\sin^2 M$, against the equant's $1 + e\cos M + \tfrac32 e^2\sin^2 M$ (Fitzpatrick § 4.4–4.5).

A numerical check at $e$ = 0.001 gives the coefficient of $\sin 2M$ as $1.0000\,e^2$ for both, and $(\rho - 1)/e^2$ at $M = 90^\circ$ as 2.000 and 1.500. For Mars, $e$ = 0.1005, the two differ by up to 3.4′ in the direction of K and 0.0050 in its distance. Copernicus' Mars has $d_1 + d_2$ = 0.196 and $d_1 - d_2$ = 0.096, against Ptolemy's $2e$ = 0.2 and $e$ = 0.1, and $d_1/d_2$ = 2.92 against 3.

Between 1986 and 2025 Mercury's greatest elongations ranged from 17.835°, on 26 September 2003, to 27.789°, on 28 March 2000. What does a circle of radius $a$ = 0.3796 about ☉ predict? On those two dates the reference sky has Mercury 0.3075 au and 0.4661 au from the Sun, and the Earth 1.0025 au and 0.9984 au. Explain the range. Then explain why the mean of the sines, 0.3796, falls short of Mercury's mean distance from the Sun divided by the Earth's at the same moments, 0.3881.

At a greatest elongation the line of sight touches Mercury's path. For a circle the radius is perpendicular to it there; for an elongated path that holds only at its nearest and farthest points.

The circle gives 22.31° at every greatest elongation. On 26 September 2003 the ratio of the distances was 0.3068, against $\sin 17.835^\circ = 0.3063$; on 28 March 2000 it was 0.4669, against $\sin 27.789^\circ = 0.4662$. Mercury was then near its nearest and its farthest points from the Sun, 0.3075 au and 0.4667 au in the reference ellipse. Its distance changes by half again between the two, and the greatest elongations follow it, so no circle about ☉ can give them.

Elsewhere the radius is not perpendicular to the line of sight, and $\sin\varepsilon_\text{max}$ is the ratio of the distances times the sine of the angle at Mercury. Over the 252 greatest elongations that sine runs from 0.956 to 0.999, which is why their mean comes out short of the mean ratio. Copernicus too found that no single circle fits: in his model Mercury's radius changes from 0.3573 to 0.3953 with the Earth's place relative to Mercury's apsides (V 27).
