Page 4.1 left Mars with loops of one size, where the real planet goes back almost twice as far in one part of the zodiac as in the opposite part. To follow it, the epicycle has to move unevenly round the zodiac, and come nearer to the Earth in some parts than in others.

Ptolemy's answer was an eccentric deferent on which the epicycle moves uniformly as seen from a second point, the equant. Fitted to Mars, it brings the error from 34° down to under a degree, and it turns out to agree with Kepler's ellipse to first order in the eccentricity.

1 · ObservationThe zodiacal anomaly at the oppositions and in the loops

To study the zodiacal anomaly on its own, we need moments when the anomaly related to the Sun has no effect. The oppositions to the mean Sun are such moments: there the planet lies on the line from the Earth through K, the centre of its epicycle, and is seen where K is (X 6).

If K moved uniformly, the planet would be seen there at its mean ecliptic longitude $\bar\lambda$, its mean place. Figure 1(a) shows, at each of the nineteen oppositions of Mars to the mean Sun of 1986–2025, how far Mars was ahead of its mean place: its ecliptic longitude less the $\bar\lambda$ of page 4.1's fit, negative when Mars was behind. It was up to about 12° ahead or behind, and it passed from ahead to behind near the ecliptic longitude 149° and back near 333°.

Figure 1(b) sets the loops of the same years against the same ecliptic longitude. Mars goes back by almost 20° in one part of the zodiac and by only 10° in the opposite part.

Ptolemy noted that this anomaly behaves the opposite way to the one related to the Sun: "the time from least speed to mean is always greater than the time from mean speed to greatest" (IX 5). An eccentric circle produces that, and "that is the hypothesis which we actually use to represent this kind of anomaly".

He also found that the oppositions and the retrograde arcs ask for different eccentricities: "using rough estimation, the eccentricity one finds from the greatest equation of ecliptic anomaly turns out to be about twice that derived from the size of the retrograde arcs at greatest and least distances of the epicycle" (X 6).

2 · ModelThe eccentric deferent and the equant

An eccentric deferent

Ptolemy's remark suggests the eccentric that served for the Sun, so we try it first on the deferent. Let K move uniformly on a circle of radius 1 whose centre C lies at the distance OC = $e$ from the Earth O, towards the apogee A of the circle, at the ecliptic longitude $\lambda_A$.

As for the Sun on page 2.1, the place of K on its circle is given by its mean anomaly $M = \bar\lambda - \lambda_A$: the angle at C from the direction of the apogee to K, counted eastward, which grows uniformly at the planet's mean motion $n$. $M$ belongs to the deferent. The planet's angle on its epicycle is still $\mu$, as on page 4.1 (on the Moon's pages it was the epicycle that carried $M$).

Seen from O, K is at $\bar\lambda + q$ and at the distance $\rho = \text{OK}$, where $q$ is page 2.1's equation of centre with K in place of the Sun. We call $q$ the zodiacal equation, Ptolemy's "equation of ecliptic anomaly":

$$q = -\operatorname{atan2}(e\sin M,\ 1 + e\cos M), \qquad \rho = \sqrt{1 + 2e\cos M + e^2}, \qquad M = \bar\lambda - \lambda_A.$$

The epicycle stays as on page 4.1, its radius KP parallel to the line from the Earth to the mean Sun, so at an opposition to the mean Sun the planet is still seen where K is (X 6). Figure 1(a) is then a picture of $q$, and its greatest value is about $e$ radians.

One eccentricity is not enough

Fitted to the eight oppositions to the mean Sun of 2010–2025, one turn of the zodiac, an eccentric needs $e$ = 0.200. With the epicycle sized to the loops of the same years, its loops come out the wrong way round: its longest loop falls in the part of the zodiac where Mars went back least, and its shortest where Mars went back most.

The direction from one point, the distance from another

To see why, look at what sets the size of a loop. The nearer K is to the Earth, the larger the epicycle looks and the longer the loop. The faster K is seen to move, the more it carries the loop forward, and the shorter the loop.

In the eccentric, C does two jobs: it is the centre of K's circle and the point about which K moves uniformly. To see which job controls the speed and which the distance, we give the second one to another point. Let K move uniformly as seen from a point Q on the line from O to the apogee, at the distance OQ from the Earth, while its circle stays centred on C. $M$ is now the angle at Q from the direction of the apogee to K, and it still grows at the rate $n$. In the eccentric Q is C. Figure 2 draws the construction, with C halfway between O and Q, where Ptolemy puts it, and dashed the side OK of the triangle OQK.

We call OC and OQ the offsets, the distances of the two points from the Earth. They are small beside the radius of the circle, 1: about 0.1 and 0.2 for Mars. To first order in the offsets we keep the terms proportional to one offset and drop the squares and the products of two, which are about ten times smaller again, as on page 3.2.

First the direction. Q lies on the line from O towards A, so seen from Q the Earth lies opposite to the apogee, and the angle OQK, between QO and QK, is $180^\circ - M$. The angle at K, between KQ and KO, is the difference between the directions of QK and OK, $\bar\lambda$ and $\bar\lambda + q$, so it is $-q$. The law of sines in the triangle OQK gives

$$\sin(-q) = \frac{\text{OQ}\,\sin(180^\circ - M)}{\rho} = \frac{\text{OQ}\,\sin M}{\rho}.$$

The right side is already proportional to OQ, so to first order $\rho$ can be taken as 1 and $\sin(-q)$ as $-q$, in radians: $q \approx -\text{OQ}\sin M$. As K goes round, $M$ grows at the rate $n$, so the direction of K, $\bar\lambda + q$, changes at

$$\frac{d(\bar\lambda + q)}{dt} = n + \frac{dq}{dM}\,\frac{dM}{dt} \approx n\,(1 - \text{OQ}\cos M).$$

K is seen to move slowest at the apogee, at $n\,(1 - \text{OQ})$, and fastest at the perigee, at $n\,(1 + \text{OQ})$. K turns at the same rate about Q all the time, but at the apogee it is farther from the Earth than from Q by OQ, and at the perigee nearer by OQ, and the same motion seen from farther away looks slower.

Then the distance. C, Q and K make a second triangle, with CK = 1, the radius, and CQ = OQ − OC. C lies on the same line as O, so the angle at Q is again $180^\circ - M$, and the law of cosines gives

$$1 = \text{QK}^2 + \text{CQ}^2 + 2\,\text{CQ}\,\text{QK}\cos M.$$

Dropping the square $\text{CQ}^2$ leaves $\text{QK} \approx 1 - \text{CQ}\cos M$. The line OK is the sum of the long segment QK and the short one OQ, at the angle $M$ to each other. Squaring, $\rho^2 = \text{QK}^2 + 2\,\text{OQ}\,\text{QK}\cos M + \text{OQ}^2$, and without the square of OQ, $\rho \approx \text{QK} + \text{OQ}\cos M = 1 + (\text{OQ} - \text{CQ})\cos M$. Since OQ − CQ = OC, the two first-order results are

$$\frac{d(\bar\lambda + q)}{dt} \approx n\,(1 - \text{OQ}\cos M), \qquad \rho \approx 1 + \text{OC}\cos M.$$

The direction and the speed of K depend only on OQ, and its distance only on OC. The oppositions measure the direction, so they fix OQ, about 0.2 for Mars. In the eccentric OC is the same 0.2, and K is 1.20 from the Earth at the apogee and 0.80 at the perigee. The change of distance then outweighs the change of speed, and the loops are longest at the perigee. The real loops are longest near the apogee, so OC must be smaller than OQ.

The equant

Ptolemy separated the two points. The epicycle, he writes, "is carried on an eccentre which, though equal in size to the eccentre which produces the anomaly, is not described about the same centre", and for every planet but Mercury its centre "is the point bisecting the line joining the centre of the eccentre producing the anomaly to the centre of the ecliptic" (IX 5). The centre of the uniform motion, Q, is now called the equant.

So OC = $e$, OQ = $2e$ and CQ = $e$. Solved exactly, the law of cosines of the triangle CQK, $1 = \text{QK}^2 + e^2 + 2e\,\text{QK}\cos M$, is the same quadratic as the crank's on page 3.2, and the triangle OQK gives K as seen from the Earth:

$$\text{QK} = -e\cos M + \sqrt{1 - e^2\sin^2 M}, \qquad q = -\operatorname{atan2}(2e\sin M,\ \text{QK} + 2e\cos M).$$

The distance is $\rho = \sqrt{\text{QK}^2 + 4e\,\text{QK}\cos M + 4e^2}$. To first order $q \approx -2e\sin M$ and $\rho \approx 1 + e\cos M$: the equant gives the directions of an eccentric with $2e$ and the distances of one with $e$. For Ptolemy's Mars, 12 and 6 where the deferent is 60, K's distance changes half as much as in an eccentric with the same directions.

The line QK turns uniformly about a point that is not the centre of K's circle, so K does not move along its circle at a constant speed. Copernicus rejected the equant for that reason: in it "a circular motion can be uniform with respect to an extraneous center not its own" (De Revolutionibus V 2, and IV 2 for the Moon).

The planet

The planet moves on its epicycle as on page 4.1, with $\mu = \bar\lambda_\odot - \bar\lambda$, and Ptolemy counts this anomaly from the diameter of the epicycle "which points towards" the equant (IX 6), the mean apogee on the line QK. The epicycle's apogee seen from the Earth lies on the line OK, $q$ further on, so the planet is $\mu - q$ from it. He then finds the planet in two steps, first K, then the planet on the epicycle (XI 9):

$$\lambda = \bar\lambda + q + \eta, \qquad \eta = \operatorname{atan2}\big(r\sin(\mu - q),\ \rho + r\cos(\mu - q)\big).$$

With $e = 0$ this is page 4.1's model. The apogee of the deferent is fixed among the stars: Ptolemy has it move "about the same ... as the amount determined for the sphere of the fixed stars" (IX 5), so in ecliptic longitude of date it moves at the rate of precession of page 2.3.

The equant and Kepler's ellipse

The equant's split of directions and distances is the feature that survived into Kepler's ellipse. Working on Mars, Kepler first gave up the equality of the two eccentricities, and in the end restored it as the two foci of an ellipse (HAMA p. 172), along which the radius from one focus sweeps equal areas in equal times (Voelkel ch. 3; page 4.5).

Kepler put the Sun at that focus. Here we lay the ellipse over Ptolemy's deferent only to compare the two curves: with the same $e$ and semi-major axis 1, its centre is C and its foci are O and Q. Expanding both in $e$, with $M$ counted from the apogee (Exercise 1), and adding the Sun's eccentric with its centre at $2e$, gives the table:

model direction, $q$ distance
eccentric, OC = $2e$ $-2e\sin M + 2e^2\sin 2M$ $1 + 2e\cos M + 2e^2\sin^2 M$
equant, OC = $e$, OQ = $2e$ $-2e\sin M + e^2\sin 2M$ $1 + e\cos M + \tfrac32 e^2\sin^2 M$
Kepler's ellipse $-2e\sin M + \tfrac54 e^2\sin 2M$ $1 + e\cos M + e^2\sin^2 M$

The equant and the ellipse agree to first order in $e$, in direction and in distance. In direction they differ by $-\tfrac14 e^2\sin 2M$, at most $e^2/4$, and in distance by $\tfrac12 e^2\sin^2 M$. The eccentric has the ellipse's directions to first order too, but its distances vary twice as much, as pages 2.1 and 3.2 found for the Sun and the Moon.

Seen from the empty focus Q, the point of the ellipse runs ahead of a uniform turn by $\tfrac14 e^2\sin 2M$, so to first order the empty focus is an equant. The expansions can be checked numerically: the coefficient of $\sin 2M$ in the exact $q$, at $e = 0.001$, is $1.0000\,e^2$ for the equant and $1.2500\,e^2$ for the ellipse.

At $e$ = 0.1, about Mars' value, the greatest difference in direction is 10.4′, where the second-order term $e^2/4$ gives 8.6′; the terms of third order make up the rest. At the Sun's $e$ = 0.0167 the greatest difference is about 3% above $e^2/4$.

3 · FitMars from its oppositions and its loops

The model has six parameters: the mean motion $n$, the mean ecliptic longitude $L_0$ at J2000.0, the apogee $\lambda_A$, OQ, OC and the epicycle $r$. We keep OC and OQ apart, so that the observations, and not Ptolemy, decide how they are related. The mean motion stays as on page 4.1: oppositions 79 years apart fall at the same place in the zodiac and in the same configuration with the Sun, so both anomalies cancel, whatever the deferent.

The equant from the oppositions

The directions of K are what the equant point changes, and at each opposition to the mean Sun the planet is seen in the direction of K. The eight oppositions of 2010–2025 go once round the zodiac, as a full turn of an anomaly needs, and give eight values of $\bar\lambda + q$. Since $q \approx -\text{OQ}\sin M$, they fix $L_0$, $\lambda_A$ and OQ; the centre C changes them only in the second order.

The centre from the loops

With the directions known, the loops measure the distance of K. Their mean size fixes $r$, as on page 4.1, and the way it changes round the zodiac fixes OC. Figure 4 shows both observations of 2010–2025 against the model, with OC free. In (a) the model's curve is $q$, how far K is seen ahead of its mean place.

Each step uses the result of the other, so we alternate between them until nothing changes, which takes two rounds. The oppositions give OQ = 0.2007 and the loops OC = 0.0983, a ratio of 0.49, close to Ptolemy's half.

We therefore take Ptolemy's bisection and fit again with OC = $e$ and OQ = $2e$. Mars then has $e$ = 0.1005, its apogee at the ecliptic longitude $\lambda_A$ = 151.1° and $L_0$ = 355.38°. Where the deferent is 60, the fitted lengths are close to Ptolemy's:

HistoryPtolemy's oppositions and his bisection

Ptolemy observed three oppositions of Mars "as accurately as possible, using the astrolabe instruments", in AD 130, 135 and 139 (X 7). The oppositions show K from the Earth, while the uniform motion is about the equant, so he first took the centre of the deferent to be the equant (HAMA p. 174), then corrected the arcs between the oppositions and computed twice more. He found the apogee at Cancer 25° 30′ (115.5°) and 12 for the double eccentricity, and Toomer finds that a further round would hardly change them. Exercise 2 redoes the computation.

He does not show how he came to the bisection. Apart from the rough estimate of X 6, the symmetric arrangement is taken for granted in his computation (HAMA p. 173), and Neugebauer suggests that its likeness to the model of Venus, found from much more direct observations (X 1–3), helped its acceptance (p. 172).

4 · TestThe equant against the real Mars

To see how much of page 4.1's error the equant removes, Figure 5 gives the error curve of the model fitted to 2010–2025, $\Delta\lambda = \lambda_\text{model} - \lambda_\text{ref}$ in degrees, with page 4.1's epicycle as a ghost.

Over 2010–2025 the equant stays within 0.9° of the real Mars, where page 4.1's epicycle was off by up to 34°. Its mean motion came from oppositions 79 years apart, and over the two centuries 1926–2126 it stays within about a degree.

The loops were page 4.1's residual, so they are the second test. Figure 6 repeats Figure 5 of page 4.1 with the equant's loops in place of the epicycle's single value. The eleven loops of 1986–2009, outside the fit, are within 1.2° of the real ones. The model's oppositions are to the mean Sun, so (b) compares the times between the real oppositions to the mean Sun, and the model's agree with them within a day.

HistoryPtolemy's Mars in his own time

Ptolemy checked his result by computing back from it the arcs between his three oppositions, and found them "in agreement with the amount observed" (X 7). He then gave the positions of Mars at an epoch in the reign of Nabonassar (X 10), so that the model could be run to any date.

Run against the reference sky, his Mars of AD 130–139 stays within about 2° of the real planet and is on average a degree behind it. His mean ecliptic longitude of Mars is a degree behind in those years, as his mean Sun is (page 2.1), and the planet carries that error.

5 · ResidualThe epicycle and the Sun

The epicycle's radius KP follows the mean Sun, which moves uniformly, while the real Sun does not (page 2.1). To see how much of the error comes from that, we turn the epicycle instead by the real Sun, with its direction and its distance from the reference sky, and fit again.

The model then stays within 32′ of Mars, and its eccentricity becomes 0.0935. The theory behind the reference sky describes Mars by an ellipse of eccentricity 0.0934, which the equant's $e$ matches to first order.

Most of the rest is the second-order difference between the equant and the ellipse. At the refitted $e$ it reaches about 9′ in the direction of K, 7.5′ from the term $e^2/4$, and near opposition it is almost tripled by the factor $1/(1 - r)$ of page 4.1.

So the model needs the Sun's own unevenness in the epicycle of Mars. In Ptolemy's models the epicycles of Mars, Jupiter and Saturn all keep step with the mean Sun, and the centres of the epicycles of Venus and Mercury move with it (page 4.1). Why a planet's motion should be tied to the Sun at all is the question of page 4.3.

Exercises

Expand the equant and Kepler's ellipse to second order in $e$ and compare them term by term. For the ellipse, with $M$ and the eccentric anomaly $E$ (not page 2.2's equation of time) counted from the apogee, the point is at $(e + \cos E,\ \sqrt{1 - e^2}\,\sin E)$ from the focus O, its distance is $1 + e\cos E$, and the area law gives Kepler's equation $M = E + e\sin E$. Then find the greatest differences for Mars, $e$ = 0.1005, and for the Sun, $e$ = 0.0167, and compare page 2.1's eccentric.

Invert Kepler's equation to $E \approx M - e\sin M + \tfrac12 e^2\sin 2M$. The direction of the point from O is $E - e\sin E + \tfrac14 e^2\sin 2E$ to second order (Fitzpatrick (4.21), counted there from the perigee). For the equant, expand QK first, then use $\arctan x \approx x$, which is enough to second order.

Equant: $\text{QK} = 1 - e\cos M - \tfrac12 e^2\sin^2 M$. Then $q = -\arctan\big(2e\sin M/(\text{QK} + 2e\cos M)\big)$ with $\text{QK} + 2e\cos M \approx 1 + e\cos M$ gives $q \approx -2e\sin M\,(1 - e\cos M) = -2e\sin M + e^2\sin 2M$. The distance squared is $\text{QK}^2 + 4e\,\text{QK}\cos M + 4e^2 \approx 1 + 2e\cos M + 3e^2 \sin^2 M + e^2\cos^2 M$, whose root is $1 + e\cos M + \tfrac32 e^2 \sin^2 M$.

Ellipse: $E \approx M - e\sin M + \tfrac12 e^2\sin 2M$. The direction from O, $\operatorname{atan2}\big(\sqrt{1 - e^2}\sin E,\ e + \cos E\big)$, is to second order $E - e\sin E + \tfrac14 e^2\sin 2E$. With $\sin E \approx \sin M - \tfrac12 e\sin 2M$ this is $M - 2e\sin M + \tfrac54 e^2\sin 2M$, so $q \approx -2e\sin M + \tfrac54 e^2\sin 2M$; the distance $1 + e\cos E$ is $1 + e\cos M + e^2\sin^2 M$.

The equant less the ellipse is $-\tfrac14 e^2\sin 2M$ in direction and $\tfrac12 e^2\sin^2 M$ in distance. For Mars, $e^2/4$ is 8.7′ and the exact greatest difference 10.5′; the distance differs by at most 0.0050. Page 2.1's eccentric, $q = -2e\sin M + 2e^2\sin 2M$, differs from the ellipse by $\tfrac34 e^2\sin 2M$, 26.0′ (exactly 31.4′). For the Sun the equant would differ from the ellipse by 0.24′ and the eccentric by 0.72′, the value of page 2.1's Today note.

Redo Ptolemy's computation of X 7. His three oppositions of Mars were at Gemini 21° (81°), Leo 28° 50′ (148.833°) and Sagittarius 2° 34′ (242.567°), and between them the mean longitude advanced 81° 44′ (81.733°) and 95° 28′ (95.467°). With OC = $e$ and OQ = $2e$, find $2e$ and the apogee, first to first order, then exactly.

To first order the direction of K is $\bar\lambda - X\sin\bar\lambda + Y\cos\bar\lambda$ (in radians), with $X = 2e\cos\lambda_A$ and $Y = 2e\sin\lambda_A$. Subtract the equations of neighbouring oppositions, and put the observed longitudes in the sines and cosines. For the exact solution use Newton's method on the three equations.

The observed arcs, 67.833° and 93.733°, fall short of the mean ones by 13.900° and 1.733°. To first order this gives two linear equations in $X$ and $Y$, whose solution is $2e$ = 0.218 (13.1 where the deferent is 60) and the apogee at 111.2°.

The first order puts the observed longitudes where the mean ones belong, and at Ptolemy's three oppositions the two differ by $q$, up to 8.6°: too much for an eccentricity of 0.2. Newton's method on the three exact equations, unknowns $e$, $\lambda_A$ and $\bar\lambda$ at the third opposition, converges to $2e$ = 0.20001, 12.000 where the deferent is 60, and the apogee at 115.49°, Cancer 25° 30′, Ptolemy's values. The mean anomaly at the third opposition is 135.66°, against Ptolemy's 135° 39′ (135.65°).

Keep OQ = 0.2007 and $r$ = 0.658 and move C in Figure 4. Before you move it, predict where the loops will be longest, at the apogee or at the perigee, with C at the Earth, and with C at Q. Then check, and compare with Ptolemy's own arcs for Mars at greatest and least distance (XII 4): 19° 53′ 32″ (19.89°) and 11° 12′ 14″ (11.20°).

Use the two first-order results of section 2: the speed of K seen from O depends only on OQ, its distance only on OC.

With C at O the distance does not change, and only the speed does: K is slowest at the apogee, so the loops are longest there, 31.9°, and almost vanish at the perigee, 1.4°. With C at Q the distance changes as much as the speed, and the nearness counts for more: 11.0° at the apogee and 31.8° at the perigee. With the fitted C the loops are 20.0° and 10.9°.

Ptolemy's own model, with his motions, 12, 6 and 39.5, gives 19.85° and 11.30° at the apsides, close to his 19.89° and 11.20°, which he computed with a few approximations of his own.
