Act III ended with the Moon placed well enough to find every eclipse of a decade. The planets need something the Sun and the Moon did not: once in every return to the same place relative to the Sun, each of them stops among the stars, goes back westward for weeks, stops again and goes on.

Ptolemy accounted for this with an epicycle that keeps step with the Sun. With it we can follow how his model makes Mars, Jupiter and Saturn go back when they are opposite the Sun, and how far. Fitted to Mars, the epicycle places the planet within 35°, where a planet moving uniformly would be up to 53° off, and it shows what is still missing: the loops of Mars are not all the same size.

1 · ObservationLoops, and when they happen

Mars moves eastward along the zodiac by about half a degree a day on average, once round in a little less than two years, but in the winter of 2024–2025 it turned back. On 6 December 2024 it stopped among the stars of Cancer, went back westward into Gemini, and stopped again on 24 February 2025, 19.2° further west and 79 days later, before going on eastward. The two stops are the stations, and the westward motion between them is the planet's retrograde motion.

The path makes a loop because Mars also moves a little north and south of the ecliptic, between about 0.9° and 4.3° north of it over these six months. This page follows only its ecliptic longitude, which goes forward, back and forward again, and leaves the ecliptic latitude aside, as Ptolemy does in his theory of the motion in longitude (Almagest IX 2).

Every planet goes back in this way, once in every return to the same place relative to the Sun. The table gives the loops of the forty years 2001 to 2040 in the reference sky: how far each planet went back, for how long, and how often. The loops of Jupiter, Saturn and Venus hardly change in size, those of Mars and Mercury change a great deal.

Mars 10.0°–19.5° 60–81 d 764–810 d opposition
Jupiter 9.8°–10.1° 118–124 d 395–403 d opposition
Saturn 6.6°–7.0° 134–141 d 377–379 d opposition
Venus 15.4°–16.5° 41–43 d 580–588 d inferior conjunction
Mercury 8.6°–16.3° 20–24 d 106–129 d inferior conjunction

Mars, Jupiter and Saturn are in the middle of every loop when they are opposite the Sun, at opposition: in each of the 93 loops of the table the opposition falls within two days of the middle.

These three can be seen at any angle from the Sun, while Venus and Mercury never get far from it, Venus no more than about 47° in ecliptic longitude and Mercury no more than about 28°. They pass the Sun's ecliptic longitude twice in each return, once while going back, at inferior conjunction, in the middle of the loop, and once while moving fastest, at superior conjunction. Ptolemy makes the same division, between the three planets "which can reach any elongation from the sun" and the two that cannot (XII 1).

Two anomalies

Figure 2 shows two kinds of unevenness, which Ptolemy calls the planet's two anomalies, "one ... related to the sun, the other to the position in the ecliptic" (IX 2). The anomaly related to the Sun is the shape that every curve shares: the planet slows down, goes back and speeds up again in the same way each time it comes round to the same place relative to the Sun. The anomaly related to the ecliptic, the zodiacal anomaly, is the spread between the curves: the loops of Mars, and the times between them, depend on where in the zodiac the opposition falls.

Ptolemy points out one more feature of the anomaly related to the Sun: "the time from greatest speed to mean is always greater than the time from mean speed to least" (IX 5). After its conjunction with the Sun in November 2023, Mars took about ten and a half months to slow to its mean motion, and then only three and a half months to reach its least speed, going backwards, at the opposition of January 2025.

2 · ModelThe epicycle that keeps step with the Sun

Deferent and epicycle

For the anomaly related to the Sun Ptolemy uses an epicycle, as for the Moon on page 3.1. In its simplest form, the model of this page, it is a small circle whose centre K moves uniformly round the Earth on a larger circle, the deferent, while the planet P moves uniformly round the epicycle (IX 5). The epicycle is older than Ptolemy: he credits Apollonius of Perge, at the end of the third century BC, and other mathematicians with a theorem on the stations of a planet on an epicycle (XII 1).

For Mars, Jupiter and Saturn, Ptolemy ties the epicycle to the Sun. He finds that for each of them "the sum of the mean motions in longitude and anomaly ... equals the mean motion of the sun", and concludes that the radius from K to the planet stays parallel to the line from the Earth to the mean Sun (X 6).

Let the deferent have radius 1, the unit of every length on this page; Ptolemy makes it 60, so his radii are $60\,r$. The centre K moves at the planet's mean ecliptic longitude $\bar\lambda = L_0 + n\,d$. The planet's angle on the epicycle is its anomaly $\mu$, counted from the epicycle's apogee, the point farthest from the Earth, in the direction of the motion, eastward, at the rate $n_\mu$. With $\hat u(\lambda)$ the unit vector at ecliptic longitude $\lambda$, as on page 3.1, the planet is at

$$\vec{OP} = \hat u(\bar\lambda) + r\,\hat u(\bar\lambda + \mu).$$

The radius KP points to the ecliptic longitude $\bar\lambda + \mu$, so it stays parallel to the line to the mean Sun, at $\bar\lambda_\odot$, if

$$\mu = \bar\lambda_\odot - \bar\lambda, \qquad n + n_\mu = n_\odot.$$

The second equation is Ptolemy's relation between the mean motions. The motion in anomaly is what the mean Sun gains on the planet's centre, so the planet comes round to the same place on its epicycle each time the mean Sun comes round to the same place relative to K.

The equation of the epicycle

In a frame turned to the direction of K, the planet is at $(1 + r\cos\mu,\ r\sin\mu)$, and it is seen from the Earth at

$$\lambda = \bar\lambda + \eta, \qquad \eta = \operatorname{atan2}(r\sin\mu,\ 1 + r\cos\mu).$$

We call $\eta$ the equation of the epicycle. It is the function of pages 2.1 and 3.1 with the sign reversed, because $\mu$ is counted the other way: $\eta$ is positive from the apogee to the perigee. Its greatest value is $\arcsin r$, where the line of sight touches the epicycle, at $\cos\mu = -r$. Ptolemy's epicycle of Mars is 39.5 where the deferent is 60, $r = 0.658$, so in his model Mars can be seen up to 41.2° from K.

The speed, and the direction of the motion

To see how the planet's speed varies we differentiate $\lambda = \bar\lambda + \eta$ with respect to time, with $d\bar\lambda/dt = n$ and $d\mu/dt = n_\mu$:

$$\frac{d\lambda}{dt} = n + n_\mu\,\frac{r\,(r + \cos\mu)}{1 + 2r\cos\mu + r^2}.$$

The planet is fastest at the apogee of the epicycle, where the speed is $n + n_\mu\,r/(1 + r)$, and slowest at the perigee, $n - n_\mu\,r/(1 - r)$. It moves at its mean rate $n$ where the fraction vanishes, at $\cos\mu = -r$. From the apogee to that point the anomaly grows by $\arccos(-r)$, more than 90°, and from there to the perigee by less than 90°, so the time from the greatest speed to the mean is the longer, as Ptolemy observed.

This fixes the direction of the motion on the epicycle. Had the planet moved westward near the apogee, as the Moon does, it would be slowest at the apogee and fastest at the perigee, and the longer time would come between the least speed and the mean. Ptolemy reasons the same way: the planet must move "not as the moon does, in advance [with respect to the motion] of the universe, but instead towards the rear" (IX 5), that is, eastward.

Why the planet goes back at opposition

At the perigee the planet's motion on the epicycle runs against the motion of K, and the planet goes back if the first is the faster:

$$n_\mu\,\frac{r}{1 - r} > n \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad r > \frac{n}{n + n_\mu}.$$

Ptolemy states this condition (XII 1). For Mars the right side is 0.532, and Ptolemy's 0.658 is well above it, so his Mars goes back once in every turn of the anomaly.

The perigee is at $\mu = 180^\circ$, where K is opposite the mean Sun. Since KP points to the mean Sun, the planet then lies on the line from the Earth through K, opposite the mean Sun too. So in the model every loop is centred on the opposition to the mean Sun. From 2018 to 2033 each opposition of Mars to the mean Sun came within a day and a half of its opposition to the real Sun.

The stations

The stations are where the speed is zero. Writing $k = n_\mu / n$, the condition $d\lambda/dt = 0$ becomes $1 + 2r\cos\mu + r^2 + k\,r\,(r + \cos\mu) = 0$, so the first station is at

$$\cos\mu_s = -\frac{1 + r^2\,(1 + k)}{r\,(2 + k)},$$

and the second at $360^\circ - \mu_s$, symmetric about the perigee. Between them the anomaly advances by $2\,(180^\circ - \mu_s)$, in $2\,(180^\circ - \mu_s)/n_\mu$ days, while K advances $n$ times that and the equation of the epicycle falls from $\eta(\mu_s)$ to $-\eta(\mu_s)$. The planet goes back from the ecliptic longitude $\lambda_1$ of the first station to the $\lambda_2$ of the second, the arc

$$\lambda_1 - \lambda_2 = 2\,\eta(\mu_s) - 2\,(180^\circ - \mu_s)\,\frac{n}{n_\mu}.$$

The arc grows with $r$, and vanishes at the threshold $r = n/(n + n_\mu)$, where the two stations meet at the perigee. Figure 3 starts with Ptolemy's epicycle of Mars.

Venus and Mercury

For the two planets that stay near the Sun, Ptolemy puts K on the line to the mean Sun: their mean motion in ecliptic longitude is the mean Sun's (IX 3), $\bar\lambda = \bar\lambda_\odot$, and the anomaly $\mu$ goes round at a rate of its own. The same equation of the epicycle applies. Seen from the Earth, the planet can then be at most $\arcsin r$ from the mean Sun, where the line of sight touches the epicycle. Ptolemy's epicycle of Venus, 43.17 where the deferent is 60, gives 46.0°, close to the greatest elongations of section 1. The perigee comes at inferior conjunction, and there the planet goes back.

The synodic period

The time from one opposition to the next, or from one inferior conjunction to the next, is the synodic period $S$, and the anomaly makes one turn in it, $n_\mu = 360^\circ/S$. For an outer planet, with $P_\lambda$ its period in ecliptic longitude and $Y$ the year, $n + n_\mu = n_\odot$ becomes

$$\frac{1}{P_\lambda} + \frac{1}{S} = \frac{1}{Y}.$$

For Mars, $P_\lambda$ = 686.93 days, and $S$ = 779.9 days, the long-run mean around which the intervals of section 1, from 764 to 810 days, scatter. Exercise 2 finds the synodic period of every planet.

3 · FitMars from its oppositions and its loops

The model of Mars has three parameters: the mean motion $n$, the mean ecliptic longitude $L_0$ at one moment, and the epicycle $r$, with the mean Sun of page 1.3. Mars has no opposition in 2026, so we fit the model to the eight oppositions from 2018 to 2033, which take Mars once round the zodiac.

The mean motion

A rate is fixed best by two observations far apart, since the error of each is divided by the time between them. Sixteen years are too few for this, so we take the mean motion of the reference Mars, $n$ = 0.52407° a day, a period in ecliptic longitude of 686.93 days.

The mean ecliptic longitude from the oppositions

At an opposition to the mean Sun the planet lies on the line from the Earth through K, so the ecliptic longitude observed there is $\bar\lambda$ itself, whatever the size of the epicycle. Ptolemy chose these observations for that reason: at them "the planet is viewed, in theory, [along the line] through the centre of the epicycle, just as if its motion on the epicycle did not exist" (X 6).

Each of the eight oppositions of Mars to the mean Sun, carried back to J2000.0 at the rate $n$, gives a value of $L_0$. They lie from 11° below their mean to 12° above it, because the zodiacal anomaly of section 1 moves the planet away from $\bar\lambda$ by different amounts in different parts of the zodiac. Their mean is $L_0$ = 354.79°, 38′ from the mean ecliptic longitude of the reference Mars.

The epicycle from the loops

The epicycle shows most clearly in the loops: the larger it is, the farther the planet goes back. The arc of a loop is also the difference of two positions only weeks apart, so an error in $\bar\lambda$ hardly changes it. The formula of section 2 gives the arc for each $r$, and inverted it gives $r$ from an observed arc.

The eight loops of 2018–2033 went back by 16.1° on average, which gives $r$ = 0.657, or 39.4 where the deferent is 60, against Ptolemy's 39.5.

Each loop on its own gives a different epicycle, from about 0.63 for the short loop of 2018 to 0.67 for the long one of 2027, so no single epicycle fits them all.

HistoryPtolemy's mean motions and oppositions

For the mean motions Ptolemy starts from period relations "computed by Hipparchus": for Mars, 37 returns in anomaly in 79 years, in which the planet goes 42 times round the zodiac (IX 3). Toomer notes that these are relations "well-known (to us)" from Babylonian astronomy, which Ptolemy seems not to have recognised as such. Ptolemy corrected them by comparing positions centuries apart, adding 3.22 days to the 79 years and 3°10′ (3.17°) to the 42 turns; Exercise 2 works out his corrected relation.

In the older records, "the more continuous series of observations concern stations and phases", first and last visibilities near the Sun (IX 2). Toomer takes them to be Babylonian observations, probably known to Ptolemy only through Hipparchus' compilation. Ptolemy found the stations hard to use: they "cannot be fixed at an exact moment, since the local motion of the planet for several days both before and after the actual station is too small to be observable" (IX 2).

He built the theory of each outer planet on three oppositions to the mean Sun that he observed himself (X 7 for Mars), since only there "we find the ecliptic anomaly isolated", the conjunctions being invisible (X 6). He took the size of the epicycle from one further position away from opposition, sighted three days after the third (X 8).

4 · TestThe epicycle against the real Mars

To see how much one epicycle explains, Figure 4 gives the error curve of the model fitted to 2018–2033, $\Delta\lambda = \lambda_\text{model} - \lambda_\text{ref}$, now in degrees, beside that of the uniform planet, one that moves at $\bar\lambda$ with no epicycle.

Over 2018–2033 the epicycle is wrong by up to 35°, a fortnight before the opposition of December 2022, against 53° for the uniform planet, and its root mean square error is a third of the uniform planet's. We took its mean motion from the reference, so the error stays the same size over the two centuries around 2026.

The errors are largest near the oppositions, 32° at the opposition of December 2022 itself, and stay within 7.3° at the conjunctions. Near the perigee an error in the place of K is magnified. Since $\mu = \bar\lambda_\odot - \bar\lambda$, the equation of the epicycle gives there

$$\frac{\partial\lambda}{\partial\bar\lambda} = 1 - \frac{d\eta}{d\mu} = \frac{1}{1 - r},$$

almost 3. At the opposition of December 2022 the real Mars was 12° ahead of K, and the model put it 32° behind the real planet.

HistoryHipparchus and the models of his time

Before Ptolemy, the astronomers who used epicycles and eccentrics for the planets had built their proofs, he says, "on the basis of a single unvarying anomaly and retrograde arc" (IX 2). Hipparchus, he says, did not even begin a theory of the planets, "not at least in his writings which have come down to us". He compiled the planetary observations, and showed "by means of these that the phenomena were not in agreement with the hypotheses of the astronomers of that time" (IX 2): each planet had two anomalies, and retrograde arcs "which are not constant".

5 · ResidualLoops of different sizes

The loops of Mars are long in one part of the zodiac and short in the opposite part, while the model's loop never changes. Figure 5 sets each loop of 2001–2040 against the ecliptic longitude of its opposition: Mars goes back by about 10° in one part and almost 20° in the other, where the model always goes back by 16°. The times between oppositions change with the loops.

So the zodiacal anomaly changes the loops as well as the place of K, and a simple epicycle cannot follow it: without an eccentricity its arcs are all of one length, as Neugebauer remarks (HAMA p. 191).

To make the loops change size round the zodiac, the model needs an unevenness that depends on the planet's ecliptic longitude, in the distance of K from the Earth and in its speed. Page 4.2 gives Ptolemy's, an eccentric deferent and the equant.

Exercises

Show Apollonius' form of the condition for a station (XII 1): at a station, half the chord that the line of sight cuts from the epicycle, divided by the distance from the Earth to the planet, equals $n/n_\mu$, the speed of K over the speed of the planet on the epicycle. Then take Ptolemy's Mars, $r = 39.5/60$ with his mean motions $n$ = 0.5240597° and $n_\mu$ = 0.4615756° a day (IX 3). Find the stations from the formula of section 2, how far Mars goes back and for how long, and compare with Ptolemy's 16°18′44″ (16.312°) and about 73 days at mean distance (XII 4). How small could the epicycle be before Mars stopped going back?

Work in a frame that turns with the line OK, at the rate $n$. There K stands still and the planet goes round the epicycle at $n_\mu$. How fast does the planet turn about O in that frame?

In the turning frame the planet moves at the speed $n_\mu r$, perpendicular to KP. Let T be the foot of the perpendicular from K to the line of sight, the midpoint of the chord. The component of the velocity across the line of sight is $n_\mu r\cos\alpha$, where $\alpha$ is the angle between KP and the line, and $r\cos\alpha$ = TP, half the chord. So in that frame the planet turns about O at $n_\mu\,\text{TP}/\text{OP}$, backwards on the near side of the epicycle. In the sky it stands still when this cancels the turning of the frame, $n_\mu\,\text{TP}/\text{OP} = n$:

$$\frac{\text{TP}}{\text{OP}} = \frac{n}{n_\mu}.$$

With Ptolemy's numbers $k = n_\mu/n$ = 0.88077 (his ratio is 0.88083, XII 4), so $\cos\mu_s = -0.95709$ and $\mu_s$ = 163.15°, 16.85° before the perigee. There $\eta(\mu_s)$ = 27.28°. Between the stations the anomaly advances 33.69°, in 73.0 days, during which K advances 38.25°, so Mars goes back $2 \times 27.28^\circ - 38.25^\circ$ = 16.312° = 16°18.7′ in 73.0 days: Ptolemy's 16°18′44″ and about 73 days. At the station OP = 0.4162 and TP = 0.4726, and TP/OP = 1.1354 = $n/n_\mu$.

Mars stops going back when $r = n/(n + n_\mu)$ = 0.5317, 31.9 where the deferent is 60. Ptolemy's Jupiter and Saturn, with epicycles of 11.5 and 6.5, are far above their thresholds, 0.0843 and 0.0340, and go back by 9.9° in 120 days and 7.2° in 138 days, against 10.0° in 121 days and 6.8° in 137 days on average in 2001–2040.

Measured against the stars rather than from γ, the relation $n + n_\mu = n_\odot$ holds with the periods of return to the same star. The mean Sun comes back to the same star every 365.256 days, the mean ecliptic longitude of Mars every 686.98 days, of Jupiter every 4332.59 days and of Saturn every 10·759.22 days. For Venus and Mercury, whose K follows the mean Sun, the radius KP of the epicycle comes back to the same star every 224.70 and 87.97 days. Find the five synodic periods and compare them with the intervals of section 1. Then take Ptolemy's relation for Mars (IX 3; HAMA p. 151): 37 returns in anomaly in 79 years and 3.217 days, years of 365.24667 days, during which Mars goes 42 times round the zodiac and 3°10′ (3.167°) more. Find the synodic period and the period in ecliptic longitude, and compare them with 779.936 and 686.930 days. Why must the number of years be the sum of the other two numbers?

For Venus and Mercury, KP turns at $n_\odot + n_\mu$.

With $Y_\text{s}$ the sidereal year and $P_\lambda$ the period against the stars, for an outer planet $1/S = 1/Y_\text{s} - 1/P_\lambda$, and for an inner one, whose KP turns at $n_\odot + n_\mu$, $1/S = 1/P_\lambda - 1/Y_\text{s}$. Mercury: 115.88 days; Venus: 583.92 days; Mars: 779.94 days; Jupiter: 398.88 days; Saturn: 378.09 days. Each lies within the intervals of section 1.

Ptolemy's 79 years and 3.217 days are 28·857.70 days (he writes 28·857.72), so his synodic period is $28857.70 / 37 = 779.938$ days, 2.6 minutes from the modern 779.936, and his period in ecliptic longitude $28857.70 / 42.0088 = 686.944$ days, 21 minutes longer than 686.930. Multiplying $n + n_\mu = n_\odot$ by the length of the period gives the turns of K plus the turns of the anomaly equal to the turns of the mean Sun, that is, to the years: $42 + 37 = 79$, a rule Ptolemy states himself (IX 3). The fractions agree as well: in 3.217 days his mean Sun moves 3.170°, the 3°10′ by which Mars goes beyond its 42 turns.

Use the model fitted to 2018–2033 ($L_0$ = 354.79° at J2000.0, $n$ = 0.524071° a day, $r$ = 0.657, and page 1.3's mean Sun) to predict the oppositions of Mars of 2018 and of 2027: the moment when K is opposite the mean Sun, the ecliptic longitude there, and the loop. Compare with the reference sky, where Mars was at opposition on 27 July 2018 at 304.1° and went back by 10.6° in 62 days, and on 19 February 2027 at 150.7°, going back by 19.5° in 81 days. Why are the two predictions wrong by different amounts?

The model's opposition is where $\bar\lambda = \bar\lambda_\odot - 180^\circ$, and its loop is the same at every opposition.

The model puts the opposition of 2018 on 4 August at 313.5°, 8.8 days late and 9.4° too far east, and that of 2027 on 18 February at 148.5°, 0.9 days early and 2.3° too far west. Both of its loops go back by 16.1° in 73 days, too far in 2018 and not far enough in 2027.

The model has K move uniformly, while the real Mars runs ahead of the uniform motion in some parts of the zodiac and behind it in others. At its opposition to the mean Sun of 2018 the real Mars was 4.3° behind K, so the mean Sun reached K's opposite point about nine days after it reached the real planet's; in 2027 the real Mars was only 0.9° from K. That unevenness, and the loops that change with it, is the zodiacal anomaly, which the model leaves out.
