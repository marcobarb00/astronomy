Predicting the Sky

From the celestial sphere to Kepler’s ellipse

When I studied astronomy, the old models of the sky kept coming up: Hipparchus’ Sun on an off-center circle, Ptolemy’s epicycles and equant, Copernicus’ circles about the Sun, Kepler’s ellipse. The books and courses I learned from gave each of them a paragraph and a drawing, and then moved on to the modern theory.

So these models always stayed a little mysterious to me. I kept wondering how anyone had thought of them, with the naked eye and a few simple instruments, and above all where their numbers came from: the size of an epicycle, the direction of an apogee, the rate of a mean motion. A drawing shows the shape of a model, but not how its author found it or chose its parameters.

This course is my attempt to answer those questions. Each page rebuilds one model as its author conceived it: the observations it starts from, its geometry, and how its parameters are fitted to the sky. Then we test it against a modern reference sky, to see how close it comes and where it fails, and the next page starts from what it still gets wrong.

By the end you will be able to compute where the Sun, the Moon and a planet are on any date, using models you have derived yourself, and you will know how wrong each one is and why.

The astronomers

The story runs from Hipparchus and Ptolemy to Copernicus, Tycho and Kepler. Some steps refine an astronomer’s own model, as Ptolemy did for the Moon; others change its form, as Kepler did.

Circles come close: the Sun’s circle fitted to 2026 stays within two arcminutes of the real Sun all year, and Ptolemy’s Moon fitted to the same year within two degrees. Much of what we meet on the way, from the coordinates of the sky to the equation of time, precession and the limits of eclipses, is used as it is today.

The planets show the limits of circles most clearly. Kepler found his ellipse in the motion of Mars, and the same law then holds for the Earth’s orbit, and so for the Sun. The Moon needs more than an ellipse, because the Sun’s pull keeps changing its orbit; the epilogue follows the story from there.
