An interactive course in classical positional astronomy

Predicting the Sky

From the celestial sphere to Kepler’s ellipse

For almost two thousand years, astronomers tried to say where the Sun, the Moon and the planets would be on a given night. This course rebuilds their models, from Hipparchus and Ptolemy to Copernicus, Tycho and Kepler, as each author conceived them, and tests every one against a modern reference sky.

By the end you will be able to compute where the Sun, the Moon and a planet are on any date, using models you have derived yourself, and you will know how wrong each one is and why.

Begin with 1.1, The turning sky › or choose a page below

Circles come close: the Sun’s circle fitted to 2026 stays within two arcminutes of the real Sun all year, and Ptolemy’s Moon fitted to the same year within two degrees. Much of what we meet on the way, from the coordinates of the sky to the equation of time and precession, is used as it is today.

The planets show the limits of circles most clearly. Kepler found his ellipse in the motion of Mars, and the same law then holds for the Earth’s orbit, and so for the Sun. The Moon needs more than an ellipse; the epilogue follows the story from there.
