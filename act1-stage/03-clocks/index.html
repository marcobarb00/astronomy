Page 1.2 placed a star in the observer's sky with $H = \theta - \alpha$, where $\theta$ is the angle the sky has turned since the vernal point γ crossed the meridian. There $\theta$ was set by hand. This page computes it from a clock and a date.

With $\theta$ known, two predictions follow: when any star crosses the meridian at any place, and how long the Sun stays above the horizon on any date and at any latitude. Section 4 tests both against the real sky.

1 · ObservationThe day of the stars and the day of the Sun

Time a star crossing the meridian on two nights with a clock that keeps ordinary time. It comes back after 23 h 56 min 04.09 s, not 24 hours, so each night it crosses 3 min 55.91 s earlier than the night before. Over a month that adds up to almost two hours.

That is why the stars seen at a given hour change with the seasons. From Rome, Betelgeuse in Orion crosses the meridian at midnight (local mean time, defined in section 2) around 20 December, at 22:00 around 19 January, and at 20:00 around 18 February.

The Sun keeps the clock's 24 hours, but only on average. The interval from one noon to the next, the true solar day, varied in 2026 from 23 h 59 min 38.6 s (the day ending 18 September) to 24 h 00 min 29.8 s (ending 23 December). Its average over the year was 24 h to within 0.06 s, because the clock is defined that way (section 2).

Ptolemy states both facts in one definition. The sky's turn, "the return of a given point on the equator" to the meridian, takes 360 time-degrees; a mean solar day takes "360 time-degrees of one revolution of the equator plus approximately $0^\circ 59'$ time-degrees, which is the amount of the mean motion of the sun during that period" (Almagest III 9).

A time-degree is the time the equator takes to turn $1^\circ$ across the meridian, $\tfrac{1}{15}$ of an hour or 4 minutes (Toomer's introduction). So Ptolemy's $0^\circ 59'$ ($0.983^\circ$) is 3.93 minutes, the difference between the two days observed above.

Copernicus counts the same difference over a year: "in the course of an entire year 365 rotations take place. These are increased by approximately a whole additional rotation as the result of a daily prolongation due to the apparent advance of the sun" (De Revolutionibus III 26 [f. 96v]).

2 · ModelSidereal time and mean solar time

The sidereal day and the solar day

The model keeps the turning sky of page 1.1: the stars turn about P at a constant rate. Their turn takes one sidereal day. Against the stars, the Sun moves east along the ecliptic by about $1^\circ$ a day, so it returns to the meridian a little later than they do.

To make this precise, let the Sun's right ascension grow uniformly, by $n$ degrees a day. With $H = \theta - \alpha$ from page 1.2, its hour angle is $H_\odot = \theta - \alpha_\odot$, and the two angles grow at the rates

$$\frac{d\theta}{dt} = \omega, \qquad \frac{dH_\odot}{dt} = \omega - n.$$

The Sun's day, from one meridian crossing to the next, is the time in which $H_\odot$ grows by $360^\circ$; the sidereal day is the time in which $\theta$ does. During one year of $Y$ solar days the Sun goes once round the sky, $nY = 360^\circ$, so in that time $H_\odot$ grows by $360^\circ\,Y$ and $\theta$ by $360^\circ\,(Y + 1)$:

$$Y \text{ solar days} = Y + 1 \text{ sidereal days}, \qquad P_\text{sid} = \frac{Y}{Y + 1} \times 24\ \text{h}.$$

With the tropical year $Y = 365.24219$ days (section 3), a year holds 366.24219 sidereal days, and one sidereal day lasts 23 h 56 min 04.091 s, the turn observed in section 1.

Sidereal time and the hour angle

A clock that follows the stars reads the angle $\theta$ directly. Its reading is the local sidereal time, $\theta$, usually expressed in hours ($15^\circ$ to the hour): it is $0^\text{h}$ when γ crosses the meridian and runs through $24^\text{h}$ in one sidereal day.

A star of right ascension $\alpha$ crosses the meridian when $H = \theta - \alpha = 0$, that is, when the sidereal time equals its right ascension. The meridian always shows the stars whose right ascension is the current sidereal time. This is the reason right ascension is counted in hours (page 1.2).

The sidereal time depends on the place. Seen against the stars, the meridian of an observer at geographic longitude $\ell$ east of Greenwich lies $\ell$ further east than the meridian of Greenwich. A meridian sits at right ascension $\alpha = \theta$ (page 1.2), so, with $\theta_G$ the sidereal time at Greenwich,

$$\theta = \theta_G + \ell, \qquad H = \theta_G + \ell - \alpha.$$

Longitudes are counted positive east on this site. Meeus counts them positive west, and Fitzpatrick writes $\varphi$ for the longitude (and $L$ for the latitude): their formulas have $-\ell$ where these have $+\ell$.

Mean solar time and Universal Time

Our clocks do not keep sidereal time; they keep mean solar time. The real Sun is a poor timekeeper, as the uneven solar days of section 1 show, so astronomers defined a fictitious mean Sun that moves along the equator at a constant rate, with right ascension equal to the Sun's mean longitude $\bar\lambda$, which grows by $n$ every day:

$$\alpha_m = \bar\lambda = L_0 + n\,d.$$

Here $d$ is the time in days of UT since the epoch J2000.0, 2000 January 1 at 12:00 UT, and $L_0$ is the mean Sun's right ascension at that epoch. Their values are given after the derivation.

The mean solar time at a place is the hour angle of the mean Sun plus 12 hours, so that the day starts at midnight. At Greenwich it is Universal Time, UT. Elsewhere the local mean time is $\text{UT} + \ell / 15^\circ$ hours: at Rome ($\ell = 12.50^\circ$) it is UT + 49 min 59 s.

Civil clocks keep the mean time of a reference meridian instead (Central European Time is UT + 1 h), so at Rome local mean noon falls at 12:10:01 CET.

The sidereal time from the clock

A clock gives the hour angle of the mean Sun; the sidereal time is the hour angle of γ. Four steps lead from one to the other. Each is checked on one moment, 20 April 2026 at 20:00 UT, at Greenwich.

Step 1: the sidereal time is the hour angle of the mean Sun plus its right ascension. Page 1.2's relation $H = \theta - \alpha$ holds for every point of the sky, the mean Sun included: $H_m = \theta_G - \alpha_m$ at Greenwich, so

$$\theta_G = H_m + \alpha_m.$$

Figure 2 shows the three angles on the equator. Going westward from the meridian, one meets the mean Sun after $H_m$, and γ after another $\alpha_m$, because the right ascension is counted eastward from γ to the mean Sun.

Step 2: the clock gives $H_m$. By the definition of mean solar time, the mean Sun crosses the meridian of Greenwich at 12:00 UT, and its hour angle grows by $15^\circ$ for every hour of UT:

$$H_m = 15^\circ\,(\text{UT} - 12\,\text{h}).$$

At 20:00 UT, $H_m = 15^\circ \times 8 = 120^\circ$.

Step 3: the date gives $\alpha_m$. On 20 April 2026 at 20:00 UT, $d = 9606.3333$ days, and $\alpha_m = L_0 + n\,d = 28.92^\circ$ modulo $360^\circ$. Then $\theta_G = 120^\circ + 28.92^\circ = 148.92^\circ$ ($L_0$ will be given later).

Step 4: $H_m$ in terms of $d$. To have a single formula in $d$, write the time of day with $d$ too. Because $d$ is counted from a noon, its whole part $N$ is the number of noons since the epoch, and its fraction is the time since the last noon:

$$d = N + \frac{\text{UT} - 12\,\text{h}}{24\,\text{h}}, \qquad 360^\circ\,d = 360^\circ N + 15^\circ\,(\text{UT} - 12\,\text{h}) = 360^\circ N + H_m.$$

Whole turns do not change a direction, so $H_m$ can be replaced by $360^\circ\,d$. On 20 April, $360^\circ \times 9606.3333$ is 9606 whole turns plus $120^\circ$, which is $H_m$. Putting steps 1 to 4 together,

$$\theta_G = 360^\circ\,d + L_0 + n\,d = L_0 + (360^\circ + n)\,d.$$

The rate has a simple meaning. In one day of UT the sky turns $360^\circ$ with respect to the mean Sun, which is what a solar day is, and the mean Sun moves $n$ further east from γ; so with respect to γ the sky turns $360^\circ + n$, the rate $\omega$ found at the start of this section.

The values of $L_0$ and $n$ that define Universal Time have been fixed by convention since 1984 (Meeus, ch. 12): $L_0 = 280.46061837^\circ$ and $n = 0.98564736629^\circ$ a day. Put into the formula above, they give

$$\theta_G = 280.46061837^\circ + 360.98564736629^\circ\, d, \qquad d = \text{JD(UT)} - 2451545.0.$$

This is the formula we will use. The rate gives $360^\circ / n = 365.24219$ days, the tropical year. Meeus adds two terms in $T^2$ and $T^3$, worth 6.3 ms in 2026, which we will not consider.

Tables of the Sun give a slightly larger mean longitude at J2000.0, $280.46646^\circ$ (Meeus 25.2), 21″ more than $L_0$. That value is the Sun's geometric position. The mean Sun of the clock follows the Sun as it is seen, which appears 20.5″ further west, the arc the Sun moves in the 8.3 minutes its light takes to reach the Earth (the aberration of light). The remaining 0.5″ is the difference between the two fits.

The 21″ is 1.4 s of time, too small to matter on this page. The clock formula always uses $L_0 = 280.46061837^\circ$; the model Sun of the length of the day (below) uses the Sun's mean longitude.

Page 1.2 placed Aldebaran in the sky of Rome on 1 January 2026 at 19:00 UT with $\theta = 38.94^\circ$, set by hand. The formula gives $\theta_G = 26.44^\circ$ at that moment, and with $\ell = 12.50^\circ$, $\theta = 38.94^\circ$ ($2^\text{h}35^\text{m}45^\text{s}$). Figure 3 starts from that moment.

The Julian Date

The formula needs the number of days $d$ between two dates. Calendars make that awkward: months of unequal length, leap years, and the Gregorian reform, after which 4 October 1582 was followed by 15 October. Astronomers therefore count days continuously.

The Julian Date JD is the number of days since noon UT on 1 January 4713 BC in the Julian calendar (a Monday), with the time of day as a fraction. J2000.0 is JD 2451545.0, and 1 January 2026 at 0 h UT is JD 2461041.5. In this count 4 and 15 October 1582 are consecutive days, JD 2299159.5 and 2299160.5.

A Julian Date starts at noon, as the astronomical day of Ptolemy did. He counts from the meridian crossing of the Sun, "not from its rising or setting", because the time of sunrise "varies according to the difference in longest or shortest day at the different latitudes", while the meridian behaves "the same at every place on earth" (Almagest III 9). "We establish all epochs at noon" (III 7).

Copernicus lists the other customs: the day began at sunrise for the Babylonians, at sunset for the Athenians, at midnight for the Romans and at noon for the Egyptians (De Revolutionibus III 26).

For a date in the calendar (year $Y$, month $M$, day $D$ with its fraction), Meeus (ch. 7) gives

$$\text{JD} = \lfloor 365.25\,(Y + 4716) \rfloor + \lfloor 30.6001\,(M + 1) \rfloor + D + B - 1524.5,$$

with January and February counted as months 13 and 14 of the previous year, and $B = 2 - \lfloor Y/100 \rfloor + \lfloor Y/400 \rfloor$ (Gregorian calendar) or $B = 0$ (Julian calendar).

Where the terms come from

Starting the year in March puts the leap day at the end of the year, so the days before a month do not depend on whether the year is a leap year. From March on, the months have 31, 30, 31, 30, 31 days, a pattern that repeats, averaging 30.6 days; $\lfloor 30.6001\,(M + 1) \rfloor$ counts them (the extra 0.0001 protects against rounding).

$\lfloor 365.25\,(Y + 4716) \rfloor$ counts the days of the past years with one leap day every four years, as in the Julian calendar; the shift by 4716 years keeps the argument positive for every date after 4713 BC. $B$ removes the three Gregorian leap days skipped every four centuries, and $-1524.5$ sets the origin.

Ptolemy had a continuous count too. He dated events in Egyptian years of exactly 365 days, counted from the first year of King Nabonassar, whose reign began the Babylonian records he used (Almagest III 7; Toomer's introduction).

From Nabonassar 1, Thoth 1, noon, to an autumn equinox he observed (in the 17th year of Hadrian, Athyr 7, two hours after noon) he counts "879 Egyptian years 66 days and 2 equinoctial hours" (III 7). These are 26 February 747 BC and 25 September AD 132. The Julian Dates of the two moments, JD 1448638.0 and 1769539.0833, differ by 320901.0833 days, and $879 \times 365 + 66 + \tfrac{2}{24}$ is the same number.

When a star crosses the meridian

A star crosses the meridian when $\theta = \alpha$. Starting from $\theta_{G,0}$, the Greenwich sidereal time at 0 h UT of the date, the sky still has to turn by $(\alpha - \ell - \theta_{G,0})$, reduced to $[0^\circ, 360^\circ)$, at $360.98564736629^\circ$ a day:

$$\text{UT of transit} = \frac{(\alpha - \ell - \theta_{G,0}) \bmod 360^\circ}{360.98564736629^\circ} \times 24\ \text{h}.$$

Adding one sidereal day gives the next transit, which usually falls on the next date. Once a year it still falls within the same date, because the sidereal day is shorter than 24 hours: on that date the star crosses the meridian twice.

Figure 4 plots the transits through a whole year as a star calendar, with the hours of night from the Sun's model of the next subsection. Each night the transit comes 3 min 56 s earlier, and the star returns to the same hour after a year.

The length of the day

The same clock gives the times of sunrise and sunset, once the Sun's declination is known. The model makes the simplest assumption: the Sun moves uniformly along the ecliptic, at the mean longitude, $\lambda = \bar\lambda$. Page 1.2 then gives its declination:

$$\sin\delta_\odot = \sin\varepsilon \sin\bar\lambda.$$

The model Sun crosses the meridian at mean noon, 12:00 local mean time, and its hour angle grows by $360^\circ$ in 24 hours, $15^\circ$ an hour. It sets at the hour angle $H_0$ of page 1.1, generalised to a horizon at altitude $h_0$:

$$\cos H_0 = \frac{\sin h_0 - \sin\varphi \sin\delta_\odot}{\cos\varphi \cos\delta_\odot}, \qquad \text{day} = \frac{2H_0}{15^\circ}\ \text{h}, \qquad \text{sunrise, sunset} = 12\,\text{h} \mp \frac{H_0}{15^\circ}.$$

Sunrise and sunset are counted when the top edge of the Sun touches the horizon. Its centre is then $h_0 = -50'$ below the true horizon: 34′ of refraction and the Sun's radius of 16′, as found in page 1.1's third exercise.

At the equator the day lasts 12 h 07 min all year: the extra 7 minutes are the 50′. The table gives the model's day at three latitudes. Ptolemy's longest day at Rhodes, $14\tfrac12$ hours with the geometric horizon (page 1.1), becomes 14 h 39 min with $h_0 = -50'$.

Place March equinox June solstice December solstice
Alexandria ($31.2^\circ$) 12 h 04 min 14 h 11 min 10 h 07 min
Rome ($41.9^\circ$) 12 h 03 min 15 h 14 min 9 h 07 min
London ($51.5^\circ$) 12 h 03 min 16 h 38 min 7 h 50 min

Near the poles the Sun stops setting. Its lowest point stays above $h_0$ when $\varphi > 90^\circ - \delta_\odot + h_0$, so with $\delta_\odot$ up to $\varepsilon = 23.44^\circ$ there is a midnight Sun somewhere in the year north of $65.73^\circ$, and a polar night north of $67.40^\circ$. At Tromsø ($69.65^\circ$) the model gives 66 days without sunset and 50 without sunrise in 2026.

3 · FitThe year, the epoch and the longitude

The clock has three parameters: the daily motion $n$ of the mean Sun, which sets the rate $360^\circ + n$; the constant $L_0$, which ties the sidereal clock to the solar clock at one moment; and the observer's longitude $\ell$.

The rate, from the length of the year

The daily motion is $n = 360^\circ / Y$, so the rate follows from the length of the year, measured between equinoxes. Hipparchus and Ptolemy found $Y = 365;14,48$ days (365.24667 days, Almagest III 1), 6.4 minutes longer than the modern 365.24219 days.

From it Ptolemy derives the Sun's mean motion, $0^\circ 59' 08.29''$ (0.985635°) a day, the "$0^\circ 59'$" of section 1. With his year the sidereal day comes out as 23 h 56 min 04.093 s, only 2.9 ms longer than the modern value. The rate is insensitive to the year: from $P_\text{sid} = 24\,\text{h} \times Y/(Y+1)$, an error $\Delta Y$ changes the sidereal day by $\Delta Y \times 24\,\text{h}/(Y+1)^2$.

The same error matters for the Sun's position. Ptolemy's mean Sun falls behind the modern one by $0.44^\circ$ a century. Pages 2.1 and 2.3 return to this.

The constant, from the Sun

$L_0$ is the mean Sun's right ascension at the epoch, which is its mean longitude. It is found from timed equinoxes, as page 1.2 found the vernal point, and page 2.1 shows how. Section 2 gave the value fixed by convention, $L_0 = 280.46061837^\circ$.

The longitude, from a timed transit

With the clock fitted, one timed transit gives the observer's longitude: at the transit $\theta = \alpha$, so $\ell = \alpha - \theta_G$. Vega (right ascension $279.4601^\circ$ in 2026) crossed the meridian of Rome on 15 August 2026 at 20:10:52 UT, timed to the second.

The formula gives $\theta_G = 266.9626^\circ$ at that moment, so $\ell = 12.4975^\circ$, against the true 12.4964°. One second of time is 15″ of longitude, 345 m on the parallel of Rome, so the error of the timing sets the error of the result.

4 · TestThe clock against the sky

The stars

The reference sky computes the sidereal time with the full Meeus formula and adds nutation (page 1.2), which shifts the equinox back and forth. Through 2026 the two sidereal times differ by at most 0.66 s, so the model's transits of the stars are right to within a second.

The Sun

Figure 6 compares the model Sun with the reference Sun through 2026. The reference Sun's noon is its real meridian crossing; its sunrise and sunset use the same $h_0 = -50'$ as the model, so the comparison measures only the motion of the Sun.

The model's noon is wrong by up to a quarter of an hour: the real Sun crosses the meridian 14.2 minutes after mean noon on 11 February, and 16.5 minutes before it on 3 November. This difference does not depend on the latitude.

The length of the day is closer. At Rome the model's day is too short by up to 5.3 minutes, at London by 7.5, at $65^\circ$ by 13.1. In the northern hemisphere it is almost never too long (by 0.6 minutes at most, at $65^\circ$); in the southern hemisphere the sign is reversed.

The reason is the uniform Sun. On 5 April 2026 the real Sun is at longitude $15.72^\circ$, $1.91^\circ$ ahead of the mean Sun. Its declination is $6.19^\circ$ instead of the model's $5.45^\circ$, and in London its day lasts 13 h 13 min 24 s instead of 13 h 05 min 57 s.

5 · ResidualWhat the clock leaves out

The Sun does not move uniformly. Both errors of Figure 6 come from the model Sun. Its longitude grows by the same angle every day, while the real Sun runs up to about $2^\circ$ ahead of it in spring and behind it in autumn. Page 2.1 measures this inequality and builds the first model of it.

Noon has a second cause. Even a Sun moving uniformly along the ecliptic would not move uniformly in right ascension, because the ecliptic is tilted to the equator (page 1.2's table showed $\alpha$ lagging behind $\lambda$). Page 2.2 adds the two effects into the equation of time.

The Earth's rotation is not a perfect clock. UT follows the Earth's rotation; the uniform time of the ephemeris (Terrestrial Time, TT) is kept by atomic clocks. The two differ by $\Delta T = \text{TT} - \text{UT}$, about 69 s today, because tides slowly lengthen the day.

The atomic second was chosen to match the length of the day around 1820, so $\Delta T$ is the sum, day after day since then, of each day's difference from 86 400 s. The friction of the tides raised by the Moon lengthens the day by about 1.75 ms a century.

In AD 132 a day was therefore only 29.6 ms shorter than 86 400 s: the Earth turned at almost exactly today's rate. But between 132 and 1820 the shortfall, 14.8 ms a day on average, accumulated over 616 542 days, to 9 118 s, about 2 h 32 min.

Both the daily shortfall and the number of days grow with the time elapsed, so $\Delta T$ grows as its square: about a minute today, hours in antiquity. Our formula, fitted to ancient eclipse records, gives 2 h 35 min for Ptolemy's time.

In 2 h 35 min the sky turns 38.8°, so a time stamp recorded by Ptolemy must be corrected by $\Delta T$ before it can be compared with the reference sky. Page 3.3 needs this for ancient eclipses.

The stars' right ascensions change. Since 2000, Vega's right ascension has grown by 52.8 s of time and Sirius's by 68.7 s, so transit times computed from an old catalogue drift by two to three seconds a year. Page 2.3 explains why.

Exercises

Show that a year of $Y$ solar days contains $Y + 1$ sidereal days, and find the sidereal day for $Y = 365.24219$ days. Repeat with Ptolemy's daily motion of the Sun, $0^\circ 59' 08.29''$ (0.985635°). Then apply the same argument to the Moon, which goes round the sky relative to the Sun in a synodic month of 29.530589 days: how much later does it cross the meridian each day?

Write the hour angle of the body as $\theta - \alpha$ and compare the rates. For the Moon, compare its day with the Sun's: relative to the Sun it goes once round in a synodic month.

With $H_\odot = \theta - \alpha_\odot$, the Sun's hour angle grows at $\omega - n$. In a year the Sun goes once round the sky, so $H_\odot$ grows by $360^\circ$ fewer than $\theta$: $Y$ solar days are $Y + 1$ turns of the sky, 366.24219 sidereal days. One sidereal day is $24\ \text{h} \times Y/(Y+1)$ = 23 h 56 min 04.091 s.

In Ptolemy's terms a mean solar day is $360^\circ + 0.985635^\circ$ of the sky's turn, so one turn takes $360 / 360.985635$ of a day: 23 h 56 min 04.093 s.

The Moon moves east relative to the Sun by one turn in 29.530589 days, so its hour angle falls behind the Sun's by $360^\circ / 29.530589$ a day. Its day lasts $1/(1 - 1/29.530589) = 1.035050$ days, 24 h 50 min 28 s: it crosses the meridian about 50.5 minutes later each day.

At what time does Sirius cross the meridian of Rome ($\ell = 12.4964^\circ$, $\varphi = 41.9^\circ$) on New Year's Eve, 31 December 2026? Its position of date is $\alpha = 101.5845^\circ$, $\delta = -16.755^\circ$. Give the answer in UT and in Central European Time (UT + 1 h), and the star's altitude. Check in Figure 4, then repeat for your own longitude.

First $\theta_{G,0}$ at 0 h UT, from $d$ = JD − 2451545.0; then the transit formula of section 2.

On 31 December 2026 at 0 h UT, JD = 2461405.5 and $d = 9860.5$, so $\theta_{G,0} = 99.4365^\circ$ ($6^\text{h}37^\text{m}44.75^\text{s}$).

The sky still has to turn $(101.5845^\circ - 12.4964^\circ - 99.4365^\circ) \bmod 360^\circ = 349.6516^\circ$. At $360.9856^\circ$ a day that takes 23.24646 h: the transit is at 23:14:47 UT, which is 00:14:47 CET on 1 January, a quarter of an hour into the new year, and 00:04:46 local mean time.

The altitude is $90^\circ - |41.9^\circ + 16.755^\circ| = 31.3^\circ$. Sirius crosses at local mean midnight on the night of 1 January. On 20 December it crosses Rome's meridian twice within the same UT date.

The model puts the earliest sunset and the latest sunrise of the winter on the shortest day, the solstice: at Rome, 07:36:18 and 16:43:44 CET on 21 December 2026. In the real sky the earliest sunset is on 8 December (16:38:44 CET) and the latest sunrise on 4 January 2027 (07:37:53 CET). Explain the difference with Figure 6.

Near the solstice the length of the day hardly changes. Compare it with the motion of noon in Figure 6.

Sunrise and sunset sit symmetrically about noon. Near the solstice the length of the day changes by only a few seconds a day (9 h 13 min 54 s on 8 December, 9 h 07 min 37 s on 21 December, 9 h 14 min 06 s on 4 January), but the real noon moves later by 29.8 s a day.

Real noon at Rome is at 12:01:53 CET on 8 December, 12:08:04 on 21 December and 12:14:51 on 4 January. Sunset is noon plus half the day. From 8 December the later noon outweighs the shortening of the day, so sunset moves later two weeks before the shortest day. Sunrise is noon minus half the day, and for the same reason it keeps moving later until two weeks after it.

The model keeps noon at 12:00 local mean time (12:10:01 CET), so its sunrise and sunset are both extreme on the solstice. The moving noon is the equation of time of page 2.2.
