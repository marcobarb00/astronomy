Every prediction on this site starts from one picture, the sky as a sphere turning about an axis. With this picture alone we can already say, for any star and any place on Earth, whether the star rises, how high it climbs and how long it stays up.

1 · ObservationCircles about one point

If you watch the sky for a night, you'll see every star rise in the east, climb to its highest point due south (for a northern observer) and set in the west. The paths are circles, all parallel to each other, and the stars keep their distances from one another as they turn.

Ptolemy describes the same observation: "the revolution of the ever-visible stars, which was observed to be circular, and always taking place about one centre, the same [for all]" (Almagest I 3). That centre is the celestial pole. Today Polaris lies 0.63° from it, close enough to mark the pole for the naked eye, and this is why it is called the pole star.

A star comes back to the same place after 23 h 56 min 4 s of clock time, about 4 minutes short of a day, a difference that page 1.3 explains. The pole stands above the north point of the horizon at an altitude equal to the observer's latitude $\varphi$: 31.2° at Alexandria, 41.9° at Rome, 51.5° in London.

Because the height of the pole depends on the latitude, so does the part of the sky we can see. As Ptolemy puts it, the further north we travel, "the more of the southern stars disappear and the more of the northern stars appear" (Almagest I 4). Canopus, the second brightest star, can be seen from Rhodes but never from Rome.

2 · ModelA turning celestial sphere

Directions, not distances

Seen from anywhere on Earth, the stars keep the same sizes and the same distances from each other, so compared with the sky the Earth is a point. Ptolemy draws the same conclusion from the horizon: "the planes drawn through the observer's lines of sight […] which we call 'horizons', always bisect the whole heavenly sphere" (Almagest I 6).

A star is therefore just a direction, a unit vector $\mathbf s$, and the celestial sphere is the set of all such directions, with no distances on it. Our model is that this whole set turns rigidly, at a constant rate, about one axis.

The turning sky

To describe the turn, we pick a frame fixed to the sky, with $z$ towards the pole P, $x$ towards the point where the equator crosses the meridian above the horizon, and $y$ towards the east. A turn by an angle $\theta$ about the $z$ axis is

$$R_z(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta & 0 \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$

With $\theta > 0$, $R_z(\theta)$ carries the $x$ axis towards the $y$ axis, from the meridian towards the east, which seen from outside the sphere, above P, is an anticlockwise turn.

The sky turns the other way. A star crosses the meridian and then moves west, so seen from above P the sky turns clockwise, although from the ground, facing the pole, the same motion looks anticlockwise. A turn by an angle $H$ to the west is therefore a turn by $-H$ in the sense of $R_z$.

A star at angular distance $\delta$ from the equator crosses the meridian at $(\cos\delta, 0, \sin\delta)$. Let $H$ be the angle the sky has turned since then, the hour angle, which grows by about 15° an hour. Then the star is at

$$\mathbf s(H) = R_z(-H) \begin{pmatrix} \cos\delta \\ 0 \\ \sin\delta \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\delta\cos H \\ -\cos\delta\sin H \\ \sin\delta \end{pmatrix}.$$

The $z$ component never changes, so every star moves on a circle parallel to the equator, as observed. The angle $\delta$ is the star's declination, and page 1.2 adds the second coordinate, which fixes where on the circle the star is.

The observer

The observer has a frame of their own, the horizon frame, with $x$ towards the south point, $y$ east and $z$ towards the zenith.

We point $x$ south because, for a northern observer, the equator crosses the meridian south of the zenith, so the sky frame's $x$ axis points south too. With both $x$ axes on the same side and the east axis shared, the two frames differ by a single tilt. An $x$ axis pointing north would force $y$ to point west, to keep the frame right-handed, and minus signs would appear everywhere. Meeus (ch. 13) also counts the azimuth from the south.

The sky frame is then the horizon frame tilted about the east–west axis, $y$, by a rotation

$$R_y(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & 0 & \sin\theta \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ -\sin\theta & 0 & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}, \qquad R_y(\theta) \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \sin\theta \\ 0 \\ \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}.$$

To find the angle of the tilt, note that it must carry the zenith direction onto the pole P. P stands on the meridian at altitude $\varphi$ in the north, so it lies $90^\circ - \varphi$ from the zenith, on the north side ($-x$). With $\theta > 0$, $R_y(\theta)$ tips $z$ towards $+x$, the south, but we need a tip of $90^\circ - \varphi$ towards the north, so $\theta = -(90^\circ - \varphi) = \varphi - 90^\circ$:

$$\mathbf s_\text{hor} = R_y(\varphi - 90^\circ)\, \mathbf s.$$

Three checks confirm the signs:

  • The pole: $R_y(\varphi - 90^\circ)(0, 0, 1)^T = (-\cos\varphi, 0, \sin\varphi)^T$, north at altitude $\varphi$.
  • The equator's meridian point: $R_y(\varphi - 90^\circ)(1, 0, 0)^T = (\sin\varphi, 0, \cos\varphi)^T$, south at altitude $90^\circ - \varphi$.
  • At the north pole, $\varphi = 90^\circ$: no tilt, and P is at the zenith.

Altitude as a dot product

The altitude $a$ of a star is its angle above the horizon plane. The zenith is perpendicular to that plane, so the angle between the star and the zenith, the zenith distance, is $90^\circ - a$.

For two unit vectors the dot product is the cosine of the angle between them, so $\hat{\mathbf z} \cdot \mathbf s = \cos(90^\circ - a) = \sin a$. In the horizon frame $\hat{\mathbf z} = (0, 0, 1)$, and the dot product just picks out the $z$ component of $\mathbf s_\text{hor}$.

The star is simpler in the sky frame, so we move the zenith there instead. A rotation keeps dot products, $(0,0,1)^T \cdot (R\,\mathbf s) = (R^T (0,0,1)^T) \cdot \mathbf s$, and the vector $R^T(0, 0, 1)^T$ is the third row of $R_y(\varphi - 90^\circ)$, which is the zenith seen from the sky frame, $\hat{\mathbf z} = (\cos\varphi, 0, \sin\varphi)$. So

$$\sin a = \hat{\mathbf z} \cdot \mathbf s(H) = \sin\varphi \sin\delta + \cos\varphi \cos\delta \cos H.$$

Every result on this page, and the altitude read-out of Figure 2, follows from this formula.

A turning Earth gives the same altitudes

Suppose instead that the stars stand still and the Earth turns eastward about the same axis. Then the star $\mathbf s_0$ is fixed and the zenith moves, $\hat{\mathbf z}(H) = R_z(H)\, \hat{\mathbf z}_0$. Because a rotation keeps dot products and $R_z(H)^T = R_z(-H)$,

$$\hat{\mathbf z}(H) \cdot \mathbf s_0 = \big(R_z(H)\, \hat{\mathbf z}_0\big) \cdot \mathbf s_0 = \hat{\mathbf z}_0 \cdot \big(R_z(-H)\, \mathbf s_0\big).$$

The altitude is the same at every instant, so no observation of the sky alone can tell the two pictures apart. Ptolemy grants this, "there is perhaps nothing in the celestial phenomena which would count against that hypothesis", and rejects the turning Earth on physical grounds (Almagest I 7).

Copernicus states the same equivalence but takes the other side: "if any motion is ascribed to the earth, in all things outside it the same motion will appear, but in the opposite direction" (De Revolutionibus I 5). He credits the turning Earth to Heraclides, Ecphantus and Hicetas.

Culminations, rising and setting

A star is highest at $H = 0$, where $\sin a = \cos(\varphi - \delta)$, and lowest at $H = 180^\circ$, where $\sin a = -\cos(\varphi + \delta)$:

$$a_\text{max} = 90^\circ - |\varphi - \delta|, \qquad a_\text{min} = |\varphi + \delta| - 90^\circ.$$

These two altitudes sort the stars into three classes. For a northern observer ($\varphi > 0$) a star never sets when $a_\text{min} > 0$, and never rises when $a_\text{max} < 0$:

$$\text{never sets:}\ \ \delta > 90^\circ - \varphi, \qquad \text{never rises:}\ \ \delta < \varphi - 90^\circ.$$

This is Ptolemy's rule, stated with a circle: the circle "which has the north pole of the equator as its pole, and the elevation of the pole as its radius, is always visible, and all stars within that circle are always visible" (Almagest II 6).

His circle is the small circle centred on the north celestial pole with an angular radius equal to the pole's altitude $\varphi$, so it just touches the horizon at the north point. A star inside it is less than $\varphi$ from the pole, $90^\circ - \delta < \varphi$, which is again $\delta > 90^\circ - \varphi$.

As the sky turns, such a star stays at the same distance from P, so it never reaches the horizon. The mirror image of the circle, around the south pole, holds the stars that never rise, and both circles are drawn, dashed and purple, in Figure 2.

Every other star rises and sets. It sets when $a = 0$, at the hour angle $H_0$ with

$$\cos H_0 = -\tan\varphi \tan\delta,$$

and it is up for $|H| < H_0$, a fraction $f = H_0 / 180^\circ$ of each turn. Since $\arccos(-x) = 180^\circ - \arccos x$, a star and its mirror image across the equator share the turn between them, $f(\delta) + f(-\delta) = 1$.

On the equator itself $\delta = 0$, so $H_0 = 90^\circ$ and $f = \tfrac12$ at every latitude. This is why Ptolemy calls the equator "the only [such parallel circle] which is always bisected by the horizon" (Almagest I 8), and why the Sun on it gives equal day and night.

3 · FitMeasuring the axis and the stars

Our model needs three kinds of number: the rate of the turn, the tilt of the axis, which is the latitude $\varphi$, and each star's declination $\delta$. All of them come from timing or measuring a star on the meridian.

The rate. We time the same star crossing the meridian on two nights. The modern value of the interval is 23 h 56 min 04.09 s of clock time, so the sky turns 15.0411° per hour.

Latitude and declination together. For these we take a star that never sets and measure its altitude at both meridian crossings, counted from the north point (so the upper one may pass 90°). The formulas for $a_\text{max}$ and $a_\text{min}$ then give

$$h_\uparrow = 90^\circ + \varphi - \delta, \quad h_\downarrow = \varphi + \delta - 90^\circ \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \varphi = \frac{h_\uparrow + h_\downarrow}{2}, \quad 90^\circ - \delta = \frac{h_\uparrow - h_\downarrow}{2}.$$

Let's try it on Kochab from Alexandria, where the real sky shows it 47.166° and 15.309° above the north point. The fit gives $\varphi = 31.238^\circ$ and $\delta = 74.071^\circ$, 2.3′ and 1.3′ too large, because the atmosphere raises the low crossing (by 3.6′) more than the high one (by 0.9′), an effect called refraction that section 4 measures.

Ptolemy measured the meridian altitudes of the noon Sun with a bronze ring and a stone quadrant (Almagest I 12). For Alexandria he gives the equator's distance from the zenith, which is the latitude, as 30°58′ (30.97°, Almagest V 12), 14′ below the modern 31°12′.

4 · TestThe model against the sky

We compare the model with the reference sky for one night, in which each star's catalogue position is carried to 2026 for its proper motion and precession and then turned by the Earth's rotation. The model's star is fitted on that same night, so its $\delta$ is right, and what remains is the effect of the atmosphere, which the model leaves out.

Starlight bends as it enters the atmosphere, more the lower it comes in, so a star appears higher than it is. For standard air this refraction is

$$R = \frac{1.02'}{\tan\!\left(a + \dfrac{10.3^\circ}{a + 5.11^\circ}\right)} \qquad (a \text{ in degrees}),$$

This is Sæmundsson's formula (Meeus 16.4), which gives 29′ at the horizon, 5.4′ at 10° and 1.0′ at 45°; a star seen exactly on the horizon is really 34′ below it. Other effects, such as aberration and nutation, shift a star by less than 1′ and by the same amount all night, so the fit absorbs them.

The model's star is at its true altitude $a$, while the sky shows it at $a + R$. The model's error is therefore $\Delta a = a_\text{model} - a_\text{real} = -R$, so Figure 5 shows the refraction itself, followed through one turn of the sky. We plot it as an error, model minus sky, as we'll do for every error on this site.

For a high star the error is small, 1.0′ at an altitude of 45° and 2.7′ at 20°. Near the horizon, though, the model puts the star up to 34′ too low, so real stars rise earlier and set later than the model says. Seen from Rome, Sirius rises 3.3 minutes earlier and sets 3.3 minutes later, so it is up for 10 h 00 min instead of 9 h 53 min.

The effect on rising times is larger for a star that crosses the horizon at a shallow angle. Canopus, seen from Rhodes, climbs only 1.3°, and the same 34′ makes it rise 14 minutes earlier and set 14 minutes later.

This page has no longitude to test, so it has no error curve. The error curve, the model's longitude minus the real one plotted against time, starts on page 2.1, with the first model of the Sun's motion, and from there on every page measures its model with it.

5 · ResidualWhat the sphere leaves out

Refraction is an effect of the atmosphere, and near the horizon it changes with the weather by several arcminutes. The models on this site therefore predict positions as if there were no air, and the pages that time risings and settings add $R$ back.

The declinations we fit do not stay fitted, because the "fixed" stars drift. Polaris is 0.63° from the pole now, will pass closest (28′) around 2102, and was 12.5° away in Hipparchus' time. Page 2.3 finds the cause.

Most important, the Sun, the Moon and the planets move among the stars. They turn with the sphere but do not keep their places on it, a second motion that Ptolemy describes as "towards the east and opposite to [the motion of] those stars which preserve their mutual distances" (Almagest I 8). The rest of this site is about that motion.

To follow a body that moves on the sphere, the declination and the hour angle are not enough. We need two coordinates for every direction, and formulas to convert between coordinate systems, which page 1.2 introduces.

Exercises

Show that, for $\varphi > 0$, a star never sets exactly when $\delta > 90^\circ - \varphi$, and never rises exactly when $\delta < \varphi - 90^\circ$. Then, with Figure 2, sort Dubhe, Capella, Deneb, Vega and Canopus at Rhodes ($36^\circ$), Rome ($41.9^\circ$) and London ($51.5^\circ$).

A star never sets when even its lowest point is above the horizon: use $a_\text{min} = |\varphi + \delta| - 90^\circ$, and say why $\varphi + \delta > 0$ for such a star.

If the star never sets, its lowest point is above the horizon, so $a_\text{min} > 0$, i.e. $|\varphi + \delta| > 90^\circ$. With $\varphi > 0$ and $|\delta| \le 90^\circ$, the case $\varphi + \delta < -90^\circ$ is impossible, so $\varphi + \delta > 90^\circ$, i.e. $\delta > 90^\circ - \varphi$.

Likewise it never rises when $a_\text{max} < 0$: $|\varphi - \delta| > 90^\circ$. Only $\varphi - \delta > 90^\circ$ is possible, so $\delta < \varphi - 90^\circ$.

With the J2000 declinations (Dubhe $61.75^\circ$, Capella $46.00^\circ$, Deneb $45.28^\circ$, Vega $38.78^\circ$, Canopus $-52.70^\circ$):

  • Rhodes: only Dubhe never sets (lowest point $7.75^\circ$); Canopus rises, but climbs only $1.30^\circ$.
  • Rome: only Dubhe never sets ($13.65^\circ$); Capella just dips below ($-2.10^\circ$); Canopus never rises ($a_\text{max} = -4.60^\circ$).
  • London: Dubhe, Capella, Deneb and Vega all never set, Vega only just (its lowest point is $0.28^\circ$ up); Canopus never rises.

Capella never sets north of $44.00^\circ$, Vega north of $51.22^\circ$. Canopus can be seen only south of $37.30^\circ$.

(a) For what fraction of the day is Sirius ($\delta = -16.72^\circ$) above the horizon at Alexandria ($31.2^\circ$), Rome and London? (b) Ptolemy takes Rhodes at $\varphi = 36^\circ$ with the obliquity $\varepsilon = 23^\circ 51' 20''$ ($23.856^\circ$), the Sun's declination at the summer solstice. Find the longest day (Almagest II 2–3). Check both in Figure 4.

$f = H_0 / 180^\circ$ with $\cos H_0 = -\tan\varphi\tan\delta$. For the Sun, the hour angle grows $15^\circ$ per hour of the clock.

(a) $-\tan\varphi\tan\delta$ is $0.1819$ at Alexandria, $0.2695$ at Rome and $0.3776$ in London. So $H_0 = 79.52^\circ$, $74.37^\circ$ and $67.82^\circ$, and $f = 0.442$, $0.413$ and $0.377$. On a clock (one turn takes 23 h 56 min) that is 10 h 34 min, 9 h 53 min and 9 h 01 min.

(b) $-\tan 36^\circ \tan 23^\circ 51' 20'' = -0.3213$, so $H_0 = 108.741^\circ$. The Sun is up for $2H_0 / 15^\circ = 14.4988$ hours: Ptolemy's $14\tfrac12$ hours. The shortest day, at $\delta = -\varepsilon$, is $24 - 14.4988 = 9.5012$ hours, as $f(\delta) + f(-\delta) = 1$ requires.

Ptolemy also runs it backwards (II 3): a longest day of $14\tfrac12$ hours gives $\varphi = 36.01^\circ$.

At an equinox the Sun is on the equator, so the model gives 12 hours of daylight everywhere. Yet on 20 March 2026 at Greenwich ($\varphi = 51.48^\circ$), the real Sun rises at 06:02:53 and sets at 18:13:03 (UT): 12 h 10 min. Where do the extra 10 minutes come from?

Sunrise is when the top edge of the Sun appears; the Sun's radius is 16′. How fast does the Sun climb at sunrise? Differentiate the altitude formula with respect to $H$.

Sunrise and sunset are counted when the Sun's top edge touches the seen horizon. The centre is then $34' + 16' = 50'$ below the true horizon: refraction plus the radius.

How fast does the Sun climb there? Differentiate $\sin a = \sin\varphi \sin\delta + \cos\varphi\cos\delta\cos H$:

$$\cos a \, \frac{da}{dH} = -\cos\varphi \cos\delta \sin H.$$

At sunrise on the equinox $a \approx 0$, $\delta = 0$ and $H = -90^\circ$ (a quarter turn before noon), so $da/dH = \cos\varphi$. The Sun's hour angle grows $15^\circ$ per hour, so it climbs $15^\circ \cos\varphi = 9.34^\circ$ per hour at Greenwich.

Geometrically: the Sun runs along the equator at $15^\circ$ an hour, and the equator cuts the horizon at the east point at an angle $90^\circ - \varphi$. Only the share $\sin(90^\circ - \varphi) = \cos\varphi$ of that motion is upward.

Climbing 50′ ($0.833^\circ$) at $9.34^\circ$ per hour takes about 5.4 min, at each end of the day: about 10.7 min in all.

The script splits the reference Sun's 12 h 10 min 10 s exactly:

  • refraction: +7.3 min;
  • the Sun's radius: +3.4 min;
  • the Sun's own motion that day: −0.5 min. It sits just south of the equator at noon, and its day is not a turn of the stars.

The extra minutes do not come from the turning sky, which the model describes correctly: the model has no atmosphere and treats the Sun as a point.
