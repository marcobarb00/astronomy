On page 1.1 we described a star by its declination $\delta$ and its hour angle $H$, which grows as the sky turns. That is enough for the fixed stars, but the Sun, the Moon and the planets move among them, and to follow them we need two coordinates for every point of the celestial sphere, and a way to convert between the coordinate systems that astronomers use.

1 · ObservationThe three coordinate systems

Take Aldebaran, the red eye of the Bull, seen from Rome on 1 January 2026 at 20:00 local time (19:00 UT). Depending on what we measure it against, we can give its position in three coordinate systems:

Measured against Coordinates Aldebaran
the local horizon (compass and quadrant) azimuth $A$, altitude $a$ $125.01^\circ$, $53.67^\circ$
the celestial equator: a modern catalogue (2026) right ascension $\alpha$, declination $\delta$ $69.354^\circ$ ($4^\text{h}37^\text{m}$), $16.559^\circ$
the ecliptic, the Sun's yearly path (2026) ecliptic longitude $\lambda$, latitude $\beta$ $70.153^\circ$, $-5.465^\circ$
the ecliptic: Ptolemy's catalogue (AD 137) ecliptic longitude $\lambda$, latitude $\beta$ $42^\circ 40'$ ($42.667^\circ$), $-5^\circ 10'$ ($-5.167^\circ$)

The horizontal coordinates change all the time: an hour later the star is higher and further south. The equatorial ones stay the same through the night and for years, and only over centuries do they change, together with the ecliptic ones.

Since Ptolemy, Aldebaran's longitude has grown by about 26° and its declination has changed by almost 6°, but its latitude has moved by only 8′. Ptolemy had noticed that the stars shift parallel to the ecliptic, so that "their positions in latitude with respect to the ecliptic must necessarily remain the same" (Almagest VII 4), and this is why he gave his catalogue in ecliptic coordinates.

2 · ModelCoordinate systems and rotations

What a frame is

To set up coordinates on the celestial sphere, a frame, we make three choices: a great circle (the fundamental circle), one of its two poles, and an origin on the circle. The longitude is then counted along the circle from the origin, and the latitude from the circle towards the pole.

In vectors, we put $x$ towards the origin, $z$ towards the pole and $y$ at longitude $90^\circ$, so that the frame is right-handed. A direction at longitude $l$ and latitude $b$ is then

$$\mathbf s = \begin{pmatrix} \cos b \cos l \\ \cos b \sin l \\ \sin b \end{pmatrix},$$

exactly as for the sky frame of page 1.1. The three coordinate systems of section 1 are frames of this kind, which differ only in these choices:

Frame Fundamental circle Pole Origin Coordinates
equatorial the celestial equator P, north celestial pole γ, the vernal point $\alpha$, $\delta$
ecliptic the ecliptic, the Sun's yearly path K, north ecliptic pole γ, the vernal point $\lambda$, $\beta$
horizon the horizon Z, the zenith the north point $A$, $a$

The equator and the ecliptic cross at two opposite points. One of them, the vernal point γ (also written ♈︎, the sign of Aries), is where the Sun crosses the equator going north at the March equinox, and both frames take it as their origin.

Right ascension is often given in hours, with $24^\text{h} = 360^\circ$ and so $1^\text{h} = 15^\circ$, which makes Aldebaran's $69.354^\circ$ equal to $4^\text{h}37^\text{m}$. Hours are used because the sky turns about $15^\circ$ an hour, so the right ascension in hours is roughly the time between the meridian crossing of γ and that of the star.

Azimuth

The azimuth $A$ is a compass bearing, $0^\circ$ at north, $90^\circ$ at east and $180^\circ$ at south. Seen from the zenith it runs clockwise, the opposite way to $\alpha$ and $\lambda$, which run anticlockwise seen from their poles.

So the azimuth is not simply the longitude in the horizon frame of page 1.1 ($x$ south, $y$ east, $z$ zenith). There north is $-x$ and east is $+y$, so

$$\mathbf s_\text{hor} = \begin{pmatrix} -\cos a \cos A \\ \cos a \sin A \\ \sin a \end{pmatrix}.$$

As a check, $A = 0$ gives $(-\cos a, 0, \sin a)$, to the north, and $A = 90^\circ$ gives $(0, \cos a, \sin a)$, to the east. Meeus (ch. 13) counts the azimuth from the south instead, so his value is $A - 180^\circ$.

Changing frames is a rotation

To convert a direction from a frame $F$ to a frame $G$, we need its components along $G$'s axes. These are its dot products with the axes, $s_G^i = \mathbf g_i \cdot \mathbf s$, so if we write $G$'s axes in $F$'s components as the rows of a matrix $M$,

$$\mathbf s_G = M \, \mathbf s_F, \qquad M = \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf g_1^T \\ \mathbf g_2^T \\ \mathbf g_3^T \end{pmatrix}.$$

The rows of $M$ are orthonormal, so $M^T M = I$ and the inverse transformation is the transpose, $\mathbf s_F = M^T \mathbf s_G$. Since both frames are right-handed, $\det M = 1$, and $M$ is a rotation.

A rotation preserves dot products, and therefore angles, so the angular distance between two stars is the same in every frame.

Ecliptic and equator

The ecliptic is the path of the Sun among the stars over a year. Day after day the Sun moves eastward along it, on average $360^\circ / 365.2422 = 0.9856^\circ$ a day, and returns to γ after a year. This yearly motion is separate from the daily rotation of page 1.1, which carries the Sun and the stars together and changes none of their coordinates $\alpha, \delta, \lambda, \beta$. Figure 2 shows the yearly motion alone.

Since the two circles cross at γ, the two frames share their $x$ axis. The ecliptic's $y$ axis points to $\lambda = 90^\circ$, the summer solstice, which lies an angle $\varepsilon$ north of the equator at $\alpha = 90^\circ$. This angle is the obliquity of the ecliptic. In equatorial components the three axes are

$$\mathbf x = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf y_\text{ecl} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ \cos\varepsilon \\ \sin\varepsilon \end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf K = \mathbf x \times \mathbf y_\text{ecl} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ -\sin\varepsilon \\ \cos\varepsilon \end{pmatrix}.$$

The pole K is therefore at $\alpha = 270^\circ$, $\delta = 90^\circ - \varepsilon$. The matrix whose columns are these three axes takes ecliptic components to equatorial ones, and it is a rotation by $\varepsilon$ about the $x$ axis:

$$\mathbf s_\text{eq} = R_x(\varepsilon)\, \mathbf s_\text{ecl}, \qquad R_x(\varepsilon) = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \cos\varepsilon & -\sin\varepsilon \\ 0 & \sin\varepsilon & \cos\varepsilon \end{pmatrix}.$$

Multiplying out, with $\mathbf s_\text{eq}$ written in terms of $(\alpha, \delta)$ and $\mathbf s_\text{ecl}$ in terms of $(\lambda, \beta)$, we get

$$\begin{aligned} \cos\delta \cos\alpha &= \cos\beta \cos\lambda, \\ \cos\delta \sin\alpha &= \cos\varepsilon \cos\beta \sin\lambda - \sin\varepsilon \sin\beta, \\ \sin\delta &= \sin\varepsilon \cos\beta \sin\lambda + \cos\varepsilon \sin\beta. \end{aligned}$$

The last line gives $\delta$ directly. The first two give $\cos\delta$ times the cosine and the sine of $\alpha$, so $\operatorname{atan2}$ gives $\alpha$ in the right quadrant, which dividing them into $\tan\alpha$ would lose.

For the Sun, whose latitude is $\beta = 0$, they reduce to

$$\sin\delta = \sin\varepsilon \sin\lambda, \qquad \tan\alpha = \cos\varepsilon \tan\lambda.$$

Equator and horizon

To place a star in the observer's sky, we connect the equatorial frame to the sky frame of page 1.1. The two share the equator and the pole, and differ only in where $x$ points: in the sky frame at the meridian, in the equatorial frame at γ, a point of the equator that turns with the sky.

Let $\theta$ be the angle the sky has turned since γ crossed the meridian. The meridian is then at $\alpha = \theta$, and a star of right ascension $\alpha$ crossed it an angle $\theta - \alpha$ ago, so

$$H = \theta - \alpha, \qquad \mathbf s_\text{sky} = R_z(-\theta)\, \mathbf s_\text{eq}.$$

As a check, the star's longitude in the sky frame is $\alpha - \theta = -H$, as on page 1.1. On this page $\theta$ is a free parameter, and page 1.3 reads it from a clock and calls it the local sidereal time.

On page 1.1 we then tilted the sky frame onto the horizon frame with $R_y(\varphi - 90^\circ)$, and the two rotations together give

$$\mathbf s_\text{hor} = R_y(\varphi - 90^\circ)\, R_z(-\theta)\, \mathbf s_\text{eq}.$$

With $R_y(\varphi - 90^\circ)$ written out (its entries are $\pm\sin\varphi$, $\pm\cos\varphi$) and $\mathbf s_\text{sky} = (\cos\delta\cos H, -\cos\delta\sin H, \sin\delta)$, the three rows give

$$\begin{aligned} \sin a &= \sin\varphi \sin\delta + \cos\varphi \cos\delta \cos H, \\ \cos a \sin A &= -\cos\delta \sin H, \\ \cos a \cos A &= \cos\varphi \sin\delta - \sin\varphi \cos\delta \cos H. \end{aligned}$$

The first line is the altitude formula of page 1.1. The second confirms the signs, since a star east of the meridian ($H < 0$) has $\sin A > 0$, an eastern azimuth. The inverse transformation, from horizon to equatorial, is the transpose.

Spherical trigonometry from the matrices

The same formulas can be read as spherical trigonometry. The pole P, the zenith Z and the star S make a spherical triangle, with sides $90^\circ - \varphi$ (from P to Z), $90^\circ - \delta$ and $90^\circ - a$, and with the hour angle $H$ as its angle at P.

The three rows above are the three classical rules for that triangle: the cosine rule for the side ZS, the sine rule, and the "five-part" rule. Every formula of spherical trigonometry used on this site comes, in the same way, from multiplying out a rotation.

Ptolemy had neither vectors nor sines. He got the same numbers from Menelaus' theorem on great-circle arcs and a table of chords (Almagest I 13–14), and printed the results as tables (I 15–16), which we test in section 4.

Where the coordinates break

At a pole of a frame the longitude is undefined, since P has no right ascension, Z has no azimuth, and $\operatorname{atan2}(0, 0)$ has no value. Near a pole, a small change in the star's position produces a large change in its longitude. The unit vector has no such singularity, which is why the code of this site works with vectors and converts them to angles only at the end.

3 · FitThe obliquity and the origin

The horizon frame needs the latitude $\varphi$, which we measured on page 1.1, and the angle $\theta$, which page 1.3 reads from a clock. The equatorial and ecliptic frames need two quantities from the sky, the angle $\varepsilon$ between them and their shared origin γ.

The obliquity from the noon Sun at the solstices

At noon the Sun is on the meridian, at altitude $90^\circ - \varphi + \delta$. Over the year $\delta$ swings between $+\varepsilon$ and $-\varepsilon$, so the noon altitudes at the two solstices give both $\varepsilon$ and $\varphi$, just as Kochab's two meridian crossings did on page 1.1:

$$\varepsilon = \frac{h_\text{summer} - h_\text{winter}}{2}, \qquad \varphi = 90^\circ - \frac{h_\text{summer} + h_\text{winter}}{2}.$$

At Alexandria in 2026 the real noon Sun stands 82.240° high on 21 June and 35.388° on 21 December. These give $\varepsilon = 23.426^\circ$, only 0.6′ below the true 23.436°, and $\varphi = 31.186^\circ$. Most of the error comes from refraction, which raises the low winter Sun by 1.4′ and the summer Sun by only 0.1′.

Ptolemy measured the same altitudes with his bronze ring and stone quadrant, the instruments of page 1.1. He found the arc between the solstices "always greater than 47⅔° and less than 47¾°" (Almagest I 12), and adopted $2\varepsilon = 47^\circ 42' 40''$, eleven parts of a circle of 83, so that $\varepsilon = 23^\circ 51' 20''$ (23.856°).

He says this agrees with Eratosthenes and Hipparchus, but it is uncertain whether the ratio 11 : 83 was theirs or Ptolemy's rounding of an older 24° (Toomer's note to I 12).

The true obliquity in AD 137 was $23^\circ 40' 40''$ (23.678°), so Ptolemy's value is 10.7′ too large, and the true value lies outside even his bounds of 23.833° to 23.875°. Section 4 follows this error into his tables of the Sun.

The origin

No star marks γ, which is defined by the Sun as the point where it crosses the equator going north. To locate γ we therefore time the March equinox, which in 2026 falls on the afternoon of 20 March (UT). Hipparchus collected such equinox times, some of them read off a bronze ring set in the plane of the equator at Alexandria (Almagest III 1).

Because γ is defined by the Sun, it can move relative to the stars, and then the coordinates of every star change with it, as section 5 shows. This is why a catalogue gives positions for its own date.

4 · TestThe model against the sky

The conversion formulas involve no approximation, so what we can test is the obliquity put into them, first Ptolemy's and then today's.

Ptolemy's tables

Ptolemy needed the Sun's declination for every point of the ecliptic, because it gives the noon altitude of the Sun, the length of the day and the shadow of a gnomon on any date (Almagest II). He computed it once and printed it as the table of inclination (I 15), which gives the declination for each degree of longitude from 1° to 90°. A second table (I 16) gives the right ascension every 10°.

He worked with chords and with Menelaus' theorem, a relation between the arcs of great circles that cross each other (I 13–14), so his tables are an independent computation of the formulas we derived with matrices, made with his obliquity $\varepsilon = 23^\circ 51' 20''$.

Our formulas, computed with the same $\varepsilon$, reproduce his 90 declinations, half of them within 1″ and nine in ten within 3″. The largest difference, 9″ at 27°, is in an entry that Toomer suspects was miscopied (57″ written for 47″). His right ascensions agree within 0.35′, as expected since he rounds them to the minute. So any error in his tables comes from the obliquity he put into them.

His declinations are too large, by an amount that grows from 1.6′ at $\lambda = 10^\circ$ to 10.7′ at the solstice, which is what the error in his obliquity produces. From $\sin\delta = \sin\varepsilon\sin\lambda$, an error $\Delta\varepsilon$ changes $\delta$ by $(\cos\varepsilon\sin\lambda / \cos\delta)\,\Delta\varepsilon$, which is the whole $\Delta\varepsilon$ = 10.7′ at $\lambda = 90^\circ$.

His right ascensions are off by at most 2.4′, because $\alpha$ depends only weakly on $\varepsilon$. The table also shows $\alpha$ lagging behind $\lambda$ in this quadrant, by about 2.5° at $\lambda = 40^\circ$ to $50^\circ$, a lag that page 2.2 identifies as one of the two causes of the equation of time.

The right obliquity and nutation

With the right obliquity, the fixed frames should reproduce the real Sun except for one effect we have left out. This is nutation, a small oscillation of the Earth's axis with a period of 18.6 years, caused mainly by the Moon, which changes the obliquity by a few arcseconds, $\varepsilon_\text{true} = \varepsilon_\text{mean} + \Delta\varepsilon$.

To isolate it, Figure 4 takes the reference Sun's ecliptic coordinates for each day of 2026 and converts them with the mean obliquity of date, as our model does. It then compares the result with the reference Sun's own $(\alpha, \delta)$, computed with the true obliquity. Since both sides start from the same $\lambda$ and $\beta$, the difference is due to nutation alone.

The shape of the curve follows from the formula we used for Ptolemy's tables. A change $\Delta\varepsilon$ changes the declination by $(\cos\varepsilon\sin\lambda / \cos\delta)\, \Delta\varepsilon$, and the model leaves $\Delta\varepsilon$ out, so $\Delta\delta = -(\cos\varepsilon\sin\lambda / \cos\delta)\, \Delta\varepsilon$. At the June solstice $\Delta\delta$ and $-\Delta\varepsilon$ both equal $-7.66''$.

In 2026 $\Delta\varepsilon$ lies between 6.7″ and 9.1″, so the declination is off by at most 7.9″ and the right ascension by at most 1.9″. The models on the following pages err by arcminutes or more, so from now on we neglect nutation and use the mean obliquity.

5 · ResidualWhat the fixed frames leave out

Our frames are fixed, but the angle between them changes. The obliquity was 23.678° in Ptolemy's time and is 23.436° now, 14.5′ less, and it is falling by 47″ a century. Even the correct obliquity of AD 137, used today, would miss the Sun's declination by 14.5′ at the solstices.

Copernicus, comparing his measurements with Ptolemy's, concluded "that the obliquity of the ecliptic also is variable" (De Revolutionibus II 2 [f. 29r]).

The origin moves too. Aldebaran's longitude has grown by about 26° since Ptolemy while its latitude stayed within 8′, because the vernal point itself moves backwards along the ecliptic. Ptolemy's own longitude for the star is also 71′ short of the real one in his time. Page 2.3 takes up both problems.

Finally, we still have no way to know $\theta$. In Figure 3 we set it with a slider, but in the sky it depends on the time, since it is the angle the sky has turned since γ last crossed the meridian. To place a star in the observer's sky at a given moment we need $\theta$ at that moment, and page 1.3 shows how to compute it.

Exercises

Show that the transformation from the equatorial frame to the ecliptic one is $R_x(-\varepsilon)$, and write out $\lambda$ and $\beta$ in terms of $\alpha$ and $\delta$. Then find Aldebaran's $\lambda$ and $\beta$ from $\alpha = 69.3539^\circ$, $\delta = 16.5594^\circ$ (2026.0), with $\varepsilon = 23.4359^\circ$. Check in Figure 2.

A rotation's inverse is its transpose. What is the transpose of $R_x(\varepsilon)$?

$R_x(\varepsilon)^T = R_x(-\varepsilon)$: transposing swaps the two $\sin\varepsilon$ entries, which is the same as changing the sign of $\varepsilon$. So $\mathbf s_\text{ecl} = R_x(-\varepsilon)\,\mathbf s_\text{eq}$, and multiplying out:

$$\begin{aligned} \cos\beta \cos\lambda &= \cos\delta \cos\alpha, \\ \cos\beta \sin\lambda &= \cos\varepsilon \cos\delta \sin\alpha + \sin\varepsilon \sin\delta, \\ \sin\beta &= \cos\varepsilon \sin\delta - \sin\varepsilon \cos\delta \sin\alpha. \end{aligned}$$

These are the formulas of section 2 with $\varepsilon \to -\varepsilon$ and the roles of the two frames swapped.

For Aldebaran, $\mathbf s_\text{eq} = (0.3380, 0.8970, 0.2850)$ and $\mathbf s_\text{ecl} = (0.3380, 0.9363, -0.0952)$. So $\sin\beta = -0.09525$, $\beta = -5.465^\circ$, and $\lambda = \operatorname{atan2}(0.93632, 0.33797) = 70.153^\circ$.

On 21 May 2026 the Sun reaches $\lambda = 60^\circ$ ($\beta = 0$). Find its declination and right ascension with $\varepsilon = 23.4359^\circ$. Check them in Figure 2 on 21 May. Then repeat with Ptolemy's $23^\circ 51' 20''$ and check the result against his values in the table of section 4 (row $60^\circ$).

With $\beta = 0$: $\sin\delta = \sin\varepsilon\sin\lambda$ and $\tan\alpha = \cos\varepsilon\tan\lambda$, with $\alpha$ in the same quadrant as $\lambda$.

$\sin\delta = \sin 23.4359^\circ \sin 60^\circ = 0.34444$, so $\delta = 20.147^\circ$. And $\tan\alpha = \cos 23.4359^\circ \tan 60^\circ = 1.58917$, so $\alpha = 57.819^\circ$, or $3^\text{h} 51^\text{m}$. The right ascension lags the longitude by 2.181°.

The reference Sun on that day differs by 5.0″ in $\delta$ and 1.2″ in $\alpha$: its latitude is $-1.7''$, and nutation shifts it slightly.

With Ptolemy's obliquity, $\delta = 20^\circ 30' 09''$ (20.503°), exactly his table I 15. And $\alpha = 57^\circ 44' 11''$ (57.736°), against his $57^\circ 44'$ in I 16.

As the sky turns, the ecliptic meets the horizon at an angle $I$ that changes. At Rome ($\varphi = 41.9^\circ$), predict the smallest and the largest $I$, and which point of the ecliptic is rising at each. Check with Figure 3 and its read-out. Then explain why a thin crescent Moon after sunset is easier to see in March than in September.

The angle between two planes is the angle between their poles, so $I = 90^\circ - a_\text{K}$, where $a_\text{K}$ is the altitude of the ecliptic pole. K is a star of declination $90^\circ - \varepsilon$: use page 1.1's highest and lowest altitudes.

K has $\delta = 90^\circ - \varepsilon$ and $\alpha = 270^\circ$. Its altitude swings between $a_\text{max} = 90^\circ - |\varphi - \delta|$ and $a_\text{min} = |\varphi + \delta| - 90^\circ$, so $I$ swings between

$$I_\text{min} = 90^\circ - \varphi - \varepsilon = 24.66^\circ, \qquad I_\text{max} = 90^\circ - \varphi + \varepsilon = 71.54^\circ.$$

The smallest comes when K is on the upper meridian, $\theta = 270^\circ$. The equator's rising point is then at $\alpha = \theta + 90^\circ = 0^\circ$: γ is rising. The largest comes at $\theta = 90^\circ$, with the autumnal point rising.

At sunset at the March equinox (17:22 UT at Rome) the Sun sets at γ, so the opposite point rises: $\theta = 91.3^\circ$ and $I = 71.5^\circ$. At the September equinox (sunset 17:06 UT) it is the other way round: $\theta = 271.5^\circ$, $I = 24.7^\circ$.

The young Moon is never far from the ecliptic, a few tens of degrees east of the Sun. On a March evening the ecliptic stands steeply over the western horizon, so the crescent is high in a dark sky. In September the ecliptic lies low, and the crescent sets in the twilight.
